# Political alignment ("compass") test: how it works

The compass data is in [`data/guide/compass.json`](../data/guide/compass.json) (the 36 items, axes and scoring rules) and [`data/guide/compass-parties.json`](../data/guide/compass-parties.json) (where the parties sit, from published data and from our own coding). Both are built by `tools/compass_build.py` from `tools/compass_items.py`, `tools/compass_party_coding.py` and `tools/compass_published.py`; the downloaded source material is in `raw/compass/` and the small extracts there are regenerated by `tools/compass_extract.py`.

Unlike the ["Find your match" quiz](quiz-method.md), which asks about concrete 2026 New Zealand dividing lines, the compass asks about **general political and social values**: how far the state should go in the economy, how much weight to put on order and tradition against personal freedom, and how far you trust politicians at all. It then shows where you sit on two standard scholarly axes (plus a secondary populism axis) and where the New Zealand parties sit on the same map.

It is a description of your values, not a voting recommendation. A two-dimensional map flattens politics (see section 7) and the parties' points are estimates with real uncertainty.

## 1. Where the questions come from, and why we may use them

Every item is taken verbatim (or with a minimal, recorded adaptation) from an instrument that is either openly licensed or a standard public survey questionnaire. We did **not** use the statement sets of politicalcompass.org, 8values, 9Axes, SapplyValues or similar sites: those are proprietary, unvalidated, and their items mix several dimensions.

What we **did not** use, and why:

- *Akkerman, Mudde & Zaslove (2014)* six-item populism scale: published in *Comparative Political Studies* (Sage, not open access). The CSES Module 5 battery was designed by the same research community, covers the same three components (people-centrism, anti-elitism, popular sovereignty) and is public, so we used it instead. The same applies to the Castanho Silva et al. (2020) short scale.
- *ISSP Role of Government (2016)* "should it be the government's responsibility to..." items: the official source questionnaire sits behind GESIS registration and we could not obtain a verified copy, so we did not reproduce the wording from memory.
- *NZES Treaty / biculturalism items* (C9, C11, C12d, C12i): these would form a fourth, New Zealand-specific axis. We left them out of this general test because the quiz already covers Te Tiriti issues concretely; they could be added later as a clearly separated optional axis.

### Adaptations (all recorded per item in `compass.json`)

- SECS items are feeling-thermometer words ("How positive or negative do you feel about: Abortion"). Everett used 0-100; we use a 5-point very negative to very positive scale mapped to -2..+2, and report an approximate 0-100 subscale score as 50 + 12.5 x mean (see scoring). The SECS item "Gun ownership" is American in flavour, as Everett acknowledges; we keep it so the economic subscale is complete and flag it in the item note.
- WVS Q106-Q109 are 1-10 bipolar scales between two statements. We present one pole as a 5-point agree/disagree statement and record the opposing pole in the item note.
- CSES "[COUNTRY]" is replaced by "New Zealand".
- VSA items were fielded on a 9-point scale; we use 5 points. One VSA item is kept with the typographical slip in the published appendix ("if we are going preserve law and order") because the licence condition is faithful reproduction with attribution; the app may silently correct it.

## 2. The axes and their scientific basis

**Economic left-right** (12 items; 6 keyed each way). Left = redistribution, public ownership, government responsibility; right = markets, limited government, fiscal restraint. This is the "lrecon" dimension of the Chapel Hill Expert Survey and the Global Party Survey (Norris 2020, V4), and the first dimension in Hooghe, Marks and Wilson (2002).

**Social: GAL-TAN / libertarian-authoritarian** (16 items; 10 keyed TAN, 6 keyed GAL). GAL = green, alternative, libertarian: personal freedom, pluralism, openness to immigrants and minorities; TAN = traditional, authoritarian, nationalist: order, tradition, national culture (Hooghe, Marks & Wilson 2002; Kitschelt 1994; CHES "galtan"; GPS V6). The VSA supplies the authoritarianism core (Duckitt's submission, conventionalism, aggression), SECS the traditionalism items, and the ESS and CSES items the tolerance and immigration components.

**Populism / anti-establishment** (8 items; 7 keyed populist, 1 reverse). Mudde's (2004) "thin ideology" that society is divided into the pure people and the corrupt elite, and that politics should express the will of the people; measured with the CSES elite battery plus the anti-pluralism item. Because the CSES items are nearly all worded in the populist direction, acquiescence bias cannot be balanced out on this axis, so the app should present it as a **secondary, indicative** axis, not as a third coordinate of equal standing.

### Validity and the limits of two dimensions

- Political attitudes are at least two-dimensional in most electorates, and the two dimensions are only loosely related: economic and social conservatism correlate modestly in mass publics, and many people are "cross-pressured" (Feldman & Johnston 2014; Malka, Lelkes & Soto 2019; Lefkofridi, Wagner & Willmann 2014). A single left-right score would hide this, which is why we show two axes.
- Even two axes flatten real structure: immigration, environment, religion and Te Tiriti do not load cleanly on either axis in New Zealand (Vowles, Coffé & Curtin 2017 on the 2014 NZES; Vowles et al. on 2017 and 2020). Our social axis bundles authority, tradition and openness because that is how GAL-TAN is defined, but a respondent can be libertarian on crime and restrictive on immigration; the per-subscale scores show this.
- Short scales are less reliable than long ones. The VSA's reported alpha is .71 in the authors' data and .54-.66 in replications; the SECS economic subscale is .70. We therefore report an uncertainty band around each point rather than a dot alone.
- The economic items were written for cross-national surveys and for the US (SECS). Wording such as "Gun ownership" or "Limited government" has a different resonance in New Zealand; we kept the items for comparability and flag them.
- The test measures stated values, not vote choice. Nothing in it is a party cue (no party names, no NZ policy specifics), which is intended, but it also means the party points come from a different kind of evidence than the user's point (section 5).

## 3. Scoring

Responses are -2 (strongly disagree / very negative) to +2 (strongly agree / very positive); a skipped item is null.

```
keyed_i   = direction_i x response_i                 (+2 always = right / TAN / populist pole)
axis      = 5 x mean(keyed_i over answered items)     (range -10 .. +10)
```

Minimum items answered to show an axis: economic 6 of 12, social 8 of 16, populism 4 of 8. Below that the app shows "not enough answers" for that axis.

**Uncertainty.** `half_width = max(1.0, 1.96 x 5 x sd(keyed_i) / sqrt(n))`, where `sd` is the sample standard deviation of the keyed scores on that axis and `n` the number answered. The floor of 1.0 says that even a complete, internally consistent set of 5-point answers cannot locate a person more finely than about a unit on a 20-unit axis. The app shows the point with a +/- half-width band and the text "based on n of N items".

**Subscales.** Each item is tagged (SECS economic, SECS social, VSA authoritarianism, CSES populism, CSES out-group, WVS economic values, ESS, NZES). A subscale score is 5 x mean(keyed) over the answered items with that tag, shown when at least half of its items were answered. For SECS we also give Everett's 0-100 metric approximately as `50 + 12.5 x mean(keyed)` (so the 5-point version spans 25-75 of the original thermometer).

**Acquiescence.** Half of the economic items and six of the sixteen social items are reverse-keyed, so a respondent who agrees with everything lands near the centre of those axes rather than at an extreme. The populism battery cannot be balanced (see above).

## 4. Where the parties sit: published data

`compass-parties.json` keeps every published value on its **original scale** with the source's own scale definition, and adds a stated rescaling to -10..+10 (`(x - 5) x 2` for 0-10 scales; `(osp - 3) x 10/3` for V-Party's 0-6 ordinal economic scale; `(index - 0.5) x 20` for V-Party's 0-1 populism index). Nothing is converted silently.

Not available: the **Manifesto Project (MARPOR) RILE** scores need a registered API key we could not use from this environment, and no published NZ 2017-2023 RILE table was found; **Vote Compass NZ** (Vox Pop Labs/1News) shows party coordinates only inside the application and does not publish them; **CHES** does not cover New Zealand. These are recorded in `not_retrieved` with the reason.

The published placements have three limits worth stating plainly. They are **old** (GPS and V-Party describe the parties at the 2017 election; ParlGov's latest expert input is 2003-04). They **cover few parties** (five for GPS; none of the 2026 minor parties). And most of them measure a **general** left-right position, which is not the same as our economic axis; we label those values `general_left_right` and never plot them as if they were economic scores.

## 5. Where the parties sit: our own coding ("the party takes the test")

For each of the 17 parties registered for the 2026 election we answered every compass item as the party would, strictly from evidence, in `tools/compass_party_coding.py`:

- **Sources, in order:** (1) the party's 2026 policy documents archived verbatim under `parties/<slug>/policies/documents/` and its policy.nz entries; (2) recorded parliamentary votes (Abortion Legislation Act 2020, Marriage Amendment Act 2013, Conversion Practices Prohibition Act 2022, Sentencing (Reinstating Three Strikes) Act 2024, Treaty Principles Bill 2025, Regulatory Standards Act 2025), attributed to a party only when its whole caucus or a clear majority voted one way; (3) on-record statements by the leader or MPs (speeches republished on Scoop, press releases, news reports).
- **Cells** are `{position -2..+2, evidence, source_url, confidence}`. `position` is relative to the item text; the direction key is applied afterwards. `confidence` is `high` (explicit policy, vote or leader statement on the substance), `medium` (a clear position needing one inferential step, e.g. a surplus rule read as "fiscal responsibility"), or `low` (indirect, dated or mixed signals).
- **Null means no evidence.** We did not infer a position from a party's general philosophy, and two VSA items of private morality ("defy authority", "premarital sex") are null for every party because no party takes a public stance on them. Thermometer items were coded only where a party expresses an attitude to the object itself (e.g. "Religion" is coded for Vision NZ and the Conservatives, not for parties that merely protect religion from hate speech).
- **Mixed records get 0** with the conflict described (for example NZ First on "government should take more responsibility": "a hand up in times of need" against calling a universal basic income "communism").
- Party axis scores use **exactly the same formula** as the user test, over the coded items, with the same minimum-items rule and uncertainty band, so a user's point and a party's point are comparable. The number of coded items and the confidence mix are stored so the app can grey out thin estimates (for example Legalise Cannabis and Free Palestine have too few codable items to be placed at all).

**Member and MP surveys.** The brief asked us to use candidate or MP survey data where it exists. The NZES has run candidate surveys (the Comparative Candidates Survey, 2008-2017), but their party-level value scores are not published in a form we could cite, and no 2023 or 2026 MP values survey was found. The party points are therefore **document-and-vote based** and describe the party's public programme, not the private values of its members. The only member-level evidence used is roll-call behaviour on conscience votes, which is explicitly attributed.

### Our coding against the published data

The `comparison` block for each party gives our axis scores next to the rescaled GPS 2019, ParlGov and V-Party values and the difference (Appendix C). Three patterns stand out and are worth showing users:

- **Ordering agrees, levels are more polarised in our coding.** On both main axes our coding ranks the five GPS-rated parties in the same order as the experts (ACT, National, NZ First, Labour, Green on the economic axis; Vision NZ-style conservatives, NZ First, National, ACT, Labour, Green on the social axis), but our scores sit further from the centre (for example the Greens at about -7.5 economic / -9.5 social against the experts' -4.4 / -6.2). This is a known property of coding manifestos item by item: a party document states positions in clear "strongly agree" terms, while experts average over behaviour in office and coalition compromises. Treat our party points as the party's *programme*, the expert points as its *reputation*.
- On the **social axis** the two sources agree closely for National, NZ First and ACT (ACT slightly liberal on social values despite being hard-line on crime; NZ First clearly conservative).
- On the **economic axis** we place **NZ First near the centre, slightly left**, where the GPS experts put it right of centre. The difference comes from the 2026 record: public ownership of assets, buying back BNZ, breaking up the supermarket duopoly and attacks on "neoliberal" privatisation all score left on our items, while its welfare and tax positions score right. V-Party's 2017 coding (also slightly left of centre) and ParlGov's composite (near centre) are closer to ours than to the GPS. Users should see both.

## 6. Reading the map

- The two main axes are economic (horizontal) and social (vertical); populism is shown as a separate bar.
- Each point has an uncertainty band. A user's band shrinks with more answers; a party's band depends on how many items it could be coded on and how consistent its coded answers are.
- Distance between a user and a party is the Euclidean distance in the economic x social plane, shown only when both have enough items on both axes. Distance is **not** a match percentage and is not comparable with the quiz's match score; the quiz measures agreement on 45 concrete 2026 issues, the compass measures proximity on two abstract value dimensions.
- Where a published placement exists, show it as a hollow marker next to our filled marker, with its source and year, so the disagreement is visible rather than averaged away.

## 7. Caveats

1. **Two dimensions flatten politics.** Environment, Te Tiriti, immigration and foreign policy each have their own structure in New Zealand; a party or voter can be anywhere on them while sharing an economic-social position with someone they disagree with profoundly.
2. **The party answers are coded from documents, not from a survey of members.** They describe what the party says it will do, weighted toward the issues it chooses to talk about. Silence is recorded as null, so parties with thin programmes have wide bands or no point.
3. **Instrument origins.** SECS is American and some items (gun ownership, limited government) carry different connotations here. VSA and the CSES battery are cross-national but short; reliabilities are modest. We cite each item's origin so a user can discount any item.
4. **The populism axis is one-sided** in wording and should be read as indicative.
5. **Published expert data are dated and sparse** (section 4).
6. **Coding judgement.** Someone else coding the same documents might score a few cells one notch differently. Every cell carries its evidence and URL so the coding can be audited and challenged.
