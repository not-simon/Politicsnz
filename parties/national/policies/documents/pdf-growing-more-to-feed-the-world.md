Building the Future:

Growing More to Feed the World
New Zealand’s farmers and growers are the best in the
world. When they do well, the whole country does well.

National is focused on the key drivers that make a
difference to growth:

Food and fibre earns around four in every five dollars
of our goods exports and supports about 360,000
jobs, most of them in our regions. That export income
pays for the hospitals, schools and roads in every
New Zealand community. When farming grows,
it delivers more jobs, wages lift and the regions
across the country thrive.

• More markets, less risk for farmers. We have
concluded free trade agreements with India, the
UAE and the Gulf states, and brought our EU
agreement into force early. Seven in ten of our
exports are now covered by free trade agreements.
National is focused on securing the next phase of
New Zealand’s trade agenda by commencing trade
negotiations with seven new economies in the next
five years.

The Plan is Working
In 2023 National set an ambitious goal to double
the value of New Zealand’s exports in ten years.
For food and fibre, that means growing from $53.3
billion in the year to June 2024 to around $106 billion
by 2034. It needs growth of 7.2 per cent a year, every
year, well above the 4.7 per cent average of the past.
To get this done we embarked on a transformational
work programme. We replaced the RMA, revoked the
agricultural intensification rules, slashed the growthstunting red tape, introduced investment boost and gave
long life consents to critical primary sector infrastructure
to support a lift in capital outlay. Our plan is working.
Food and fibre exports reached a record $65.2 billion
in the year to June 2026, up almost $12 billion, or
22 per cent, in two years. That puts us about $4 billion
ahead of the path to doubling. We are ahead of
schedule, but the job is not done.

No new taxes and no price on
agricultural emissions
National is the party of low tax. We delivered income
tax relief for working New Zealanders for the first time
in 14 years and we have committed to no new taxes if
re-elected.
New Zealand’s farmers are among the most emissionsefficient food producers in the world. It makes no sense
to shut down efficient production here and send it
offshore to countries with a larger emissions footprint.
National’s climate policy is based on practical solutions.
We kept agriculture out of the ETS and invested more
than $400 million with industry to get real emissionsreduction tools into farmers’ hands.
We’ve also reset the methane target to a more realistic,
science-based level. Our focus is lower emissions, not
lower production.
Some believe New Zealand should step back from
international climate commitments altogether. Others
think the answer is more pricing, more regulation
and more cost. We don’t agree with either approach.
There will be no tax on agricultural emissions under
a National government.
We will meet our commitments, keep New Zealand in
Paris, back innovation over taxation, and make sure
farmers remain competitive.

Land use flexibility: producing more
to sell to the world
National knows New Zealand farmers have always
done better when they are empowered to solve
problems, innovate, and make decisions about what
happens behind their own farm gate.
We have worked hard over the last term of government
to axe the rules that were too rigid, systems that were
too slow, and the old approach that the primary sector
is something to constrain, rather than something
to champion.
The future of farming will not be about prescribing one
model from Wellington. It will be about giving farmers and
growers the flexibility to choose the best use of their land
as markets, technology and climate conditions change.
For some, that could mean shifting part of the farm into
a higher-value use. For others, it could mean combining
pastoral, cropping and horticultural systems in smarter
ways, while retaining the strengths of the existing
farm business.
It could mean investing in irrigation and water storage
to strengthen resilience, manage dry periods and make
new land use options commercially viable.
It could mean adopting precision tools, animal wearables,
automation, improved genetics and better data to lift
productivity and farm output.
The goal is not for every farm to look the same. It is for
every landowner to have the confidence, information and
freedom to adapt their business, respond to opportunity
and make the best long-term choices for their land.
Land use flexibility is how New Zealand farms will keep
lifting productivity and secure their future.

At Fieldays 2026, we announced $143 million of land
use flexibility projects across dairy, sheep and beef,
horticulture, forestry, whenua Māori and aquaculture.
These projects test new ideas on real farms, so farmers,
banks and investors can see what works before
they commit.

Water security for growth
Water is one of New Zealand’s greatest strategic
advantages. It underpins our food and fibre production,
supports regional jobs and earns the export income
that pays for hospitals, schools and roads.
We get more rainfall and renewable freshwater per
person than almost any other developed nation. Yet too
many regions still face water shortages. We have the
water. We just don’t store enough of it. New Zealand
captures and uses about two per cent of its available
water resources. We can and should do better.
National believes we should plan for water security the
same way we plan for fuel and energy. That means storing
water when it is plentiful and using it well when it is scarce.
It means knowing where water will be needed and giving
regions a clear path to build the storage they need.

Get schemes built
A re-elected National Government will commit $300
million in concessional loans to get water storage projects
built. The Primary Sector Growth Fund will continue to
provide support for pre-construction studies for productive
water storage projects. Water projects can spend years
moving between hydrology, engineering, environmental
assessment, land access, user commitments, consenting
and finance. National wants water storage projects
approved and built faster. Our promise of concessional
loan support will help bridge the gap.

On-farm energy independence
Farms use a lot of power for milking, cooling,
pumping and irrigation, and lines charges are set
to keep rising through to 2030. Farms are also well
placed to generate their own.
Farmers and growers know what it costs when the lines
go down in a storm, or when global events push up fuel
and fertiliser prices. National trusts farmers to make the
right decisions for their land, and that includes energy.
But they still face costly, confusing rules to develop
small-scale renewables on their own property, making
the process slow and expensive.
For example, Rewiring Aotearoa found Environment
Canterbury has required ground-mounted solar
installations to apply for a water discharge consent,
because rain runs off the panels and could carry
bird droppings. Hurunui District Council, meanwhile,
requires consent for any free-standing installation
larger than 25m², barely enough to power an average
New Zealand home.
It’s not just consenting that’s hard. Energy network
regulations get in the way of farmers making the most
of the energy they produce as well.
National is committed to supporting on-farm energy
independence. Farmers and growers should be able
to harness the resources available on their own land,
easier, faster, and cheaper. If re-elected, we will clear
the way for them to generate, store and sell their own
power. This will cut costs, build resilience and give
farmers and growers another way to earn from their
own land without unreasonable rules getting in the way.

A re-elected National Government will:
1.

Set a national standard for paddock-based
solar. Small-scale ground-mounted solar
on farms will be a permitted activity, with
standard rules to manage any real effects.
Farmers will know what is allowed and won’t
need a resource consent for a standard setup.

2. Make it easier to put in micro-hydro. Small
run-of-river micro-hydro for on-farm use will
be permitted within set limits that protect
rivers, fish and other water users.
3.

Allow small-scale battery storage as of
right, so farmers can make the most of their
investment in solar, micro-hydro and other
renewables.

4.

Let farmers sell their power to the best
buyer. Every farmer will have the right to sell
the power they export to any power company,
not just the one they buy from.

5. Make lines companies base export limits on
what the local network can handle. If they
want to restrict how much a farm exports, they
will have to show there is a genuine constraint.
6. Speed up connections. Farmers will be able
to choose their own accredited contractor for
connection work, cutting costs and delays.
7.

Allow rural developers, major energy
users, and investors to build and own new
transmission and recover their costs from the
parties who use the lines.

8. Let property owners install rooftop solar
without a consent, even where height limits
or landscape overlays would otherwise get in
the way.

Guaranteeing critical nutrients
Fertiliser is a critical nutrient input into New Zealand’s
food and fibre economy. Reliable access supports soil
fertility, farm productivity, export earnings and rural
communities. A serious supply disruption would
be felt well beyond the farm gate through lower
production, higher costs and weaker export returns.
New Zealand uses over two million tonnes of fertiliser
to grow food each year. Our fertiliser production relies
on imported phosphate, which is then processed
on shore. This leaves our primary sector exposed
to international supply shocks. National will reduce
New Zealand’s exposure by supporting domestic
production, strengthening supply relationships,
and attracting investment in strategic infrastructure.
This is about ensuring a continued, stable supply
of fertiliser in the country. We will back measures
to support farmers to responsibly manage their
nutrient use to better support productive farming
and the environment.

Strengthening supply partnerships
New Zealand imports around two-thirds of its
urea, with significant volumes sourced from the
Gulf, particularly Saudi Arabia. Recent conflict and
disruption in the Middle East have shown how quickly
shipping risks can flow through to global fertiliser
prices and availability. Other key nutrients, including
phosphate rock, potash and sulphur, are also exposed
to concentrated production, commodity cycles and
long transport routes.
National will treat fertiliser as a strategic priority
in trade, diplomatic and investment engagement.
Using the New Zealand–Singapore Agreement
on Trade in Essential Supplies as a blueprint,
National will pursue arrangements with key
supplier countries focused on reliable access,
market diversification, early warning and rapid
government-to-government coordination.

A re-elected National Government will:
• Negotiate fertiliser security agreements
with key supplier countries, using the
New Zealand–Singapore Agreement on
Trade in Essential Supplies as a model.
• Establish a $10 million Critical Agri Inputs
Fund to co-invest in fertiliser production,
processing, storage and distribution projects
from the Primary Sector Growth Fund.

More vets for rural New Zealand
National will double the number of graduate vets
placed in rural New Zealand through the Voluntary
Bonding Scheme, from around 35 a year to 70 a year.
Animal health is the foundation of more than
$40 billion a year of dairy, meat and wool exports,
of our biosecurity defences, and of the rural
communities that depend on them. Yet rural practices
are struggling to recruit, too many of our young vets
are heading offshore, and New Zealand relies on
overseas-trained vets for two in every three new
registrations. The bottom line is that our farmers
can’t grow the economy if they can’t get a vet.
National is already strengthening the domestic training
pipeline. From 2027, the number of funded domestic
places in Massey University’s Bachelor of Veterinary
Science will increase from 125 to 140 each year,
allowing an additional 15 New Zealand students to
begin veterinary training annually.
The expansion of the bonding scheme is expected to cost
up to $3.4 million over the four year forecast period rising
to around $2 million a year once fully implemented, and
will be funded from within MPI baselines.

Training more vets matters, but we also need them
working where they are needed most. The Voluntary
Bonding Scheme gives new graduates a financial
incentive to start their careers in rural areas where
shortages are worst. The scheme helps attract and
retain veterinarians in hard-to-staff regions, easing
pressure on existing clinics and improving access
to animal care for farmers.

A re-elected National Government will:
• Double the scheme to up to 70 places a year
from the 2027 funding round, for new graduate
vets committed to five years of rural practice
with production animals and working dogs.
• Open the scheme to New Zealand citizens
and permanent residents who graduate from
accredited Australian vet schools and are
registered to practise here.
• Report on results each year, publishing where
bonded vets work and how many stay, with a
full review of the scheme in three years.

Strong biosecurity at the border and on the farm
Farmers care deeply about biosecurity. It is our
first line of defence against pests and diseases
that could devastate our primary industries,
environment and economy. The arrival of Highly
Pathogenic Avian Influenza, H5N1, shows the value
of preparation and cooperation between industry
and government. Producing more for the world
depends on keeping our biosecurity status.
Growers want faster approvals for new crop protection
tools. The National-led Government opened a pathway
for growers to follow international Codex standards for
agrichemicals where New Zealand limits have not been
set. In September 2026 we passed the Agricultural
Compounds and Veterinary Medicines Amendment
Act, which will speed up approvals for new products.
As travel and trade grow, so does the risk at our border.
National will strengthen our biosecurity laws so our
border protections stay among the strongest in the
world, and those who put our biosecurity at risk face
real consequences.

A re-elected National Government will:

strengthen our ability to prevent, detect and
respond to threats.
• Pass the Hazardous Substances and

horticultural products and veterinary medicines.

discretion to refuse entry or deport certain
temporary visa holders.

Improving Rural Diagnostic Access
National is focused on delivering timely, high-quality
healthcare for rural New Zealand. That means putting
rural communities at the centre of health planning,
shifting the focus towards prevention and earlier
diagnosis, bringing more services closer to home,
supporting people who still need to travel, and building
a stronger rural health workforce.
The message from the frontline is clear: access to
diagnostics remains one of the biggest unmet needs
in rural healthcare. A survey of 192 rural health
professionals identified diagnostic imaging and cardiac
testing as major unmet needs, with travel barriers the
single greatest obstacle to timely care.
For rural families, accessing a routine diagnostic test
can mean hours of travel, time away from work and
additional pressure on those juggling family and caring
responsibilities. These barriers can delay diagnosis and
treatment, adding unnecessary pressure to patients,
families and the wider health system.
A re-elected National Government will build on
progress in rural healthcare by maximising capacity in
our public and private hospitals and continue to roll out

mobile diagnostic scanners to deliver more services for
patients. By making better use of existing resources,
technology and partnerships with healthcare providers,
National will bring more essential diagnostic services
closer to rural communities.
Improving access to diagnostics will help patients
get answers sooner, reduce unnecessary travel and
hospital transfers, support earlier treatment and make
better use of the rural health workforce..

A re-elected National Government will:
• Maximise diagnostic capacity across public and
private hospitals, making better use of existing
equipment, facilities and clinical expertise to
deliver more timely testing for patients.
• Roll out mobile diagnostic scanners, prioritising
rural communities facing the greatest access
barriers and working with local health providers
to bring more services closer to home.
• Measure and report results, including
diagnostic wait times, avoided patient travel
and hospital transfers, service volumes and
improvements in access and patient outcomes.

decade more than 17,000 farm and orchard owners
will reach 65. For a sector that supports 360,000

record $65.2 billion in exports, we need more
young people coming through.

Science curriculum, rescued Apprenticeship Boost

We saved Telford, disestablished the centralised
Wellington bureaucracy of Te Pūkenga and restored
standalone regional polytechnics, so rural and
agricultural training is delivered locally and shaped
by local industry needs.
At Fieldays 2026 we launched the Future Leaders
Scholarship, giving emerging leaders in agriculture,
horticulture and forestry $10,000 towards study,
industry experience and governance skills.
We want to go further.

career in farming.
• Grow Landcorp’s dairy and livestock

• Back the Future Leaders Scholarship to

in the years to come.
