National’s plan for the government’s books
Maintaining a strong set of government books matters
for New Zealanders. It is essential to delivering lasting
prosperity for New Zealand and is one of the most
practical things a government can do for household
budgets.
Spending restraint helps take pressure off inflation and
keep taxes low. When government debt is at moderate
levels, interest rates are lower, the cost of financing this
debt is less, and New Zealand has fiscal headroom when
the next earthquake, severe weather event or global
economic shock hits us.
When National entered government in 2023, we
inherited a very difficult set of books, with ongoing
budget deficits. Over the previous six years, government
spending had gone up 70 per cent, and government
debt had tripled. The interest costs on this debt were
rising fast, and today taxpayers are still paying the
equivalent of four Dunedin Hospitals every year just to
cover them.
National introduced much-needed fiscal discipline. We
did so by carefully prioritising where taxpayers’ money
is spent, stopping spending in low-value areas and
reinvesting it in the frontline services New Zealanders
rely on such as hospitals and schools. In total, we have
reprioritised around $50 billion over three Budgets to
invest in frontline services, rather than increasing taxes
to find the revenue.

Operating and capital allowances
National will continue with $2.4 billion operating
allowances for future Budgets. Operating allowances are
the maximum amount of net new operating funding – on
average, per annum – the government intends to spend
in each Budget, in areas that are under its direct control.
$2.4 billion makes for very tight allowances, but National
has shown in its three Budgets so far that it can manage
within these.
In contrast, new operating spending in the previous
Government’s last two Budgets averaged $7.2 billion.
National will also continue with $3.5 billion capital
allowances for Budgets 2027 and 2028, and $5 billion
capital allowances thereafter. Capital allowances are
the net new one-off funding the Government intends
to allocate to capital projects in each Budget.

Election commitments
The following table shows how National’s election
commitments fit into these operating and capital
allowances.
By far the biggest commitments are in the health system.
Health policies make up 88 per cent of all National’s
spending commitments, reflecting New Zealand’s ageing
population and National’s focus on delivering quality
health care for all.

As a result of our careful economic management,
independent Treasury forecasts show that overall
government spending is coming down as a proportion
of the economy, a return to surplus is expected soon,
and after next year the government debt curve bends
down rather than up.

A total of $7.5 billion operating is left unallocated over
the four years. This is to cover net new operating
spending in that period, over and above the election
commitments specified in the table. In addition, a total
of $14.2 billion remains to be allocated from capital
allowances in future Budgets.

Under a National Government, this fiscal repair will
continue.

•

return to surplus in 2028/29 and stay there, and

•

National does not need to find any new funding for pay
equity. A contingency for the costs of future pay equity

Tax relief
National recognises that the last few years have been
tough for New Zealand households and businesses. In
2024, the Government adjusted personal income tax
thresholds for the first time in 14 years. Alongside other
increases in government assistance, this delivered
an additional $60 a fortnight on average to around
1.9 million households.
But, as is always the case, inflation and wage growth
mean people are paying a slightly higher proportion of
their income in tax every year. The median full-time wage
and salary earner, for example, is now earning $82,000
a year, which puts them in the second-highest, 33 per
cent, income tax bracket.

While National’s fiscal plan shows a pathway to potential
tax relief for working New Zealanders, now is not the
time to cut taxes. The responsible thing to do now is
reduce debt and invest in frontline services, especially
health and infrastructure.
That approach means future generations are not left
paying off debt. It strengthens New Zealand’s resilience
to global shocks and gets the balance right between
economic responsibility and investing in the services
people rely on.
Once the books are back in the black and surpluses are
growing, a National Government will be in a position
to consider tax relief. But the priority is making the
forecasts a reality by delivering a return to surplus and
less debt.
