Building the Future:

Electrify NZ 2.0
Delivering secure, affordable energy is critical to
National’s plan to grow the economy and support
New Zealanders with the cost of living. That’s why
over this term National has undertaken a series of
significant actions designed to enable new renewable
generation, supported by more backup supply, and
pro-competition rules that ensure the market works
for everyone.
As a result, wholesale electricity prices have fallen
significantly, and the country now has the strongest,
most secure pipeline of new renewable generation

in a generation – with new wind farms, solar farms,
geothermal and battery projects. According to the
Electricity Authority, more renewable generation
has now been built in the last three years than the
previous eight.
For households, that means there now exists a credible
path to more affordable electricity prices over time.
For businesses, lower wholesale electricity prices
also mean manufacturers and exporters can remain
internationally competitive, supporting economic
growth, job creation, and higher wages.

So far this term, a National-led Government has:
•

Repealed Labour’s reckless ban on oil and gas exploration: Reopening New Zealand’s oil and gas fields
to bring gas back into the system to firm our renewables, protect manufacturing jobs, and keep power
prices down for households and businesses.

•

The biggest boom in renewables in a generation: New Zealand has been generating up to 96 per cent
of its electricity from renewables, with more new renewable generation built in the past three years than in
the previous eight, and the first ever permits for offshore wind about to open.

•

Putting power in the hands of households: More New Zealanders than ever are generating their own
affordable electricity, with solar connections up 47 per cent since National came to office.

•

Fast-track consenting: One-year consent decisions and 35-year consents mean nationally significant
infrastructure now has a single, time-bound pathway rather than years of litigation, driving new investment.
Eight renewable energy projects have already been approved by Fast-track panels.

•

Restored confidence in energy investment: National scrapped Labour’s Lake Onslow scheme and
unrealistic 100 per cent renewables target, both of which were chilling investment in new generation.

•

Stronger competition: The Energy Competition Task Force was established between the Electricity
Authority and the Commerce Commission, delivering eight initiatives to strengthen competition in the
electricity market.

•

A level playing field: Since 1 July, the four big gentailers have had to supply hedge contracts to
independent retailers and generators on a non-discriminatory basis, rather than favouring their own retail
arms, giving consumers more genuine choice.

•

A strategic coal stockpile: New Zealand now holds strategic fuel reserves at Huntly, so that a dry year is
managed rather than a national crisis that puts jobs and industry at risk.

•

LNG procurement: New Zealand households and businesses are a step closer to relief on their power
bills and greater energy security with National starting a procurement process to deliver an LNG facility
that will keep the lights on and protect jobs.

•

EV charging: National is co-funding a nationwide public charging network so New Zealanders can
travel the length of the country with confidence, using the cheapest transport fuel available to us –
our own electricity.

Authorised by J de Joux, ps@national.org.nz

However, there is more work to do to put downward
pressure on the total cost New Zealanders pay.
While wholesale electricity prices have now fallen
considerably over the last two years, network charges,
the extent of retail competition, and the cost of

keeping the lights on in a dry winter all still determine
the price and security of supply that households and
businesses get, and the jobs that depend on secure,
affordable energy.

That’s why, to deliver secure, affordable energy for more households and businesses, a
re-elected National Government will introduce our nine-point energy plan, Electrify NZ 2.0:
• Unlock new energy development zones by letting developers, major energy users, and investors build
and own new transmission, and recover their costs from the parties who use the lines.
• Make connections faster and cheaper by giving every household, builder, and business the right to
choose an accredited contractor for the connection work they’re paying for, not the one their network
hands them.
• Let Kiwis sell their power to the best buyer, giving every household and business with solar or a battery
the right to sell the power they export to any power company, not just the one they buy from.
• Addressing the dry year problem by levelling the playing field for backup power. National will use
transparency, a standardised backup power contract, and regular auctions to open the closed backup
market and draw in the competition and investment that keeps the lights on through dry periods.
• Tackle lines charges through a targeted review of Part 4 of the Commerce Act and put an end to slow
solar approvals by requiring distribution businesses to sign off residential and small business
connections within two working days.
• Make affordability a core objective for the Electricity Authority by writing it into law, so the cost of
power for households and businesses carries equal weight with competition, reliability, and efficiency in
every decision the regulator makes.
• Cut more red tape holding back renewables at every scale, from national rules letting Kiwis install solar,
micro-hydro, and batteries on their own property without a consent, to implementing the RMA
replacement so wind, solar, and hydro get built fast as standard, not the exception.
• Launch the Home Energy Fund, giving Kiwis low-interest, long-term loans to invest in their own power
and cut their own bills.
• Require AI data centres to bring new, firmed power, so new digital investment adds to the grid and
creates jobs instead of pushing up prices for families and businesses.

Unlocking new energy development
zones
Some of New Zealand’s best wind, solar, and
geothermal resources are in parts of the country where
limited transmission capacity restricts how much new
generation can connect and how much power can reach
major centres of demand. That makes it harder to build
generation where it is needed and stops cheaper power
in one region from reaching consumers in another.
Cost allocation is a further barrier. When new
transmission is treated as a connection asset, the

first party to trigger the investment can be left paying
for infrastructure that will ultimately benefit multiple
generators, electricity users, and regions. This firstmover risk discourages the coordinated investment in
generation and transmission that New Zealand needs.
The Electricity Authority’s transmission pricing
methodology already allocates the costs of
Transpower’s interconnection investments according to
who benefits. The same principle should apply where
a private investor is prepared to finance, build, and
own high-voltage infrastructure that serves a wider
interconnection function.

A re-elected National Government will:

A re-elected National Government will:

•

•

Establish a single accreditation scheme for
connection contractors, with standards set
by the Electricity Authority and assessed
by independent certification bodies rather
than by the networks contractors will be
competing against.

•

Make it so a contractor accredited once will
be able to work on any network in the country.

•

Allow anyone paying for a new connection,
or an upgrade to an existing one, to seek
quotes from accredited providers and choose
the best price and timeline.

•

Require networks, which will still set their
own safety and technical rules, approve
designs, inspect finished work and connect
it, to complete their part within set timeframes
so competition cannot be frustrated by delay.

•

•

Allow private businesses to help build
the transmission New Zealand needs,
by amending the Electricity Industry Act
and working with the Electricity Authority
on changes to the Electricity Industry
Participation Code, so the transmission
pricing methodology can recover the costs
of qualifying interconnection investments
made by parties other than Transpower.
Require privately funded interconnection
investments to use the same benefit-based
charging methodology as Transpower’s,
so costs fall on the parties who benefit from
the extra capacity rather than entirely on the
first mover.
Provide a clear regulatory pathway for
privately owned transmission assets
to connect to and operate alongside
Transpower’s grid, subject to the same
fundamental technical, reliability and systemoperation requirements as comparable
transmission infrastructure.

Faster, cheaper connections
Solar is not the only connection that takes too long.
Connecting a new home, subdivision, farm, factory
or business to the network can be one of the slowest
and most expensive steps in any development, and
every week of delay adds to the cost of building
homes and electrifying businesses, holding back
investment and the jobs that come with it. In most
cases the customer pays for the work but has little
say over who does it, since the network company
sets the requirements, prices the job, and either
builds it or picks who will.
This term, the Electricity Authority has set new
rules for how connections are priced and processed,
and the Commerce Commission has told every
distribution business and Transpower that it expects
them to let approved independent providers compete
for connection work wherever it is safe to do so.
National will turn that expectation into a right.

Selling your power to the best buyer
More Kiwi households and businesses are putting in
solar panels and batteries, but the rules still assume
power flows one way, from a big power station to a
customer. Today each home can have only one retailer,
so a family exporting surplus solar has to sell it to the
same company it buys from. The only way to get a
better price for what they sell is to move everything
they buy as well. That hurts competition for Kiwis.
This term, the Electricity Authority has consulted on
letting households use one company for the power
they buy and another for the power they sell. National
will turn that proposal into a right, so families who invest
in their own power, including through the Home Energy
Fund, get the best price for the power they don’t use.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Give every household and business the right
to sell the power they export to any retailer or
buyer they choose, and direct the Electricity
Authority, through the Government Policy
Statement and legislation if needed, to have
this in place within two years.

•

Require lines companies to base export limits
on the real capacity of their local network, so
households can export more wherever there
is room, and make any lines company that
restricts exports demonstrate the genuine
network constraint that justifies it.

Addressing the dry year problem

Tackle lines charges

New Zealand runs on renewable power, some of the
cheapest and cleanest in the world. But when the lakes
are low for weeks on end, we need backup that can
run for weeks or months. Gas used to do that job, but
domestic gas is running down, and wind and solar can’t
store energy for months at a time.

Network charges now make up a substantial and
rapidly growing share of household power bills. Unlike
generation and retail, they are set by regulation rather
than competition. Part 4 of the Commerce Act, passed
in 2008 and applying to lines companies since 2009,
determines how much revenue most lines companies
may recover from consumers and what standards they
must meet. It was designed for a stable, one-directional
system, not one in which households generate, store
and export their own power.

Part of the problem is that the market for backup power
is thin and concentrated. The four big gentailers own
more than 95 per cent of the flexible generation that
backs these contracts, much of it used to cover their own
customers. There is now a standard product for covering
daily peaks, but still no standard, tradable contract for
the weeks or months of cover a dry year needs.
National has already levelled the playing field in the retail
and hedge markets this term, with the level playing field
reforms, the non-discrimination rules, and the Energy
Competition Task Force. Now it’s time to do the same for
backup power, the next step in the same job.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Require the Electricity Authority to publish a
single, regular, independent assessment of
the backup gap’s size and timing, covering dry
periods when the sun’s not shining, the wind’s
not blowing, and the lakes are low.

•

Promote greater transparency of the backup
market, with the Authority publishing data on
contract volumes, lengths, and price ranges,
so buyers, sellers, and investors know what
cover is worth.

•

Require the creation of a backup power
contract, so any retailer, business, or new
investor can buy guaranteed backup cover
from any supplier, on standardised terms,
with large generators required to participate
in regular auctions of these contracts.

For most lines companies, the Commerce Commission
sets a five-year revenue cap that includes a return
linked to interest rates. The current cap was set in
November 2024, when interest rates were high after
the highest inflation in more than 30 years, and it locks
in a higher allowed return until March 2030. Over the
past year, lines charges accounted for around twothirds of the rise in the average power bill.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Undertake a targeted review of Part 4 of
the Commerce Act on electricity distribution
businesses, examining whether the regime
delivers value for money for consumers,
balances investment with affordability,
and gives distribution businesses the right
incentives to make efficient use of the networks
New Zealanders have already paid for.

•

Require distribution businesses to approve
residential and small business solar connection
applications within two working days, backed
by automatic compensation for the customer
for every day a network runs over.

Putting affordability at the heart of
electricity regulation
Every household and business in New Zealand
depends on affordable power, whether it’s a family
heating their home through winter, a small business
keeping its doors open, or a manufacturer competing
on the world stage. However, the main objective of the
Electricity Authority, New Zealand’s electricity market
regulator, does not require it to consider what its
decisions mean for the power bills Kiwis pay.
Competition, reliable supply, and an efficient
market will continue to guide the Electricity
Authority’s work. National will give affordability
for households and businesses the same standing
in law, so the regulator considers the cost to families
and businesses in every decision it makes.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Implement the replacement of the RMA,
with more enabling rules for new renewable
generation built in as standard, so wind, solar,
and hydro projects are developed quickly and
power prices are kept down, without every
project needing to compete for a place on
Fast-track.

•

Set clear, national rules so that small-scale
renewables are largely permitted as of
right on people’s own property, including
letting property owners install rooftop solar
without a consent, even where height limits
or landscape overlays would otherwise get
in the way.

•

Make small-scale on-farm ground-mounted
solar a permitted activity, with standard rules
to manage genuine effects.

•

Allow small-scale battery storage not enclosed
by an existing building as of right, subject only
to standard rules.

•

Permit small run-of-river micro-hydro for
on-site use, within a set size and standard
conditions to protect rivers, fish passage and
other water users.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Amend the Electricity Industry Act to make
affordability for households and businesses a
core objective of the Electricity Authority, with
equal weight alongside competition, reliability,
and efficiency.

Cutting red tape for renewables, big
and small
RMA reform this term has already made a measurable
difference. The Southland Wind Farm was declined
under the old Act after years of process, then approved
in around five months once it moved to Fast-track. That
shows what a properly designed planning system can
do, and it should not take a special process to get that
result, or that investment and those jobs, delivered.
The same problem shows up at the other end of the scale.
Kiwis putting solar, micro-hydro, or a battery on their own
land still face costly, confusing rules that vary from council
to council. Environment Canterbury has required groundmounted solar installations to get a water discharge
consent, because rain running off the panels could
carry bird droppings. Hurunui District Council requires
consent for any free-standing installation larger than
25 square metres, barely enough to power an average
home. Micro-hydro and battery storage face the same
fragmented, region-by-region treatment.
New Zealanders should be able to draw on the
resources on their own land, and the country’s best
renewable resources at scale, with as little red tape
as possible.

The Home Energy Fund
Kiwi families are under pressure from rising power bills.
Rooftop solar, batteries, insulation and modern electric
appliances can ease that pressure and make homes
more resilient, but high upfront cost put them out of
reach for many households, and existing green loans
from commercial banks are limited.
The Ratepayer Assistance Scheme, long advocated for
by Local Government New Zealand (LGNZ) and
Rewiring Aotearoa, offers a practical, market-based
answer by providing a low-interest loan secured against
a homeowner's property and repaid over time on their
rates bill.
At the same time, National is enabling plug-in solar so
renters, who often cannot install rooftop systems, can
also generate their own cheaper power.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

•

•

•

•
•

Implement a narrower version of the
Ratepayer Assistance Scheme, called the
Home Energy Fund, a national financing
platform jointly owned by central government
and participating councils that offers
ratepayers low-interest, long-term loans
secured against their property.
Have the Fund pay the approved installer
upfront, with the homeowner repaying the
low-interest loan over ten years, either
through a charge on their rates bill or on sale
of their property.
Make eligible for support: residential solar
and battery storage; and insulation, heat
pumps, and other investments in residential
electrification.
Amend the Local Government Act and make
a one-off Crown equity investment of $7
million for a 20% shareholding in the new
entity, with the balance funded by
participating councils and the LGFA.
Make loans available to ratepayers with at
least 20% equity in their property.
Enable plug-in solar so renters can easily
generate their own cheaper power.

Data centres that bring their own
power
Large-scale data centres represent a significant
economic opportunity for New Zealand, offering
high-value investment, skilled employment and export
revenue, particularly in regions like the South Island that
combine abundant renewable energy with a cool climate
well suited to the technology. That opportunity must not
come at the expense of household power bills.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Require new large-scale data centres to bring
their own new, firmed power, applying an
additionality test so that data centre load is
always matched by new generation sufficient
to cover it.

Don’t put it all at risk
New Zealand’s energy security has been hard won this
term. Chris Hipkins and Labour would throw it away,
again, if given the chance.
•

Labour banned new oil and gas exploration once
already, and has pledged to do it again. A second
ban would tell investors New Zealand can’t be
trusted, choke off the gas that firms our renewables,
and push power prices up.

•

Labour let inflation rip out of control, and
construction and network costs went with it. If given
the chance again, every dollar lands straight back
on the lines charges in power bills.

•

Labour would leave New Zealand exposed
to every dry winter, again. No firming, no fuel
reserves, no new generation. Just rationing,
outages, and Wellington ideology deciding whether
the lights stay on, instead of a plan.

New Zealand is on the right track. A re-elected National
Government will keep delivering the secure, affordable
energy, and the jobs, that New Zealanders need.

Data centres that bring their own
power
Large-scale data centres represent a significant
economic opportunity for New Zealand, offering
high-value investment, skilled employment and export
revenue, particularly in regions like the South Island that
combine abundant renewable energy with a cool climate
well suited to the technology. That opportunity must not
come at the expense of household power bills.

  A re-elected National Government will:
  •   Require new large-scale data centres to bring
      their own new, firmed power, applying an
      additionality test so that data centre load is
      always matched by new generation sufficient
      to cover it.
