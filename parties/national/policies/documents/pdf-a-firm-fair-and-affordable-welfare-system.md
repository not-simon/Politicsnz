Fixing the Basics:

A firm, fair and affordable
welfare system
Introduction
New Zealand needs a welfare system that is firm,
fair and affordable. It should provide a safety net
when people need it, support Kiwis to move back
into employment, and remain affordable for taxpayers.
The longer someone remains on welfare, the harder
it can become to return to work, with consequences
for individuals, families and taxpayers.
However, under Labour, the number of people receiving
a Jobseeker unemployment benefit for more than two
years surged by 60 per cent – from 49,000 to 78,000 –
between 2017 and 2023, despite unemployment falling
over that period. Too many New Zealanders became
stuck on welfare for years, rather than getting the
support and facing the clear expectations needed
to move back into work.
Since coming into Government, National has put
employment back at the centre of the welfare system,
restoring the principle of rights and responsibilities
through clearer expectations and consequences under
the Traffic Light System.
But there is more to do.
A re-elected National Government will build on
the progress already made by investing earlier to
help people move into work, while strengthening
expectations for those who remain on Jobseeker long
term. With Treasury forecasting 220,000 more people
in employment over the next four years, we want to
ensure Kiwis are ready to take up those opportunities,
particularly in growth areas such as primary industries,
construction and infrastructure, tourism and hospitality.
Our plan is centred on five initiatives:
•

Investing earlier to help long-term Jobseekers into
work. National will provide more intensive, workfocused support for 30,000 work-ready jobseekers
who have been on a benefit for more than a year,
supporting them move into employment before
long-term dependency becomes entrenched, with
greater funding for employment programmes that
support young people into work.

•

Stronger expectations after two years. From 1 April
2028, jobseekers who have received Jobseeker
for two of the previous three years will move to a
lower rate of payment, with a smaller reduction for
households with dependent children.

Authorised by J de Joux, ps@national.org.nz

•

Introduce random drug testing for Jobseekers.
National will introduce random drug testing as part
of MSD’s work-readiness and employment planning,
starting with 10,000 tests in the first year and
expanding the programme once established.
Those who fail a second test will be required to
undertake an alcohol and drug assessment, with
continued non-compliance resulting in sanctions.

•

Double the stand-down for repeated voluntary
unemployment. People who repeatedly leave
employment without good and sufficient reason
will face a 26-week non-entitlement period, up
from 13 weeks.

•

Extending the benefit residency requirement
to five years. From 1 April 2028, the minimum
residence period for main benefits will increase
from two years to five years.

Together, these will provide more support to help people
into work, while making clear that those who can work
are expected to.

Welfare support should always be
temporary
National believes the Jobseeker unemployment benefit
should be a temporary safety net for New Zealanders
who find themselves out of work. However, historically
around 40 per cent of people receiving a Jobseeker
unemployment benefit have remained on a benefit
continuously for more than two years, irrespective of
underlying economic conditions.
This is not what the system was intended to do, and
the consequences of long-term dependency can be
significant. The longer someone remains on a benefit,
the harder it can become to return to work. Those
consequences can also carry across generations.
Children who grow up in benefit-dependent households
are more likely to receive a benefit themselves later
in life, increasing the risk that disadvantage becomes
entrenched from one generation to the next.
Reducing long-term dependency is therefore not just
about helping people into work today. It is also about
improving opportunities for their children and breaking
the cycle of intergenerational poverty.

However, there is strong evidence that targeted
employment support, backed by clear expectations,
can help more people move off welfare and into work.
Under the previous National Government, a series of
reforms introduced in 2012 and 2013 strengthened
work obligations and encouraged labour force
participation and formal education. Where work
and work-preparedness obligations were not met,
financial sanctions were introduced and applied to
encourage compliance.
In the period immediately following these reforms,
13,000 individuals moved off a main benefit, including
8,000 fewer jobseekers and nearly 5,000 fewer
people on sole parent support. This was estimated to
contribute to a $4.4 billion decline in the Government’s
long-term welfare liability, driven by higher rates of exit
from welfare and into work, and a lower rate of return
onto a benefit for those who had exited into work.
Getting the balance between support and responsibility
right is critical to reducing long-term welfare
dependency and ensuring the welfare system remains
sustainable for those who need it. A re-elected National
Government will invest earlier to help people into work,
while strengthening expectations and consequences
for those who remain on welfare long term.

National’s plan to rebalance welfare
towards work
Jobseeker Employment Support: Investing to
help long-term Jobseekers back into work
The best time to intervene is before long-term
dependency becomes entrenched and people
become increasingly detached from the labour
market. That is why National will establish a new
Jobseeker Employment Support initiative, providing
targeted, work-focused support to 30,000 work-ready
Jobseekers who have received a benefit for at least
12 months. At least 5,000 of these places will be
specifically targeted at young Jobseekers, aged 18-24.
Modelled on the “Invest to Save” initiative funded
through this year’s Budget, Jobseeker Employment
Support will fund intensive, work-focused case
management and greater access to employment
programmes. Recipients will receive a needs
assessment, an individual employment plan and
practical support to address the barriers keeping
them out of work, including targeted training,
employment assistance and support connecting
with employers.

1

Targeted case management works. By identifying what
is keeping someone out of work and providing practical
support to address those barriers, this initiative will help
more long-term jobseekers make sustained moves into
employment, lift household incomes and reduce the
number of New Zealanders reliant on welfare, including
children growing up in benefit-dependent households.
As part of Jobseeker Employment Support, National will
also invest an additional $12 million per annum in He
Poutama Rangatahi, on top of existing baseline funding,
to provide more employment support for young
New Zealanders at risk of long-term unemployment.
He Poutama Rangatahi has demonstrated strong
results in helping young people overcome barriers
and move towards work. The Ministry of Social
Development has evaluated the programme and
found it was effective at achieving its short-term
goals, delivering employment, education and training
outcomes for young people. This additional investment
will build on what is working and help more young
New Zealanders get the support they need to move
towards employment and independence1.

Reduce Jobseeker payments after two years
To rebalance Jobseeker Support towards its original
intent and strengthen the incentive to move into work,
National will introduce a lower rate of Jobseeker
Support for people who have been on the benefit
for two of the previous three years.
From 1 April 2028:
•

Single Jobseeker recipients without children
would now receive the rate paid to 20–24 year
olds and 18–19 year olds living independently. If
introduced today, this would be a reduction of
$48.05 per week.

•

If introduced today, couples without children would
receive $552.18 a week, an effective reduction of
$81.76 per week.

•

Jobseeker recipients with dependent children
would see a smaller reduction, equivalent to $24.02
per week if introduced today, half the reduction
applying to a single recipient without children.

Actual reductions will reflect Jobseeker rates in 2028,
which will have increased on 1 April 2027 and 1 April
2028 to reflect inflation. The Supported Living Payment
is unchanged.

Ministry of Social Development, He Poutama Rangatahi Evaluation, 2024.

To prevent people avoiding the lower rate through
short breaks from the benefit, eligibility will be based on
cumulative time receiving Jobseeker Support. Anyone
who has received Jobseeker Support for at least two
of the previous three years will move to the lower rate.
This change reinforces the principle that Jobseeker
Support is there to help people back into work, not to
keep them on a benefit indefinitely. It sends a clear
signal that support comes with an expectation of work,
strengthens the incentive to take up available jobs,
and helps keep the welfare system affordable for the
New Zealanders who need it most.

Introduce random drug testing for
Jobseekers
Being ready for work means being able to take up
the jobs that are available. For many New Zealanders,
drug testing is a normal condition of employment. It is
reasonable to expect work-ready Jobseekers to meet
the same basic expectation.
National will introduce random drug testing for
Jobseekers, integrated into MSD’s work-readiness and
employment planning. We will undertake 10,000 random
drug tests in the first year, with the programme expanding
once it is established and operating effectively.
Those who fail a random drug test will be required
to undertake a second test. A second failed test will
trigger a mandatory alcohol and drug assessment to
identify whether drug use is a barrier to employment
and what steps are needed to address it. Where
appropriate, those steps will become part of the
person’s work-readiness plan.
Jobseekers will be expected to comply with testing,
assessment and any resulting work-readiness
requirements. Continued non-compliance without
good and sufficient reason will result in sanctions.
New Zealanders are regularly required to pass drug
tests to get or keep a job. It is not too much to ask that
people receiving Jobseeker Support who are required
to be available for work take reasonable steps to
remain work-ready. Drug use should not be allowed to
become an unaddressed barrier to moving off welfare
and into employment. We expect this policy to cost
approximately $1.5 million per annum, which will be
funded from MSD baselines.

Double the stand-down for repeated
voluntary unemployment
The welfare system is there to support people
who need it, but it should not remove personal
responsibility. Where someone repeatedly chooses
to leave employment without good and sufficient
reason, taxpayers should not be expected to
immediately pick up the cost.
The current rules allow a 13-week non-entitlement
period for repeated voluntary unemployment, but
there should be stronger consequences where this
behaviour continues.
A re-elected National Government will extend the
current 13-week non-entitlement period for people
who repeatedly choose to leave their job to 26 weeks.
This is consistent with the approach taken in the
United Kingdom, where repeated failures relating
to employment can attract substantially longer
benefit sanctions.
This change reinforces the principle that Kiwis who
can work and repeatedly choose to leave employment
without good reason should be accountable for that
decision – not taxpayers. The welfare system should
provide support when people genuinely need it,
alongside clear responsibilities for those who are
able to work.

Extend the benefit residency requirement
to five years
New Zealand’s welfare system is funded by taxpayers
to provide a safety net for people who need it. But
under the current rules, a new resident can become
eligible for a main benefit after only two years in
New Zealand. That does not reflect the reasonable
expectation that people settling in New Zealand should
support themselves and their families through work
or their own resources before becoming eligible for
long-term taxpayer-funded income support.
A re-elected National Government will extend the
minimum residency requirement for main benefits from
two years to five years, from 1 April 2028. Starting on
that date gives time to pass any necessary legislation.
The new requirement will apply to everyone who has
not yet completed five years of residence at that date,
but anyone already receiving a main benefit will keep
it. Refugees and protected persons will continue to
be exempt, and MSD will retain its existing ability to
provide hardship assistance in genuine emergencies.

This is consistent with the approach in other countries.
In Australia, most new permanent residents must wait
four years before they can receive JobSeeker Payment.
In the United Kingdom, most migrants cannot claim

benefits until they are granted settlement, usually after
five years. This change will support the affordability of
the welfare system while maintaining a fair safety net
for those who need it.

Net fiscal savings

Jobseeker Employment Support
Jobseeker Employment Support will target 30,000
work-ready Jobseekers who have been receiving
Jobseeker for more than a year and are at risk of
remaining on welfare long term. The cost and annual
funding profile for the initiative is based on the Budget
2026 Invest to Save initiative, adjusted pro rata from
25,000 to 30,000 places, with savings scaled for the
relative weekly cost of Jobseeker, and a modestly
lower rate of success, reflecting the reality that this
programme is targeted at individuals who have been
on jobseeker for longer than one year.

Lower Jobseeker Rate for Long-term
Recipients
The lower rate of Jobseeker for long-term recipients
is estimated to save around $572 million over the
forecast period.
This estimate starts from the number of people who
have been receiving Jobseeker Support for more than
two years and projects the affected cohort forward
using the PREFU 2026 forecast for Jobseeker numbers.
The fiscal costing is therefore based on the official
forecast rather than assuming National’s Jobseeker
reduction target is achieved.

Savings are net of tax and allow for one-fifth of the
reduction being offset by increases in other assistance.
Rates are indexed to inflation each year. Estimated
savings do not assume any further fiscal benefits from
more people moving off a benefit and into work.

Residency Requirement
The five-year residency requirement is estimated to
save around $50 million over the forecast period.
The change will take effect on 1 April 2028, which
allows time to legislate, and applies to all residents
who have not yet completed five years of residence
at that date.
Because the change stops new benefit grants rather
than removing existing ones, savings build over time.
These estimates allow for people already receiving a
benefit to keep it. The longer stand-down for voluntary
unemployment and mandatory clinical assessment will
generate further savings, although these are expected
to be modest and are not reflected here.
