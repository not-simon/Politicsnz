## Full text (reading order)

Building the Future:

More medicines and earlier care
Over this term, National has reduced wasteful spending
to invest in the frontline, putting patients back at the
heart of the health system with faster access to care
and the largest ever funding boost for new cancer and
other life-saving medicines. Now, National will lock in
that progress and go further.
Responsible fiscal management is paying a dividend.
Together, National and hardworking Kiwis have put
New Zealand back on a path to surplus, and National
will maintain that discipline by bringing money
back home. We will reduce foreign aid outside the
Pacific, where we will continue to support our closest
neighbours and strongest interests. Every dollar saved
will be put to work for Kiwis fighting cancer and other
serious illnesses, with no new taxes.
A re-elected National Government will deliver a further
$604 million uplift in Pharmac’s medicines budget, paid
for by these savings. The last time National made this
investment, it delivered 66 new medicines benefiting
hundreds of thousands of Kiwis, including 33 new
cancer treatments.
National will also back new ways to bring the latest
treatments to New Zealand through more clinical trials
and locally produced CAR-T cell therapy. Money that
was once spent offshore will instead be invested in
cutting-edge care, ensuring more Kiwis benefit from
world-class treatment here at home.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Deliver a further $604 million uplift in
Pharmac’s medicines budget, building on
National’s track record of expanding access to
new cancer and other life-saving medicines.

•

Turn responsible fiscal management into
more medicines, by reducing foreign aid
outside the Pacific.

•

Set a national target for clinical trials in
New Zealand, so more Kiwis can access the
newest treatments sooner, with a focus on
cancer and rare disorders.

•

Unlock CAR-T cell therapy by supporting
New Zealand’s medical sector to develop and
produce this life-changing cancer treatment
here at home.

Authorised by J de Joux, ps@national.org.nz

Preventing serious illness
The best time to beat cancer and chronic disease is
before it takes hold. A re-elected National Government
will expand screening and prevention, building on
the progress achieved this term. National’s package
brings hundreds of thousands more Kiwis into free
cancer screening, trials new ways to find prostate and
lung cancer early, and helps more Kiwis get active and
avoid chronic diseases like diabetes. That means more
cancers caught when they are most treatable, and more
New Zealanders staying healthy.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Lower the free bowel screening age to 50
by 2029, backed by additional colonoscopies,
so bowel cancer is caught earlier when it is
most treatable.

•

Complete the free breast screening
extension to age 74 by 2029, finishing the
job National has started so more women have
access to early detection.

•

Launch new screening pilots for prostate
and lung cancer, so more cancers are found
earlier.

•

Deliver free cervical screening for all women
aged 25 to 69 from March 2027, so cervical
cancer can be stopped before it starts.

•

Establish the Healthy Kiwis Programme,
expanding active prescriptions with a target
to double the number of people receiving
them by 2030, helping more Kiwis get active,
stay healthy, and avoid chronic diseases
like diabetes.

Increasing the medicines budget
When National entered Government in 2023, Labour
had left behind a funding cliff in Pharmac’s budget,
putting New Zealanders’ access to medicines at risk.
National filled that gap, then went further with a record
$604 million uplift in Pharmac’s medicines budget,
delivering 66 new medicines benefiting around
250,000 Kiwis, including 33 new cancer treatments.
But there’s more to do. Too many New Zealanders
are still missing out on medicines available in other
countries, making their fight against cancer and other
serious illnesses even harder.
A re-elected National Government will invest that same
$604 million again, largely paid for by reducing foreign
aid outside the Pacific.
For Kiwis, this means even more people can access
the medicines they need. Pharmac will be able to
fund new cancer and other life-saving medicines that
aren’t currently available here, and widen eligibility for
treatments that are already funded so more people
benefit from them.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Deliver a further $604 million uplift in
Pharmac’s medicines budget, building on
National’s track record of expanding access to
new cancer treatments and other medicines
for New Zealanders.

Clinical trials for more Kiwis
Clinical trials are one of the best ways for Kiwis to get
the newest treatments, often years before they are
publicly available. For someone facing cancer, a trial
can be a chance to try a promising new medicine they
could not get any other way. It matters even more
for people living with a rare disorder, where so few
treatments are funded that a trial can be one of the only
options available.
Trials are good for New Zealand too. Between 2013 and
2018, trials of new medicines added more than $150
million a year to our economy and supported more than
700 jobs. However, companies wanting to run a trial
here often face a slow and confusing approvals process
that changes from district to district, adding cost and
delay. As a result, trials are too often taken to other
countries, and New Zealanders miss out.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Set a national target that within ten years, 5
per cent of New Zealanders being treated for
cancer will have part of their care delivered
through a clinical trial, where a trial is right for
them and they want to take part, aligning with
Australia.

•

Set up a single national front door for clinical
trials, so companies have one clear place to
go and patients can be matched to trials more
easily, including those with a rare disorder
who often struggle to find a relevant study.

•

Simplify and standardise the approvals
process, so a trial approved once can run
right across the country, while keeping strong
protections for patient safety.

Unlocking CAR-T cell therapy for cancer
patients
CAR-T cell therapy works by taking a patient’s own
immune cells, reprogramming them to recognise and
attack their cancer, and returning them to the body to
do the job. For some people with rare or aggressive
cancers that have not responded to other treatments,
it can be life-changing. New Zealanders who need
this therapy currently rely on having their cells sent
overseas to be made, which is hugely expensive, yet
New Zealand has the scientists, clinicians, and knowhow to do it here.
A re-elected National Government will back BioOra, a
New Zealand-based commercial-stage biotechnology
company, with a $25 million co-investment to expand
its cell therapy manufacturing, research, and clinical
facilities in Christchurch and produce CAR-T right here
at home. BioOra’s Christchurch precinct could supply
the whole country, anchoring a high-tech industry
in the South Island, supporting skilled jobs, and
keeping New Zealand at the cutting edge of the fight
against cancer.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Co-invest $25 million with BioOra to expand
its cell therapy manufacturing, research, and
clinical facilities in Christchurch, so Kiwis
who need CAR-T can be treated at home
rather than sent overseas.

The Prevention Package
The best time to fight cancer and chronic disease is
before it takes hold. National’s Prevention Package will
find more cancers early and keep more Kiwis healthy
and out of hospital.

Catching bowel cancer earlier
New Zealand’s rate of bowel cancer is among the
highest in the world, with more than 3,300 Kiwis
diagnosed every year and over 1,200 losing their
lives to it. National has already lowered the screening
age from 60 to 56 as first steps towards aligning with
Australia, but we are not stopping there.
Early detection is the difference between survival and
tragedy. People whose bowel cancer is found and
treated at an early stage have a 90 per cent chance
of long-term survival. National will take the next steps
to lower the screening age to 50, bringing hundreds
of thousands more Kiwis into the programme. Taking
steps to lower the screening age to 50 is expected to
prevent 1,149 cancer cases over ten years.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Extend free bowel screening, by lowering the
age by two years every year to reach 50 in the
next term of Government, so we catch bowel
cancer earlier when it is most treatable.

•

Continue the roll out of free bowel cancer
tests for people of any age with symptoms, so
cancer is found sooner no matter how old a
patient is.

•

Deliver additional colonoscopies to increase
system capacity.

Earlier detection for breast and prostate
cancer
Breast cancer is New Zealand’s most commonly
diagnosed cancer in women, with nearly 3,900
new cases a year, and the risk grows with age. Free
mammograms currently stop at 69, leaving older
women to pay for screening themselves or go without.
National has already begun extending free breast
screening to women aged 70 to 74, and a re-elected
National Government will finish the job.

Kiwi men deserve the same focus on early detection.
Prostate cancer is the most common cancer among
New Zealand men, with more than 4,000 diagnosed
and more than 700 deaths each year, making it the
second biggest cause of cancer death for men after
lung cancer. Found early, more than 90 per cent of men
survive at least five years, but that falls below 40 per
cent once the cancer has spread.
Unlike breast, cervical, and bowel cancer, there is no
organised screening programme for prostate cancer, so
whether a man gets checked is too often left to chance.
National will pilot organised screening in the two
districts with the highest demand, allowing Health New
Zealand to gather evidence for a potential nationwide
programme, based on a proposal from the Prostate
Cancer Foundation.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Complete the free breast screening
extension to age 74 by 2029, ensuring older
women have access to early detection and
life-saving preventative care.

•

Launch a prostate cancer screening pilot in
two high-demand districts, offering men aged
50 to 70 regular screening to evaluate what
works for a potential nationwide programme.

Free cervical screening for all women aged
25 to 69
Cervical cancer is among the most preventable
cancers, because nearly every case is caused by HPV,
a common virus the screening test picks up well before
any cancer develops. When it is caught early, around
91 per cent of women survive, but that falls to around
19 per cent once it has spread.
Unlike breast and bowel screening, cervical
screening is not free for every woman. That’s despite
successive Parliamentary Reviews since 2018 having
recommended it.

As announced in September, National has funded free
cervical screening for all women aged 25 to 69 from
1 March 2027, meaning an estimated 786,000 women
will no longer have to pay.

A re-elected National Government will:
•

Establish the Healthy Kiwis Programme to
deliver active prescriptions, with a target to
double the number of people receiving them
by 2030, helping more Kiwis get active, eat
well, and avoid chronic diseases like diabetes.

•

Work with Bowel Cancer New Zealand to
determine how the CHALLENGE exercise
programme could be made available
for bowel cancer patients, with evidence
suggesting it can reduce the risk of death by
37 per cent in bowel cancer patients who have
completed surgery.

Earlier detection for lung cancer
Lung cancer is New Zealand’s biggest cancer killer,
claiming more than 1,700 lives every year, more
than breast cancer, prostate cancer, and melanoma
combined. Most people are only diagnosed once the
disease is advanced and the chance of a cure is slim.
Screening can change that. International trials have
found that low-dose CT scans for people at high risk
reduce lung cancer deaths by 20 to 25 per cent, and
Australia began screening through its own national
programme in July 2025.
A re-elected National Government will deliver a lung
cancer screening pilot in Bay of Plenty and Hawke’s
Bay, offering low-dose CT scans to people at high risk,
such as current and former smokers. The pilot will test
the best way to find, invite, and screen those most at
risk, catching more lung cancers early and building the
evidence for a potential nationwide programme.

Keeping Kiwis active, healthy, and out of
hospital
New Zealand is facing a chronic disease epidemic.
The number of Kiwis with type 2 diabetes is set to
nearly double by 2040, yet most cases are preventable,
and billions are spent every year treating disease that
could be stopped before it starts. Beating this epidemic
means getting Kiwis moving and eating well.
Active prescriptions do exactly that. A GP or practice
nurse prescribes exercise and healthier eating, and a
trained coach then provides free, personalised support
over several months to help the changes last. National
introduced the scheme in 1998 to get Kiwis active and
healthy, and it works. The evidence shows that 85 per
cent of patients are more active after receiving their
prescription, as are 80 per cent of child patients, and
patients see significant improvements in HbA1c, blood
pressure, and BMI. Yet only 26,000 people a year
receive this support, a fraction of those who need it.
National will expand active prescriptions through a
new Healthy Kiwis Programme, working with local
providers across the country, and setting a target for
Health New Zealand to double the number of people
receiving them by 2030. That means more Kiwis getting
the support they need to stay healthy, fewer cases of
chronic disease, and less pressure on our hospitals.

Fiscal impact
The Pharmac uplift will be paid for by bringing money
back home from foreign aid, with the remaining funded
from future operating allowances.
Since 2017, MFAT’s budget has grown from $1.05
billion to nearly $2 billion a year and its staff numbers
have risen by more than 34 per cent. Millions of
dollars have gone towards, among other things, smart
agriculture systems in Latin America and the Caribbean,
workshops with indigenous farmers in Patagonia, and
installing livestock emissions equipment at universities
in South Africa.
National will reduce New Zealand’s International
Development Cooperation (IDC) budget, which funds
our foreign aid, and associated administration costs
by 10 per cent, saving $431.2 million over the forecast
period. Aid to the Pacific, which makes up around
66 per cent of IDC funding, will be fully protected,
reflecting the importance of our closest neighbours and
strongest interests.
National will also make MFAT more efficient. When other
government agencies found savings in the 2026 round
of baseline reductions, MFAT was exempt. National
will now bring MFAT into line with the rest of the public
sector, catching up through a 2 per cent baseline
expenditure reduction that saves a further $45.6 million
over the forecast period.
The Prevention Package will be funded through
existing baseline health funding and future operating
allowances. Under National, the expanded breast
screening age, free cervical screening for all women
aged 25 to 69, and a lung cancer screening pilot
are already funded, and National is committed to
protecting that funding and delivering these services
for New Zealanders.
