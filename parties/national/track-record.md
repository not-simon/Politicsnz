# National Party — track record: promises vs delivery (2008–2026)

How reliable has National been? This page goes through National's main election promises promise by promise, for the Key/English governments (2008–2017), the two campaigns it lost (2017, 2020), and above all the Luxon-led coalition government elected in 2023. Each promise gets what was promised, what happened, a verdict and a source. After the tables come a scorecard, an analysis of where National's actions matched or departed from its stated values, its notable U-turns, and the trends over time.

## How to read the verdicts

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ✅ **kept** | Delivered substantially as promised. It may have been late or changed in detail, and the table says so. |
| 🟡 **partially kept** | Some of it was delivered, or it was delivered in a clearly weaker form, or the headline claim overstated what most people would get. |
| ❌ **broken** | National did something different from what it promised, or missed a deadline it set, while in a position to deliver. |
| ⛔ **abandoned** | Dropped. This mostly happened in coalition negotiations, or because National lost the election (marked "untested"). |
| ⏳ **in progress** | Under way, with an outcome or deadline still to come. These are left out of the percentages. |
| 🗳️ **not yet due** | A 2026 campaign promise, listed for reference. |

**Caveats.** The same standard applies to every party in this archive:
1. **Selection.** The list covers the high-profile, checkable promises: pledge cards, flagship policies, 100-day plans, and promises that critics or the media singled out. It is not every line of every manifesto. Leaving out many small, low-profile promises (most of which are routinely kept) would push the "kept" share up.
2. **Easy vs hard promises.** Many of the 2023 promises were to repeal or stop something the previous government did. A government with a majority can always deliver these. Outcome promises (a surplus, waiting times, child poverty, police numbers) are much harder and depend on the economy. The analysis below looks at the two types separately.
3. **Coalition and minority government.** Under MMP, National has always governed with partners: on confidence-and-supply agreements in 2008–17, and in a formal three-party coalition since 2023. Where a promise died in coalition talks it is marked *abandoned*, not *broken*, and the partner that blocked it is named.
4. **External shocks.** The table notes these where relevant: the 2008–09 global financial crisis, the Canterbury earthquakes of 2010–11, the 2024 recession, and the 2026 Middle East oil shock.
5. A few figures come from the government or a party. They are labelled as such. Where independent sources disagree, both are given.

**Headline findings**

- **Key/English (2008–17): a modest number of promises, mostly kept, with one memorable breach.** The government delivered its flagship pledges: the flag referendum, free GP visits for under-13s, longer paid parental leave, and a (narrow) return to surplus in 2014/15, which is often wrongly remembered as missed. It pushed through the partial asset sales despite a referendum against them. The clearest broken promise was **GST**: Key said in 2008 that National would not raise it, and in 2010 it went from 12.5% to 15% as part of a tax switch. Several promises fell just short, including the Christchurch anchor projects, the Auckland Housing Accord (96% of its target) and the asset-sale proceeds (about $4.7b against $5–7b signalled).
- **Luxon (2023–26): a very high delivery rate on "stop/repeal/legislate" promises, and a weak one on outcome promises.** Of the 28 "kept" verdicts for 2023, about half are repeals or reversals of Labour-era policy: Fair Pay Agreements, Three Waters, the Natural and Built Environment Act, the Reserve Bank's dual mandate, light rail, the regional fuel tax, the Clean Car Discount, the oil and gas ban, the Māori Health Authority and others. These were all delivered, many under urgency. The broken or slipping promises are mostly about outcomes:
  - the **2026/27 surplus** (now forecast for 2028/29, and only on a new "OBEGALx" measure);
  - the **500 extra police** by November 2025;
  - **fees-free** (promised to keep it, moved it to final year in coalition, then scrapped it in Budget 2026);
  - the **Dunedin Hospital** pledge;
  - the "halve child poverty by 2028" commitment, which is off track;
  - the health waiting-time targets, which are far from 95%.
- **Coalition costs were real but limited.** Three campaign planks died in talks with NZ First and ACT: the foreign-buyer tax (which was to help fund the tax cuts), raising the super age to 67, and the "two-out-one-in" farm rule. In the other direction, the partners pushed National further than it had campaigned: faster restoration of interest deductibility, the Treaty Principles Bill going to select committee, repeal of the smokefree generation law, and the Regulatory Standards Act.
- **Independent and partisan tallies.** National says it "kept the promises we made to New Zealanders in 2023" ([National, 27 Sep 2026](https://www.national.org.nz/news/260927-christopherluxon-1)). The volunteer-run [NZ Politics Promise Tracker](https://www.nzpoliticspromisetracker.nz/parties) lists 45 National promises: 23 kept, 18 in progress, 1 broken (page saved 23 Oct 2026). [Parliament Pulse](https://parliamentpulse.co.nz/promise-tracker) also runs a tracker, but its data loads by script and could not be read; a search-engine summary reported 9 kept, 1 in progress, 1 stalled, 1 broken, which this archive could not verify. This page finds more broken and partial verdicts than those trackers do. The main reasons are that it counts outcome promises (surplus date, child poverty, Dunedin) and treats the fees-free reversal as broken.

## Where actions matched National's stated values, and where they did not

National describes itself as the party of "limited government", "competitive enterprise", "personal responsibility", fiscal prudence, lower taxes, law and order, and "recognition of the Treaty of Waitangi as the founding document" ([party principles, via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_National_Party)).

**Where actions matched**
- **Lower taxes and smaller government.** The 2024 income-tax relief ($3.7b a year), the restored interest deductibility, the shorter bright-line test and Investment Boost (2025) all match the platform. The public service shrank 4.6% from its December 2023 peak to June 2025 ([Public Service Commission](https://www.publicservice.govt.nz/data/workforce-data/public-sector-composition/workforce-size)).
- **Law and order.** It delivered the gang-patch ban, three strikes, the youth offender category and the sentencing changes (capped discounts, longer sentences) it campaigned on. Both of its crime targets were met early ([RNZ, June 2026](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/politics/613970/pm-christopher-luxon-s-public-sector-targets-the-hits-and-misses-in-latest-update)).
- **Pro-development deregulation.** Fast-track, the RMA replacement, the return of oil and gas exploration and the end of the Auckland light-rail programme are all consistent with the 2023 platform.
- **Moderation on the Treaty.** National kept its pre-election line on ACT's Treaty Principles Bill: it would support it to select committee and no further. It voted the bill down 112–11 in April 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_Principles_Bill)).
- **Key era.** It campaigned openly on asset sales in 2011 and then carried them out, so it was transparent about an unpopular policy. The 2015 Budget's hardship package and benefit increase for families with children ($790m) went further than many expected of a centre-right government ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_National_Government_of_New_Zealand)).

**Where actions did not match**
1. **Fiscal prudence versus results.** National promised a surplus in 2026/27 and said its tax cuts would need no extra borrowing. In practice:
   - Budget 2024 still increased total borrowing by about $12b ([Newshub analysis](https://newshub.co.nz/home/politics/2024/05/budget-2024-jenna-lynch-analysis-the-as-advertised-budget-brings-tax-cuts-for-most-but-who-s-left-behind.html)).
   - The surplus date slipped several times. The government then moved to a more favourable measure (OBEGALx, which excludes ACC).
   - Net debt is forecast to peak at 43.9% of GDP ([RNZ on PREFU 2026](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/politics_election-2026/1647005/treasury-gives-willis-pre-election-boost-as-surplus-forecast-improves)), above National's own 40% ceiling.
   - Weak growth explains much of this, but not all of it.
2. **Health promises versus health choices.**
   - The coalition repealed the world-first smokefree generation law. National had not campaigned on this, and Willis acknowledged the revenue mattered to the books (about $1b) ([ODT](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/national/smokefree-law-changes-backwards-step-experts)).
   - Budget 2024 left out the promised cancer-drug funding, which was only fixed after a backlash ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2024/06/04/luxon-promises-cancer-drug-announcement-before-christmas)).
   - The Dunedin Hospital promise was reversed ([ODT](https://www.odt.co.nz/news/dunedin/national-pledges-dunedin-hospital-budget-boost)).
3. **"Cuts won't touch the frontline."** Luxon said cuts would be confined to the back office ([Newstalk ZB](https://newstalkzb.co.nz/news/politics/prime-minister-christopher-luxon-defends-public-sector-job-cuts-govt-wants-more-medical-doctors-not-spin-doctors)). Yet early savings rounds hit disability respite funding (partly reversed after an outcry) and Health NZ roles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_National_Government_of_New_Zealand)). Public service headcount then rose again by 2.0% in the year to June 2026.
4. **Process.** Several major changes were made under urgency, with no public submissions and no campaign mandate:
   - four laws in the 100-day plan were passed with no public input ([RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/511135/100-day-plan-report-card-15-items-completion-up-for-debate));
   - the Equal Pay Amendment Act 2025, which cancelled 33 pay-equity claims, mostly affecting women in care and health work ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_Pay_Amendment_Act_2025));
   - Budget 2025 halved the government's KiwiSaver contribution.

   Ministers argued these were necessary fiscal or legal fixes.
5. **Child poverty.** Luxon recommitted to the 2028 target, but the interim target was loosened by gazette notice. Child material hardship then rose to 14.3% in the year to June 2025 ([1News / Stats NZ](https://www.1news.co.nz/2026/02/26/unacceptable-childrens-commissioner-says-child-poverty-progress-has-stalled); [RNZ](https://rnz.co.nz/news/political/523167/minister-defends-lowered-child-poverty-targets-as-achievable)).
6. **Climate.** National kept the "Net Zero 2050" pledge on paper. But it cut the methane target and reopened oil and gas exploration ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2025/10/12/govt-to-lower-methane-reduction-target-rules-out-methane-taxes)), and in 2026 passed a law blocking climate tort claims. The near-term emissions budgets are still on track.
7. **Key era.** It raised GST after saying it would not. It went ahead with asset sales after 67% opposed them in a referendum (on a 45% turnout). The housing affordability crisis grew through its nine years, despite housing accords.

## Trends over time

1. **Vote share (party vote / seats).**

   | 2008 | 2011 | 2014 | 2017 | 2020 | 2023 | 2026 polls |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | 44.9% / 58 | 47.3% / 59 | 47.0% / 60 | 44.4% / 56 | 25.6% / 33 | 38.1% / 48 | about 26–32% |

   Under Key, National had the highest vote shares of the MMP era. It then collapsed in 2020 and partly recovered in 2023. By October 2026 its polling had fallen back near its 2020 level.
2. **Promise style has changed.** Key and English made fewer, broad, incremental promises and generally met them, with little fanfare. The Luxon era has relied on published checklists: an eight-point pledge card, a 49-point 100-day plan, quarterly action plans and nine 2030 targets. This makes delivery easier to audit. It also produces a high "ticked off" rate, because many items are procedural ("begin work", "introduce a bill") or repeals.
3. **Outcome promises are the weak spot in every era.** Surplus dates (missed in 2026/27; narrowly met in 2014/15), job numbers (2011), housing targets (2013–16), police numbers (2025), waiting times and child poverty (2026) are where National most often falls short. External shocks explain part of each miss.
4. **Coalition dynamics have intensified.** Under the 2008–17 confidence-and-supply arrangements, National's own manifesto largely survived. The 2023 three-party coalition dropped major National planks (the foreign-buyer tax and the super age) and added policies National had not campaigned on (smokefree repeal, Regulatory Standards, Treaty Principles to select committee). Both partners also used "agree to disagree" clauses against National's own bills, such as the under-16 social media ban.
5. **Stable themes across five elections:** income-tax relief, Roads of National Significance, a surplus target, work-focused welfare, longer sentences, and (until the 2023 coalition agreement dropped it) a higher super age.
6. **Leadership stability has fallen.** National had one leader from 2006 to 2016 and five from 2016 to 2021. Luxon survived caucus confidence votes in April and August 2026, the second followed by the sacking of minister Chris Penk ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2026/08/12/pm-wins-leadership-vote-after-challenge-fails-penk-wont-seek-re-election/)).
