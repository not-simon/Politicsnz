# Minor parties in New Zealand, 2011–2026

This page covers every party *other than* National, Labour, the Greens, ACT, NZ First and Te Pāti Māori that contested
the 2011, 2014, 2017, 2020 or 2023 general elections or is contesting the 7 November 2026 election. Each name links to
a profile. Numbers are party-vote percentages from the official results as tabulated in the Wikipedia election
articles (extracted by `tools/minor_parties_results.py`; raw data `raw/minor-parties/results.json`); seats won are in
bold brackets. Status is as of 23 October 2026.

## Trends

### 1. A crowded ballot, but the same small club in Parliament
The number of registered parties on the ballot rose from 13 (2011) to 17 (2020, 2023, 2026), yet the number winning
seats fell from eight to five or six. Every party that won seats from 2017 on was one of the "big six". The two minor
parties that held seats in 2011–14 — United Future and Mana — did so only through a single electorate seat, and both
lost it (Dunne retired in 2017; Harawira was beaten in 2014 after National and NZ First leaders encouraged tactical
votes for Labour's Kelvin Davis). [1][5][6]

### 2. The 5% threshold and the electorate "lifeboat"
Under MMP a party needs 5% of the party vote **or** one electorate seat. In this period no new party crossed 5%; the
closest were the Conservatives (3.97% in 2014) and TOP (2.44% in 2017). The electorate route explains every minor-party
MP: Ōhāriu (United Future, helped by National's low-key campaigns there, which Labour called an "unprincipled political
deal"), Te Tai Tokerau (Mana), and — for the big six — Epsom (ACT) and the Māori seats (Te Pāti Māori, which won six
seats in 2023 on 3.08%). Small parties therefore chase winnable electorates: Colin Craig's hoped-for accommodation on
the North Shore (refused by John Key in 2014), TOP's Ōhāriu (2020) and Ilam (2023, Raf Manji second by 7,830 votes),
DemocracyNZ's Northland (2023) and Te Tai Tokerau Party's bid in 2026. [5][7][8][9]

The Independent Electoral Review (final report November 2023) examined the threshold and the one-electorate rule; its
report could not be re-fetched for this page, so its recommendations are not summarised here. No threshold change was
made for 2026: the Electoral Amendment Act 2025 dealt with enrolment deadlines, prisoner voting, donation disclosure
thresholds and conduct near polling places. [10]

### 3. "Wasted" votes peaked in 2020
The share of party votes for parties winning no seat nearly doubled from 3.3% (2011) to 6.1% (2014) as the Conservatives
and Internet MANA both missed out, and peaked at **7.7% in 2020**, when NZ First (2.60%) fell out of Parliament alongside
TOP (1.51%), New Conservative (1.48%) and Advance NZ (0.98%). Because these votes are excluded before seats are
allocated, Labour won an outright majority (65 of 120 seats) on 50.0% of the vote. In 2023 it fell to 5.5%. [1][4]

### 4. The COVID-era protest vote, 2020–2023
2020 and 2023 saw a wave of anti-lockdown, anti-mandate and conspiracy-tinged parties:

| Election | Parties (party vote %) | Combined |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | Advance NZ (with NZ Public Party) 0.98, Outdoors 0.11 | ≈1.1% |
| 2023 | NZ Loyal 1.21, Freedoms NZ (Vision NZ + Outdoors & Freedom) 0.34, DemocracyNZ 0.24, New Nation 0.05 | ≈1.8% |

(Grouping is this archive's; parties such as ONE/NewZeal, New Conservatives and the Leighton Baker Party overlapped with
this movement on some issues but are counted separately.) The movement fragmented rather than consolidated: Freedoms NZ
failed to bring in DemocracyNZ or the Leighton Baker Party, DemocracyNZ lost candidates and donors to NZ First, and NZ
Loyal split between Liz Gunn and Kelvyn Alp. Seven parties deregistered between the 2023 and 2026 elections — DemocracyNZ,
Leighton Baker Party, NZ Loyal (since re-registered), Democratic Alliance, New Nation, Freedoms NZ and NewZeal — and
NewZeal's leader Alfred Ngaro joined NZ First, which itself returned to Parliament in 2023 with 6.09%. [11][12][13][2]

### 5. The Christian-conservative lane
Christian and socially conservative parties have repeatedly split their vote: in 2020 New Conservative (1.48%), ONE
(0.28%) and Vision NZ (0.15%) together took ≈1.9%; in 2023 NewZeal (0.57%), New Conservatives (0.16%), the Leighton Baker
Party (0.07%) and Vision NZ (inside Freedoms NZ) again fell far short. [1][14]

### 6. The durable fringe
Some parties persist regardless of results: the Legalise Cannabis Party has polled 0.31–0.52% at every election since
2011; Social Credit stood until its leader's death in 2023; the Alliance re-registered in 2026 after an 11-year gap with
the backing of the Maritime Union. [15][16]

## 2026 outlook

- **17 registered parties** are contesting the party vote: the six parliamentary parties plus Alliance, Animal Justice,
  Legalise Cannabis, Conservative Party NZ, Free Palestine, NZ Loyal, Opportunity, NZ Outdoors & Freedom, Te Tai Tokerau,
  Vision NZ and Women's Rights. Three (Free Palestine, Te Tai Tokerau Party and NZ Loyal) were registered on 5 August
  2026, the same day the Alliance's re-registration took effect. [2][3]
- **Opportunity (TOP)** is the only minor party with a realistic chance of crossing 5%: it peaked at 9.5% (Roy Morgan,
  August) and polled 4.8–8% in October 2026, qualified for TVNZ's multi-party debate, and has been ruled out as a partner
  by National (27 July). See the [TOP profile](opportunity/README.md). [17][18]
- **Te Tai Tokerau Party** could win a seat through the electorate route if Kapa-Kingi holds Te Tai Tokerau. [19]
- Broadcasting money: of the minor parties, Opportunity received the largest allocation ($118,755 amended), then NZ
  Loyal ($86,306); "Change New Zealand" and "People's Party NZ Inc." were provisionally allocated $78,887 each but did not
  register and received nothing. [2]
