## 1. Summary

Te Pāti Māori is the only party built entirely around Māori political self-determination. Its career has had three
phases:

1. **Founding and early years (2004–08).** Tariana Turia left Labour over the Foreshore and Seabed Act 2004 and won the
   by-election that followed. The party won 4 Māori seats in 2005.
2. **Inside the tent with National (2008–17).** The party had confidence-and-supply "relationship accords" and
   ministers outside Cabinet: Turia, Pita Sharples and Te Ururoa Flavell. It won Whānau Ora, the replacement of the
   2004 Act, support for UNDRIP, a constitutional review, tobacco control and a new Māori language law. Its vote fell
   steadily (2.39% → 1.43% → 1.32% → 1.18%) and it lost all its seats in 2017. Metro and others called this a backlash
   against propping up National.
3. **A more radical revival (2020–26).** Under Ngarewa-Packer and Waititi the party returned in 2020 and won a record
   6 seats in 2023. In opposition it has relied on protest, from the Treaty Principles Bill haka to nationwide hīkoi.
   It has also faced serious problems:
   - Parliament's harshest-ever privileges suspensions (2025);
   - repeated questions about party finances and data use linked to organisations led by its president;
   - a 2025 civil war that ended with two MPs expelled. The High Court later found one expulsion unlawful.

### Timeline
- **2004**: Turia resigns from Labour and Parliament over the Foreshore and Seabed Act. She wins the July by-election
  for the new party.
- **2005**: 2.12%, 4 electorates (Turia, Sharples, Hone Harawira, Flavell), creating a 1-seat overhang. The party
  stays in opposition.
- **2008**: 2.39%, 5 electorates (Rahui Katene wins Te Tai Tonga), creating a 2-seat overhang. Confidence and supply
  agreement with National (16 November 2008). Sharples becomes Minister of Māori Affairs; Turia becomes Minister for the
  Community and Voluntary Sector and later Minister for Whānau Ora.
- **2011**: Harawira leaves the party in February after clashing over its support for National and for the
  replacement foreshore law, and forms Mana. The party
  falls to 1.43% and 3 seats, with a 1-seat overhang, and signs a second agreement with National.
- **2014**: Turia and Sharples retire. 1.32%: Flavell holds Waiariki and Marama Fox enters as the party's first list
  MP. Third agreement; Flavell becomes Minister for Māori Development and for Whānau Ora.
- **2017**: Electoral pact with Mana: the party stands aside in Te Tai Tokerau. **Labour wins all 7 Māori seats.** The
  Māori Party gets 1.18% and is out of Parliament. Fox says Māori had "gone back like a beaten wife to the abuser".
- **2020**: Tamihere and Ngarewa-Packer become co-leaders. Waititi wins Waiariki by 836 votes. On 1.17%, the party
  gets 2 MPs. Waititi replaces Tamihere as co-leader.
- **2020–23**:
  - the party is referred to Police over late donation disclosures (see §5);
  - it walks out over speaking rights and calls for "divorce" from the monarchy;
  - it petitions to rename the country Aotearoa (70,000+ signatures);
  - Meka Whaitiri defects from Labour (May 2023); the co-leaders are ejected for the haka welcoming her;
  - name change to Te Pāti Māori (July 2023).
- **2023**: **3.08%, 6 electorates** (Waiariki, Te Tai Hauāuru, Hauraki-Waikato, Tāmaki Makaurau, Te Tai Tokerau, Te
  Tai Tonga), creating a 2-seat overhang (123 MPs). This is the party's best result. **Hana-Rawhiti Maipi-Clarke**, aged
  21, unseats Nanaia Mahuta. Takutai Tarsh Kemp wins Tāmaki Makaurau by 42 votes. Whaitiri loses Ikaroa-Rāwhiti.
- **2023–26**: in opposition (§4–§6).

## 3. The 2008–2017 support deals: what the party gained

The 2008 confidence-and-supply agreement, announced 16 November 2008, committed National to:
- set up a group to consider constitutional issues, including Māori representation;
- not remove the Māori seats "without the consent of the Māori people" (entrenchment was shelved);
- review the Foreshore and Seabed Act and repeal it if necessary;
- appoint Sharples and Turia as ministers outside Cabinet.

What followed across the three terms:

| Gain | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Whānau Ora** | 2010 (expanded 2011, 2014) | Delivered. It became the party's signature programme, with three commissioning agencies from 2014. Contracts were re-tendered in 2025. |
| **UNDRIP endorsed** | 20 April 2010 | Delivered, as a non-binding statement, announced by Sharples at the UN |
| **Foreshore and Seabed Act repealed** and replaced by the Marine and Coastal Area (Takutai Moana) Act 2011 | 2011 | Delivered as a compromise. Turia called it "drafted by politics, rather than what is fair and moral". Harawira quit over it. The 2025 coalition amendment tightened it again. |
| **Constitutional review** | from 2011 | Delivered as a process; no major constitutional change followed |
| **Smokefree 2025**, tobacco excise rises, plain packaging (Turia) | 2011 onward | Delivered |
| **Te Ture mō Te Reo Māori 2016** (Te Mātāwai) | 2016 | Delivered under Flavell |
| **Te Ture Whenua Māori land reform** | 2016–17 | **Not passed**; withdrawn by Labour in 2017 |
| Recognition of the NZ Wars (day of commemoration from 2017) | 2016–17 | Fox credited Flavell with this, and with work towards the pardon of Rua Kenana |

**What it cost.** The party gave confidence and supply to a government that:
- raised GST to 15% (2010);
- sold minority stakes in state power companies (67.3% opposed the sales in the 2013 citizens-initiated
  referendum);
- refused to entrench the Māori seats.

The party lost Harawira and the left of its base to Mana in 2011, and every election after 2008 brought a lower
vote. Labour's 2005 campaign line was that "a vote for the Māori Party is a vote for National". After nine years in
support of National, the party was wiped out in 2017.

## 4. Opposition, 2020–2026: protest politics

- **5 December 2023**: "National Māori Action Day" protests at the opening of Parliament. The party's MPs modify
  their oaths to refer to Te Tiriti.
- **December 2023**: Maipi-Clarke's maiden speech, which attracted wide attention. She said the new government had
  "attacked my whole world from every corner".
- **30 May 2024**: "Toitū Te Tiriti" national day of action on Budget day. The party calls for a Māori parliament.
- **14 November 2024**: at the first reading of ACT's **Treaty Principles Bill**, Maipi-Clarke tears up the bill and
  leads a haka, joined by both co-leaders and Labour's Peeni Henare. The Hīkoi mō te Tiriti reaches Parliament later
  that month ([background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C4%ABkoi_m%C5%8D_te_Tiriti)).
- **May–June 2025**: the Privileges Committee finds Maipi-Clarke, Waititi and Ngarewa-Packer in contempt for
  "acting in a manner that could have the effect of intimidating a member". It recommends suspensions of **7 days**
  (Maipi-Clarke) and **21 days** (each co-leader), the harshest it has ever proposed. The MPs had boycotted the
  hearing, calling it a "kangaroo court". Parliament imposed the suspensions on **5 June 2025** on a party-line vote.
  Labour agreed the rules were broken but proposed 24 hours.
- **February 2025**: Tākuta Ferris is found to have deliberately misled the House and told to apologise, after
  calling other MPs liars.
- **6 September 2025**: **Tāmaki Makaurau by-election**, after Kemp's death on 26 June 2025. **Oriini Kaipara** wins
  with 6,948 votes to Henare's 3,429. National, ACT, NZ First and the Greens did not stand.
- **October 2025**: Ferris and Ngarewa-Packer film themselves burning a copy of the new takutai moana amendment on
  Parliament's grounds.
- **September 2026**: Ngarewa-Packer is ordered out of the House for using an obscenity in an exchange with the
  Assistant Speaker.

## 5. Governance, finance and data controversies

- **2020 donations.** In April 2021 the Electoral Commission referred the party to Police for not disclosing about
  $320,000 of donations in time. These included $158,224 from Tamihere and $48,880 from the National Urban Māori
  Authority (NUMA). Police referred the case to the SFO, which **closed it in 2022 without prosecution**.
- **Charities and campaigns (2022).** Charities Services examined a $385,307 loan from the Te Whānau o Waipareira
  Trust group and $82,695 from NUMA towards Tamihere's 2020 campaign. Tamihere leads both organisations, and charities
  may not fund parties. Tamihere called the inquiry discrimination and the party boycotted the NZ Herald over its
  coverage.
- **Census and vaccination data (2023 election).** In 2024 former Manurewa Marae staff alleged that 2023 Census forms
  had been copied and the data used to target voters, and that people were given vouchers to switch to the Māori roll.
  Labour separately complained that texts from a Waipareira-registered short code urged people to vote for the party.
  The party denied wrongdoing. The PM ordered a Public Service Commission inquiry. Outcomes so far:
  - Stats NZ cleared the Whānau Ora Commissioning Agency (January 2025);
  - Police and the SFO **stopped their criminal inquiry for lack of evidence** (October 2025);
  - privacy inquiries were continuing at last report.
  Rawiri Taonui and Radio Waatea disputed the allegations and linked the whistleblowers to Destiny Church.
- **Whānau Ora money and electioneering (2025).** Te Puni Kōkiri reviewed whether the Whānau Ora Commissioning Agency
  (Te Pou Matakana) had misused funding on a campaign urging Māori to join the Māori roll. The agency had already lost
  its contract in a 2025 re-tender.
- **2023 policy apology.** The party apologised to migrant and refugee communities for "harmful narratives" on its
  website. These included an "Indigenous first" housing plank that would have curbed immigration. It also deleted a
  2020 sports-policy claim that "Māori genetic makeup is stronger than others"; Waititi denied the claim was racist.

## 6. The 2025–26 internal crisis

- **July 2025**: according to Tamihere, MPs Mariameno Kapa-Kingi (Te Tai Tokerau) and Tākuta Ferris (Te Tai Tonga)
  plotted to replace the co-leaders.
- **September 2025**: Ferris posts criticism of non-Māori Labour volunteers during the by-election. The party
  apologises for the post but Ferris refuses to. Kapa-Kingi is removed as whip.
- **October 2025**: Toitū Te Tiriti, led by Kapa-Kingi's son Eru, cuts ties with the party, alleging bullying and
  authoritarian leadership. A party email to members accuses Kapa-Kingi of a $133,000 office overspend and unpaid
  staff, and Eru of abusing a security guard. Founding member Amokura Panoho calls for Tamihere to resign. The
  co-leaders announce a "reset".
- **3 November 2025**: Tamihere publicly accuses the two MPs of "greed, avarice, and entitlement" and tells them to
  quit. The National Iwi Chairs Forum tries to mediate.
- **9 November 2025**: the national council **expels Kapa-Kingi and Ferris**. Former co-leader Flavell and a law
  expert question the process. Hone Harawira calls for reinstatement.
- **5 December 2025**: the High Court gives Kapa-Kingi interim reinstatement. On **10 March 2026** Justice Radich rules
  that her suspension and expulsion **breached the party's own rules and were unlawful**. The party says it will
  comply.
- **2026**: the Te Tai Tokerau and Te Tai Tonga electorate committees resign in support of the two MPs. Ferris
  will stand as an **independent** in Te Tai Tonga (from March 2026). Kapa-Kingi leaves and forms the **Te Tai Tokerau
  Party** (May 2026; registered August 2026). At Waitangi in February 2026 a confrontation between Eru Kapa-Kingi and
  party supporters ended any hope of reconciliation.

The crisis matters for voters because it splits the vote in two Māori seats, and because it raises questions about
how the party is run, given that it campaigns on mana motuhake and tikanga.

## 9. 2026 polling

Te Pāti Māori is polling **0.4%–2.2%** in party-vote surveys from August to October 2026. Examples: 1News–Verian 0.7%
(15–19 October); Freshwater Strategy 2.0% (9–19 October); RNZ–Reid Research 2.0%. That is below its 2023 result, but
it is also what the party's own strategy aims for: Te Pāti Māori is asking supporters to give their party vote to
Labour or the Greens. Its seat count depends on the Māori electorates, which are not covered by national polls. If it
wins more electorates than its party vote entitles it to, an **overhang** follows, as in 2023. Full series:
[`data/polls-2026.json`](../../data/polls-2026.json).

## 10. 2026 pitch and headline policies

The campaign was launched in Gisborne on 16 August 2026. It is built on getting Māori onto the Māori roll and on
strategic voting to remove the coalition. Headline commitments:

- **Constitution and Te Tiriti**:
  - an independent **Te Tiriti Commission** to investigate Crown breaches;
  - **binding Waitangi Tribunal recommendations**;
  - a 2040 target for constitutional reform and a $200m **Mātike Mai** fund;
  - implement UNDRIP;
  - state accountability for te reo Māori.

  In February 2025 the party had demanded a Te Tiriti commissioner with veto power as a coalition "bottom line".
- **Justice**: **abolish prisons by 2040** and move to restorative justice.
- **Tax ("Kiwi Tax Plan")**:
  - first $30,000 tax-free (a rebate) and no GST on kai;
  - 48% top rate above $300,000;
  - a wealth tax starting at 1.5%;
  - a higher company tax rate and a 5% tax on profits sent offshore;
  - land-banking and vacant-home taxes, and a 5% stamp duty on residential sales.
- **Economy and kai**: Crown land and $100m for Māori supermarket collectives; support for the Māori economy.
- **Energy**: a $1b energy-sovereignty fund for Māori and community-owned renewables.
- **Environment**: ban seabed mining; assert tino rangatiratanga over freshwater.
- **Social services**:
  - a Māori Education Authority;
  - whānau-led care for Māori children;
  - Māori-led disability services;
  - a $200m Takatāpui Commission.
- **Foreign policy**: declare New Zealand "military neutral"; focus on the Pacific.

The full list is in [./policies/README.md](./policies/README.md).

## 11. Coalition stance

The party ruled out a right-wing coalition in 2022 and has kept to that. In 2026 it is campaigning openly for a
Labour–Green–Te Pāti Māori change of government. Labour, its main rival in the Māori seats, has rejected several key
policies:
- Hipkins ruled out a Te Tiriti commissioner able to overturn laws (February 2025);
- Labour is "dead-set against" abolishing prisons;
- Labour has ruled out the Te Tiriti Commission and Mātike Mai (September 2026).

The Greens say the constitutional package fits their own Te Tiriti policies. ACT and NZ First call it divisive.
