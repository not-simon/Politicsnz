# Te Pāti Māori: track record, 2008–2026

> How reliable has the Māori Party / Te Pāti Māori been? What did it win inside National-led governments, what has it
> done in opposition since 2020, and do its actions match its kaupapa? Current to 23 October 2026.

## How to read this

The status labels are the same as in the other party files:
- **kept**: delivered substantially as promised;
- **partially-kept**: delivered in a smaller or later form;
- **broken**: not delivered, or the party acted against the commitment;
- **abandoned**: the party dropped the commitment;
- **not-yet-due**: a 2026 pledge.

"Broken" means not delivered, whatever the reason; each entry's `detail` explains why. Te Pāti Māori has never
held more than 5 of about 120 seats. Its only route to delivery was bargaining as a support partner (2008–17) or
pressure from opposition (2020–26).

## 2. Term by term

### 2008–2011: first confidence & supply agreement (5 MPs, 2 ministers outside Cabinet)
- **Won:** National dropped its policy of abolishing the Māori seats (it would not remove them "without the consent of
  the Māori people"). Also won: a constitutional review, a review and then repeal of the Foreshore and Seabed Act, Whānau
  Ora (2010), and New Zealand's support for **UNDRIP** (2010). Sharples was Minister of Māori Affairs and Turia was
  Associate Health Minister, with the tobacco portfolio.
- **Gave up:** entrenchment of the Māori seats. The party also accepted a replacement foreshore law, the Marine and
  Coastal Area Act 2011, that Turia herself called "drafted by politics". Hone Harawira's departure (February 2011) and
  the founding of Mana split the movement.
- **Assessment:** the most productive term in the party's history. The party kept its word to National and won real
  concessions, but the compromise on the issue it was founded over cost it unity.

### 2011–2014: second agreement (3 MPs)
- Whānau Ora became the "cornerstone" of the 2011 agreement. Turia drove the **Smokefree 2025** goal (2011), with
  repeated tobacco excise rises and plain packaging.
- The party kept supporting a government that sold minority stakes in state power companies; 67.3% voted against the
  sales in the 2013 referendum. Its party vote fell to 1.43%.

### 2014–2017: third agreement (2 MPs; Flavell Minister for Māori Development and Whānau Ora)
- **Delivered:** Te Ture mō Te Reo Māori 2016 (Te Mātāwai), enacted in both te reo Māori and English. Work towards
  recognising the New Zealand Wars; the national commemoration day began in 2017.
- **Not delivered:** the Te Ture Whenua Māori land reform bill. It had not passed by the 2017 election and was
  withdrawn by the incoming Labour-led government.
- **Result:** wiped out in 2017. The Mana pact failed, and Labour took all seven Māori seats.

### 2017–2020: outside Parliament
The senior leadership resigned, and the party rebuilt under new leaders and president Che Wilson. In 2020 Wilson
said any future deal with National would have to be "a big deal".

### 2020–2023: opposition (2 MPs)
- Waititi won Waiariki by 836 votes. The party campaigned for a Māori Health Authority; Labour created **Te Aka Whai
  Ora** in 2022, and the coalition abolished it in February 2024.
- **Integrity lapse:** the 2020 donations were disclosed late (about $320,000). The case was referred to Police, then
  the SFO, and closed without charges in 2022.
- **Policy U-turns:** in 2023 the party apologised for "harmful narratives" about migrants and removed an "Indigenous
  first" immigration curb from its housing policy. It also deleted a 2020 claim that Māori genetics are "stronger".
- **Consistency:** voted with the Greens and some Labour MPs for Swarbrick's alcohol harm bill (2023). Supported the
  Palestinian self-determination motion (2021).

### 2023–2026: opposition (6 MPs, then 4)
- **Votes:** consistently opposed the coalition on the Treaty Principles Bill, Māori wards referendums, the
  takutai moana amendment, the Clean Car Discount repeal, three strikes and the Electoral Amendment Act 2025.
- **Protest:** the Treaty Principles haka (November 2024) led to the harshest privileges punishment on record (suspensions
  of 7 and 21 days, imposed 5 June 2025). The party boycotted the hearing.
- **Electoral success:** held Tāmaki Makaurau at the 2025 by-election by a big margin (6,948 to 3,429).
- **Breakdown:** the expulsions of Kapa-Kingi and Ferris (November 2025). The High Court ruled the expulsion of
  Kapa-Kingi **unlawful** under the party's own rules (March 2026). Two electorate organisations walked out.

## 4. Did it stick to its word?

- **To its partners, 2008–17: yes.** It honoured three confidence-and-supply agreements in full and never brought down
  a government. That reliability had a cost: voters punished the party for propping up National.
- **On its founding kaupapa: partly.** It got the 2004 Act repealed, but accepted a replacement that its own founder
  criticised. That replacement has now been tightened by a later government.
- **On coalition choices since 2020: yes.** It ruled out the right in 2022 and has campaigned consistently for a
  Labour–Green–Te Pāti Māori government.
- **On electoral announcements: not always.** In April 2025 it said it would stand in general seats. By October 2026
  it was standing only in Māori electorates. The "bottom line" Te Tiriti commissioner with veto power (February 2025)
  became a broader 2026 constitutional package. We have no source on whether the veto is still a bottom line.
- **On party rules and governance: poorly.** The High Court found the party breached its own kawa in expelling
  Kapa-Kingi. Questions about funding links to organisations run by its president keep coming back: the 2020
  donations, Charities Services in 2022, Whānau Ora advertising in 2025, and the census and vaccination-data
  allegations. Formal outcomes have mostly been no charges: the SFO closed the donations case, Police and the SFO
  stopped the census inquiry, and Stats NZ cleared the Whānau Ora Commissioning Agency. Privacy inquiries were
  continuing at last report.

## 6. Trends

1. **From inside to outside.** The party went from three terms as a quiet support partner to the National-led
   governments, which gave it concessions but cost it voters, to loud opposition (2020–26), which has brought record
   seats but no policy wins.
2. **Electorate seats are what count, and the party vote is optional.** Its party vote has never been above 3.1%.
   Its seat count comes from the Māori electorates. In 2026 it has formalised this with a one-name list and a call for
   supporters to party-vote Labour or Green.
3. **Each split is followed by a slump.** Harawira's 2011 exit was followed by falling votes in 2011–17. The 2025
   expulsions now mean former MPs are running against the party in two seats.
4. **Leadership is closely tied to its president's organisations.** Tamihere, president since 2022, also leads the
   Waipareira Trust and NUMA, and is closely linked to the Whānau Ora Commissioning Agency. Many of the party's
   controversies trace back to this. None has resulted in charges.
5. **Policy has stayed consistent.** The 2023 and 2026 tax plans are almost identical: zero tax under $30,000, 48%
   over $300,000, wealth tax, GST off kai. So are the constitutional demands. The party is more predictable on policy
   than on organisation.
