Te Tiriti
Entrenchment
Te Tiriti o Waitangi is the constitutional
foundation of Aotearoa. It is not a creation of
Parliament, and its authority does not depend
on Parliament’s permission.
Successive governments have nevertheless treated
Crown obligations under Te Tiriti as politically
discretionary, able to be weakened, removed or
ignored by the Government of the day.
Te Pāti Māori will change that by creating durable
and enforceable minimum obligations on the Crown,
strengthening remedies when breaches occur, and
resourcing Māori-led constitutional transformation.
The purpose is to entrench the Crown’s
conduct, not to entrench a Crown version of
Te Tiriti.
The legislation will not constitute, define, replace,
limit or domesticate Te Tiriti. It will bind Ministers,
departments and Crown agencies to obligations that
arise from Te Tiriti itself.

He Whakaputanga o te Rangatiratanga o Nu
Tireni, signed in 1835, affirmed the mana
and rangatiratanga of the rangatira and hapū
of Aotearoa before Te Tiriti o Waitangi was
signed. It declared that sovereign authority
resided with the rangatira and hapū and
could not simply be assumed by another
authority.
Te Tiriti o Waitangi followed in 1840. It did not
extinguish that authority. Te Tiriti established
a relationship between continuing Māori
authority and the authority exercised by the
Crown.
Te Tiriti o Waitangi precedes New Zealand’s
parliamentary system. It established an
enduring relationship between Māori
authority and Crown authority. As a
consequence, legislation cannot be the
source of Te Tiriti o Waitangi’s mana.
The problem is that Crown institutions have
too often acted as though the Crown can
decide when, how and to what extent its Te
Tiriti obligations apply.
Māori should not continually have to take the
Crown to court to make it honour Te Tiriti.
Nor should Te Tiriti protections depend on
the political preferences of the Government
of the day.
Mātike Mai Aotearoa has already undertaken
foundational Māori-led constitutional work.
The Waitangi Tribunal has established
an extensive record of Crown breaches.
Aotearoa has endorsed the United Nations
Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous
Peoples. What is missing is enforceable
accountability and the resources to advance
the next stage.

• We acknowledge He Whakaputanga o
te Rangatiratanga o Nu Tireni as the
foundational assertion of the mana
and rangatiratanga of Māori and the
constitutional authority of rangatira
and hapū.
• Te Tiriti did not create Māori authority. It
recognised a relationship with authority
that already existed.
• Te Tiriti o Waitangi has authority in its own
right. Parliament does not give Te Tiriti
its mana.
• Tino rangatiratanga is an authority, not an
advisory interest.
• Legislation should bind Crown conduct
to Te Tiriti, not bring Te Tiriti under
Parliamentary control.
• Te Tiriti o Waitangi in te reo Māori will
be the primary reference point for
Crown obligations.
• The Crown must be legally accountable
when it breaches its Te Tiriti obligations.
• Constitutional transformation must be led
by iwi, hapū and Māori, not imposed by
political parties.
• Te Pāti Māori’s role is a mahi function, not
a mana function. Our role is to resource
the people to determine the constitutional
future for themselves.

Te Pāti Māori will...
1. • Establish a Mātike Mai Fund
Establish a dedicated Mātike Mai Fund
of $220 million over four years to
independently resource the next stage of
constitutional transformation.
The Fund will:
1. • establish and resource an independent
Mātike Mai secretariat;
2. • fund iwi, hapū and Māori-led constitutional
kōrero across every rohe;
3. • resource the further independent working
group envisaged by Mātike Mai;
4. • support nationwide constitutional
engagement, including rangatahi
participation;
5. • convene a Māori Constitutional Convention
and begin formal dialogue with tangata
Tiriti, local government and the Crown; and
6. • progress toward a national Tiriti
Convention.
Te Pāti Māori will establish 2040 as the target
for constitutional transformation in Aotearoa.
2. • Implement the United Nations
Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous
Peoples
Within the first 100 days, Te Pāti Māori will
restore and resource development of a
national action plan to implement the United
Nations Declaration on the Rights
of Indigenous Peoples.
Aotearoa has already endorsed UNDRIP.
What is missing is a domestic implementation
framework. The plan will identify the
legislative, policy and institutional changes
required to give practical effect to Indigenous
rights, with meaningful and independent
Māori participation.
UNDRIP will complement, not replace,
Te Tiriti.
3. • Establish an Independent Te Tiriti
Commission
Establish an independent Te Tiriti
Commission as an Officer of Parliament,
with expertise in Te Tiriti, tikanga Māori,
constitutional law and Māori-Crown relations.
The Commission will not define Te Tiriti or
become the source of its meaning. Its role
is to independently assess and enforce the
Crown’s minimum statutory obligations
arising from Te Tiriti.
Its governance and appointment
arrangements will include meaningful Māori
participation and protect the Commission
from control by the Government of the day.
The Commission will operate independently
of the Government of the day and will have
statutory powers to:
1. • investigate Crown decisions, policy and
Executive action for Te Tiriti compliance;
2. • require information and evidence from
Ministers, departments and Crown
agencies;
3. • report directly to Parliament on breaches
and systemic failures;
4. • require remedial action where Crown
processes fail to meet Te Tiriti obligations;
5. • stop or suspend Executive decisions where
a serious breach is found; and
6. • create Te Tiriti Compliance Orders
Where the Commission finds that an
Executive decision seriously breaches
statutory Te Tiriti obligations, it may issue
a Te Tiriti Compliance Order stopping or
suspending that decision.
If the Crown disagrees, it must apply to the
High Court to have the order overturned.
Until the Court determines otherwise, the
order remains in force.
This reverses the burden of accountability:
where a serious breach is found, the Crown
must justify why it should be permitted to
proceed.

The Commission will not invalidate an Act of
Parliament. It may, however, assess proposed
legislation and report directly to Parliament
where a Bill is inconsistent with Te Tiriti.
4. • Make Waitangi Tribunal
recommendations binding
Amend the Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 so
that Waitangi Tribunal recommendations
are binding on the Crown, with clear
implementation timeframes, reporting
requirements and judicial enforcement
where necessary.
The two institutions will have complementary
roles:
• The Waitangi Tribunal will investigate
breaches and provide binding remedies.
• The Te Tiriti Commission will scrutinise
Executive action and seek to prevent
serious breaches before they occur.
Together, they make the Crown accountable
for remedying breaches and for preventing
new ones.
Our Commitment
Te Pāti Māori will move Crown responsibility
under Te Tiriti from political discretion to
enforceable legal obligation.
We will not ask Parliament to give Te Tiriti
its authority, define its limits, or make itself
the owner of its meaning. Parliament did not
create Te Tiriti.
Instead, we will use legislation to bind Crown
conduct to obligations arising from Te Tiriti,
establish independent mechanisms to bring
the Crown to account, and resource Māori to
lead the constitutional transformation that
comes next.
This legislation binds the Crown. It does not
bind Te Tiriti.
