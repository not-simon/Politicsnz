# New Zealand First: track record, 2011–2026

This page covers promises against delivery, reliability, how NZ First's actions lined up with its stated policies (including its role in blocking or permitting other parties' policies), and trends. Each entry, with its sources, is in [`data/promises/nz-first.json`](../../data/promises/nz-first.json). The party profile is in [README.md](./README.md) and 2026 policies are in [policies/README.md](./policies/README.md).

## How to read this

The status definitions are the same as for every party in this archive:
- **kept**: delivered substantially as promised.
- **partially-kept**: some of it delivered, or delivered late or watered down.
- **broken**: not delivered, whatever the reason.
- **abandoned**: NZ First itself dropped or traded away the commitment, usually in coalition talks.
- **in-progress**: underway but not finished.
- **not-yet-due**: a 2026 promise.

NZ First was in opposition from 2011 to 2017, in coalition with Labour from 2017 to 2020, out of Parliament from 2020 to 2023, and in coalition with National from 2023. The sample covers its headline and coalition-agreement promises; it is not every manifesto line. The official coalition-agreement PDFs could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so agreement contents come from contemporary reporting.

**Summary.**
- NZ First's pattern is distinctive. **What makes it into a coalition agreement usually gets delivered**: the PGF and the RIF, the minimum wage, waka-jumping, Pike River, super at 65, English as an official language, Māori-ward referendums, the COVID inquiry, and oil and gas exploration.
- Its **most prominent campaign promises are often traded away**. In 2017 that meant the Māori-seats and 100-MP referendums, the anti-smacking repeal and the 10,000 immigration cap. In 2023 it meant designating gangs as terrorists, withdrawing from UNDRIP and moving the port to Northport.
- Several of those traded promises reappear at the next election: the Māori-seats referendum (2017 → 2026) and Kiwi bank ownership (2017 → 2026).
- Delivery in this term was also weakened by **missed targets**, notably 500 police by November 2025, and one **self-reversal**: Fees Free was moved to the final year in 2025, then scrapped in Budget 2026.

## Alignment: actions vs stated policies, and the kingmaker role

**Consistent with its brand:**
- **Seniors and super.** NZ First secured the SuperGold Card (2005–08) and kept the super age at 65 in 2023. This is its most consistent and reliably delivered commitment.
- **Regions.** It delivered the PGF ($3b, 2017–20) and the RIF ($1.2b, 2023–26) and pushed Northland projects. The money was delivered, but the quality of spending was questioned (Auditor-General 2020 and 2023), and RIF payouts have been slow.
- **Law and order.** It blocked Labour's planned repeal of three strikes in 2018, then helped reinstate the law in 2024 and backed the gang laws.
- **Anti-co-governance and national identity** in 2023–26: English language law, English names, Māori-ward polls, the Treaty-clauses review, abolishing Te Aka Whai Ora. Critics, including Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and the Waitangi Tribunal, describe these as anti-Māori. The actions do match NZ First's campaign.
- **Tax.** It opposed a capital gains tax and in effect vetoed one in 2019.

**Where actions diverged from words:**
- **1996.** NZ First campaigned against National, then formed a coalition with it. Wikipedia's account of the 1999 election, when the party fell to 4.3%, says some voters "had not forgiven Peters for forming a coalition with National after being led to believe that a vote for him would help get rid of National".
- **2005.** Peters spoke of avoiding the "baubles of office", then became Foreign Minister.
- **Regulatory Standards Act (2025).** NZ First voted it through all its stages, then pledged a week after the third reading to repeal it, saying it had only ever supported it because of ACT's deal. ACT called this bad faith.
- **Oil and gas.** NZ First was part of the 2018 government that banned new offshore exploration, then repealed the ban in 2025.
- **Public Service Act DEI provisions.** NZ First helped pass the Public Service Act in 2020, then in 2025 introduced a bill to remove its diversity provisions.
- **Fees Free.** It won the move of Fees Free to the final year (2025), then agreed to scrap it in Budget 2026.

**Blocking and permitting other parties' policies:**
- **2017–20, as a brake on Labour.** NZ First blocked or diluted:
  - the capital gains tax (2019);
  - the three-strikes repeal (2018);
  - Labour's water levy (2017);
  - the clean-car feebate and clean-car standard (2020; later enacted by Labour alone in 2021);
  - the Auckland light-rail process (June 2020).
  It forced a referendum on End of Life Choice but failed to get one on abortion. It supported the 2019 firearms ban; analysts cited by Wikipedia link that vote to gun owners moving to ACT in 2020 (see the [ACT profile](../act/README.md)).
- **2023–26, as a brake on ACT and National.**
  - It allowed the Treaty Principles Bill only through first reading, then voted it down.
  - It invoked "agree to disagree" over the COVID inquiry scope, residency pathways, the India FTA and the under-16 social media ban.
  - It opposed the India FTA legislation, which passed with Labour's votes.
  - It opposed the Gene Technology Bill unless changed.

## Trends (2011 → 2026)

1. **Boom-and-bust results.** 6.59% (2011), 8.66% (2014), 7.20% (2017), 2.60% (2020, no seats), 6.08% (2023), then polling 7.5–15% in 2026. Each time NZ First has left a coalition (1998–99, 2008, 2020) its support has collapsed. It recovered in opposition each time.
2. **It goes with either side.** It enabled National in 1996, Labour in 2005 and 2017, and National in 2023. In 2026 the choice has narrowed: NZ First and Labour have ruled each other out, so NZ First has positioned itself firmly on the right bloc.
3. **The same promises keep returning.** The Māori-seats referendum, Kiwi bank ownership, immigration limits and Northport and Marsden Point development have featured across 2014, 2017, 2023 and 2026. They are often traded away in coalition talks and then revived.
4. **Increasingly a culture-war platform.** In 2017 its flagship items were economic and regional (PGF, minimum wage, trees). In 2023–26 they shifted towards identity and culture: English language, gender, co-governance, citizenship and voting, the Waitangi Tribunal. Cost-of-living interventionism has been retained (power-company and supermarket break-ups, BNZ).
5. **Delivery capacity.** Its regional funds are delivered in name but slowly or contentiously in practice (Auditor-General findings on the PGF; RIF only about 14% paid out of $1.07b committed by July 2026). Targets such as one billion trees, 1,800 police and 500 police have each been missed or redefined.
