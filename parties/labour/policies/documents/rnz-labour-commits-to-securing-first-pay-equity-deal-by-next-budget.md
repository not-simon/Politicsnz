## Labour commits to securing first pay equity deal by next Budget

Russell Palmer

Photo credit:RNZ / Nick Monro

While the government is hailing a bigger surplus as evidence its plan is working, not all the numbers are so rosy and the opposition is warning of more cuts to come.

Both major parties will have explaining to do, with spending cuts built into the projections and Labour yet to explain how it will pay for its promises.

The party's Finance spokesperson Barbara Edmonds has also added to leader Chris Hipkins' commitment to pass pay equity legislation within 100 days of being elected, promising the first deal - with care and support workers - would be done by her first Budget.

The Pre-election Economic and Fiscal Update (Prefu) opened the government's books for the first time since the Budget in May and revealed improved numbers.

However, economists and business groups have warned the improvements mask some deeper challenges.

#### Labour promises pay equity deal done by first Budget

Edmonds pledged the first pay equity deal under the restored legislation would be done by the time of her first Budget.

"That's my commitment," she said.

"I want to be able to settle this by my first Budget for that care and support workers deal, especially because that's what the unions are saying should be done first.

"We might have to do a mini-Budget anyway because of just the other commitments that we might have, but obviously we'll probably have to set up a contingency for it given there is none."

She refused to accept that could impact the government's ability to negotiate.

"No. What's hamstringing is that our working women have less pay in their pocket over the last three years."

She fended off repeated questions about how much the party would set aside for restoring pay equity deals under the previous settings, saying her fiscal plan setting out the contingency funding would be coming "very soon" and the figure would be "sufficient".

RNZ / Nick Monro

Hipkins, speaking in Auckland after a speech to the EMA, said the Prefu was "the verdict" on National's track record with government debt, unemployment, and inflation staying higher for longer, and a return to surplus still forecast later than the update prior to the last election.

Both stood by the modelling for their capital gains tax in spite of weaker house price growth, saying revenue was still expected to be higher than when Labour did the costings.

"We deliberately costed it conservatively," Hipkins said.

#### 'Our plan is working' - National

National leader Christopher Luxon was touting the improved numbers, but also appeared to back off earlier commitments to changing the superannuation eligibility age.

He praised Finance Minister Nicola Willis' "responsible economic management".

"Our plan is genuinely working, it is working. We have been doing the right things from day one, under Nicola's leadership. She's done an exceptionally good job, there is no better finance minister in this country, and she's going to go down as one of the greatest."

However, having previously said the party would campaign on raising the age from 65 at this year's election, he cast doubt on National's ability to secure changes to the superannuation age of eligibility.

Christopher Luxon speaks to media at The Borough Cafe in Tawa on 29 September 2026.

RNZ / Baz Macdonald

"If we do it, it would need to be in a very slow, considered, you know, giving people lots of advance notice and lots of time, but it also does require a degree of political consensus as well, and that's missing at the moment," he said.

Willis at this year's Budget railed against parties refusing to lift the age, saying they were "prepared to rob everyone in this country under the age of 50 for their own political expediency".

#### 'Hope is this government's plan' - Greens

Greens co-leader Chlöe Swarbrick brought back the prime minister's quote that "hope is not a plan" from the beginning of the fuel crisis, levelling it at the coalition.

"Unfortunately today it appears as though hope is this government's plan, as they sit on their hands and refuse to do anything to actually increase our resilience and improve New Zealanders' outlook," she told reporters.

RNZ / Samuel Rillstone

Swarbrick pointed to what she said was a missed opportunity for the government to decarbonise the economy and better prepare the country for price shocks when the Strait of Hormuz initially closed.

The Luxon government had driven fuel inflation higher, she said, pointing at 10 additional risks highlighted by Treasury in the Prefu - particularly $5b in committed but unfunded military spending.

The government had "very clearly" shifted the cost of the economic recovery to New Zealand, she said.

#### 'Vindicated' - Seymour

In Auckland, ACT leader David Seymour said the Prefu had "vindicated" his party's drive for savings.

"We have saved money in this government, but actually nowhere near enough. And as the bond market increases interest rates on government debt all around the world, we are going to have to go further and faster to balance the books with no new taxes."

He reiterated his call for fewer government departments, and reducing the number of public servants to 1 percent of the total population.

"The surplus being promised is two years later than was promised before the last election. To achieve that is going to require major reductions in the number of public servants and government departments, which I think we should do."

RNZ / Nick Monro

He said that could be achieved without affecting nurses, police officers or doctors, and also reiterated his call for fuel taxes to continue rising.

"You've got to collect enough money from your excise tax to pay for the roads. If you're not prepared to do that, you're not being honest."

#### Too early for tax cuts - economist

Independent economist Cameron Bagrie told Checkpoint that after repeated delays on reaching surplus, the Prefu was one of the first reports showing the goalposts a bit closer.

But it all relied on the government making further spending cuts of 2.5 percentage points over four to five years.

"If you strip out New Zealand superannuation and if you strip out interest costs which are going to go up as a share of GDP, then the belt tightening is 2.9 percent of GDP," he said.

With spending on health, defence and education expected to rise as a percentage of GDP, the cuts would have to come from core services, law and order, cultural services or superannuation, he said.

"Yeah... she's running pretty skinny over the next sort of few years."

Willis had at a recent Business NZ conference hinted National might campaign on more income tax cuts, but Bagrie said that should wait.

"Not at the moment ... once we get a little bit of money in the tin, hopefully, yeah, we should be able to tax adjust tax brackets and remove what's called the effect of fiscal drag. But that's tomorrow's story, it's not today's."

He said the jobs data shows the economic recovery is being skewed towards the owners of capital, rather than workers.
