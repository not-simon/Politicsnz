## Summary

**Labour's record over the last five elections splits sharply in two.**

1. **In opposition (2011, 2014)** Labour ran on bold, unpopular-but-"responsible" policies — a capital gains tax, raising the pension age to 67, NZ Power, KiwiAssure — lost twice, and **dropped every one of them** before 2017. The CGT has returned in 2026 in a narrower form.
2. **In government (2017–2023)** Labour delivered most of its *legislative and money-transfer* promises — the 100-day plan, Families Package, fees-free first year, minimum wage to $20, Healthy Homes, foreign-buyer ban, Zero Carbon Act, 10 sick days, Matariki, Fair Pay Agreements, 39% top tax rate. But it **failed on most of its big physical-delivery targets** — KiwiBuild's 100,000 homes, Auckland light rail (promised twice), the migration cut — and only partly met its child-poverty, mental-health, police and tree-planting targets.

Plus 7 opposition-era promises (2011/2014, all abandoned), 5 from the lost 2023 campaign, and 19 groups of 2026 promises (not yet due) — 92 entries in all.

**Key nuance:** coalition partner NZ First blocked or diluted several Labour policies in 2017–20 (CGT, water charges, three-strikes repeal, light rail, 90-day trials); COVID-19 dominated 2020–22. But the 2020–23 majority term — when Labour had no partner able to block it — produced its own broken promise on tax, a second light-rail failure, the mandatory Three Waters U-turn, and a 2023 "policy bonfire" of its own initiatives. Many of the promises it did keep were reversed by the 2023–26 coalition within months (see [Durability](#durability-what-survived-the-change-of-government)).

## 1. Opposition-era promises (2011 & 2014)

Labour lost both elections, so these could not be tested in office. They matter for **consistency**: did Labour keep campaigning on what it said it believed?

**Pattern:** after two defeats Labour concluded its "brave" policies were electoral liabilities. Little's reset — fewer, simpler, popular promises — set up the 2017 platform. Fair-minded reading: dropping policies voters rejected is normal democratic responsiveness; but the CGT's cycle (promised 2011/14 → dropped 2015 → revived 2017 → ruled out 2019 → ruled out 2023 → promised 2025/26) is the clearest example of Labour saying it believes in a policy while repeatedly declining to put it to voters or implement it.

Context: Labour won 36.9% and governed in **coalition with NZ First**, with **confidence & supply from the Greens**. Both agreements added commitments (NZ First: $1b/yr regional fund, 1,800 police, 1 billion trees, minimum wage path; Greens: Zero Carbon Act, cannabis referendum). NZ First also vetoed some Labour policies.

**Cost-of-living aftermath.** Heavy pandemic spending, low interest rates and global shocks were followed by inflation of **7.3% in the year to June 2022**, a 32-year high ([1News/Stats NZ](https://1news.co.nz/2022/07/18/inflation-rises-to-73-a-32-year-high/)). The government responded with a temporary 25c fuel-excise cut, half-price public transport and a cost-of-living payment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_Labour_Government_of_New_Zealand)), and from 2023 Hipkins re-centred the government on "bread and butter" issues. Economists differ on how much of the inflation was domestic policy vs. global; we do not adjudicate that here.

## 6. Reliability scorecard

### By term (scored promises only)

| Term | Scored | ✅ Kept | 🟡 Partly | ❌ Broken | ⛔ Abandoned | Kept or partly |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2017–20 (coalition with NZ First; Green C&S) | 38 | 22 (58%) | 9 (24%) | 4 (11%) | 3 (8%) | 82% |
| 2020–23 (Labour majority) | 23 | 14 (61%) | 1 (4%) | 3 (13%) | 5 (22%) | 65% |
| **Total** | **61** | **36 (59%)** | **10 (16%)** | **7 (11%)** | **8 (13%)** | **75%** |

Of the 3 first-term abandonments, 2 (CGT, water royalty) were driven by NZ First; the third (fees-free years 2–3) was Labour's own choice. **All 5 second-term abandonments were Labour's own decisions**, made with a majority.

### By type of promise

| Type | Examples | Scored | Kept | Partly | Broken/abandoned |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Big physical-delivery or numeric outcome targets** | KiwiBuild 100k and year-one, light rail ×2, migration cut, 1,800 police, 1bn trees, child poverty, mental health, rivers, Pike River, public housing ×2 | 13 | 2 | 6 | 5 |
| **Laws, regulations, transfers, taxes, inquiries** | 100-day plan, Families Package, min wage, Healthy Homes, foreign buyers, Zero Carbon, sick leave, Matariki, FPAs, 39% rate, abortion, conversion ban | 48 | 34 | 4 | 10 |

This is the single clearest finding: **when a promise could be delivered by passing a law, changing a payment or announcing a review, Labour usually delivered it (about 71% kept outright); when it required building things or hitting an outcome number, it kept 2 of 13 outright** — and the two it "kept" (public housing) are disputed on counting method.

### Durability: what survived the change of government
At least 15 kept promises were reversed or substantially wound back by the 2023–26 National–ACT–NZ First coalition, most within its first year: Fair Pay Agreements, smokefree generation, Three Waters, Te Aka Whai Ora, three-strikes repeal, Clean Car Discount, oil and gas ban, interest-deductibility removal, free prescriptions, youth public transport fares, fees-free first year (converted to final year), Te Pūkenga, light rail, the 2023/24 child-poverty targets (lowered) and the foreign-buyer ban (partial exemption) ([OAG](https://www.oag.parliament.nz/2025/child-poverty/part1.htm); other sources on each entry in the JSON). Several were rushed through late in the 2020–23 term or legislated without cross-party support, which made reversal easy. Measures with broader support (Zero Carbon Act, Healthy Homes standards, Matariki, conversion-practices ban, minimum wage path, 10 sick days, Families Package) have largely survived.

## 8. Trends

- **Volatile support, personality-driven.** 27% (2011) → 25% (2014) → 37% (2017, after Ardern replaced Little) → 50% (2020, COVID rally) → 27% (2023) → ~27–31% in October 2026 polls after leading through early 2026 ([README](./README.md#2026-polling)).
- **Ambition outran delivery capacity.** Labour's signature 2017 promises were large build/outcome targets that depended on state delivery capability it did not have (KiwiBuild, light rail). Its later promises have become smaller, costed, and transfer- or regulation-based (fare caps, free GP visits, KiwiSaver settings) — a lesson visibly learned, though the 2026 NZ Future Fund and $4b infrastructure pledge return to delivery-heavy territory.
- **Coalition constraint was real but is not the whole story.** NZ First demonstrably blocked or diluted at least six Labour policies in 2017–20. Yet Labour's kept-rate barely changed with a majority (58% → 61%), while its own abandonments rose (3 → 5) and two of the three majority-term broken promises (bright-line, mandatory Three Waters) were entirely self-inflicted.
- **Tax promises are Labour's weakest area for consistency.** Of the tax pledges we track, Labour kept those it made *as limits* in government (no new taxes 2017–20; no wealth tax 2020–23; 39% only) except the bright-line, but repeatedly abandoned tax *reforms* it advocated (CGT ×4, water royalty).
- **Late-term rush, low durability.** Big structural reforms (Three Waters, Health NZ, Te Pūkenga, FPAs, smokefree, RNZ/TVNZ) were legislated or attempted late in the 2020–23 term, often on Labour votes alone, and most were reversed within a year of the 2023 election.
- **Strong crisis management, weaker routine delivery.** Independent reviews credit the COVID response (lowest-tier mortality; wage subsidy "much to commend"), the Christchurch firearms response and emergency payments; the same period saw slow delivery on mental health, KiwiBuild and Te Pūkenga.
- **2023–26: re-positioning.** Out of office Labour has re-adopted a CGT, moved to "cost of living + competition" policy (supermarkets, banks, airlines, price gouging), and tied itself to the Greens while ruling out NZ First and ACT.

## 10. Method and caveats

- **What counts as a promise:** specific commitments in Labour's manifestos, 100-day plans, pledge cards, coalition/support agreements it signed, and clear public commitments by the leader or finance spokesperson. Some government initiatives that were not manifesto pledges (Te Pūkenga, Health NZ, Three Waters, smokefree, Ihumātao, COVID measures) are included because the user asked about them and because they show whether actions matched stated aims; they are marked as such.
- **Verdicts:** *kept* = done substantially as promised; *partially kept* = meaningful but incomplete delivery, or delivered in diluted form; *broken* = not done, or contradicted, while Labour was in government; *abandoned* = Labour itself dropped the promise (we note when a partner forced it); *not yet due* = 2026 promises or carried-forward ones. Reversal by a later government does not change a "kept" verdict, but is recorded under [Durability](#durability-what-survived-the-change-of-government).
- **Scoring:** only the 61 promises testable while Labour was in government are scored. Opposition-era and 2026 promises are listed but excluded from percentages.
- **Limits:** this is a selection of ~60 significant promises, not every manifesto line (Labour's 2017 and 2020 manifestos contained hundreds). We could not obtain the full 2017/2020 manifesto PDFs during this research (web.archive.org not reachable; see `docs/AGENT_BRIEF.md`), so promise wording is taken from contemporaneous news reports and official releases. Our web-search budget ran out before we could double-check a few items (e.g. whether Labour formally dropped GST-off-fruit-and-vegetables); those are flagged in the text. Counts would shift somewhat with a different selection; the *pattern* (legislation kept, big builds missed) is robust across every independent source we read.
- **Even-handedness:** the same categories and evidentiary standard are used for all parties in this archive. Partisan sources (party press releases, ACT/National campaign pages) are cited only for what a party said, never as evidence of outcomes.
