# Immigration

**We very much welcome hard-working immigrants who can support themselves and their families, and who share our values.**

They leave their old lives behind, bringing their much-needed skills, their families, their life savings (for example, they may sell their former properties), and they pay tax - all in all making a major contribution to the fabric of New Zealand society.

However, if our population grows faster than our stock of social goods (medical professionals, hospital beds, school places, housing, etc.) then all New Zealanders become poorer.

**Background**

We see in many countries overseas the fruits of poorly controlled or managed immigration.

As a small island country, distant from the world's main population centres, New Zealand can avoid these problems - but only if we keep up our guard.

Canada - a country like ours that welcomes immigrants - stands as a perfect example of how not to do things.  As a result of decades of poorly managed immigration, they now have: strictly rationed healthcare; many Canadians unable to afford a home; a high penetration of organised crime from overseas; high rates of immigration fraud; low productivity (through over-reliance on imported unskilled labour); and higher rates of youth unemployment.

In New Zealand there appear to be an increasing number of overseas drivers of goods vehicles who may not be properly qualified (observation).

The issue of residence visas based on money alone ("golden" visas) is a magnet for organised criminals (New Zealand is no different in this regard to other countries).

New Zealand has a deservedly poor reputation among good quality migrants, because we are always changing the rules without notice (bringing uncertainty, wasted time, and expense to them).

**Our Policies**

The granting of work and residence visas should be adjusted away from unskilled and semi-skilled workers (and their dependents) towards skilled professionals and entrepreneurs.  This should be based on a stable points system (as is the case in other countries), with only minor changes over time (and signalled in advance).

With the exception of these skilled migrants, net immigration to New Zealand should be capped based on a broad measure of social goods (such as housing stock, the number of medical professionals, hospital beds, school places).  If our social goods are not increasing, net immigration of unskilled and semi-skilled workers and their dependents should be zero.

Immigrant drivers of goods vehicles should sit the appropriate driving licence test in New Zealand before they are allowed to drive on our roads.  If they fail twice, they should be repatriated at the sponsoring employer's expense.

More rigorous cross-checking and audits of supporting documents used in residence, work, and student visa applications should be introduced.  Restrictions should be placed on immigration from countries known or discovered to have high rates of fraud in this regard.

The staffing and processes used in the granting of residence and work visas should be regularly reviewed and audited from the ground up (immigration departments overseas have been known to be infiltrated by organised criminals).

The practice of issuing residence visas based on money alone ("golden" visas) should cease.

Overall - as in other fields prone to fraud - it is important that New Zealand is not seen as a "soft touch" by undesirable immigrants.

Lastly, GDP (gross domestic product) should routinely be expressed by the government on a *per capita* basis (and after adjusting for inflation).  This is a better measure of whether we are becoming richer or poorer.
