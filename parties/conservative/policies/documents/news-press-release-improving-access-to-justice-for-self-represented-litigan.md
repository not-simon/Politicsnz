# Press Release

Law & Justice Policy Statement - Improving Access to Justice for Self-Represented Litigants

 

"It is long past time for self-represented litigants navigating the judicial system to be adequately resourced in their endeavours", says Stephen Taylor, Law & Justice Spokesman for the Conservative Party of New Zealand, and a successful self-represented litigant in the Disputes Tribunal, District Court, High Court, Court of Appeal, and the Supreme Court of New Zealand.

In the civil jurisdiction, self-represented litigants make up 71% of the cases on file with court registries throughout New Zealand.

In the New Zealand Family Court, 12.8% of cases involve a self-represented litigant.

In the criminal jurisdiction, just 3% of cases involve a self-represented party, with the Public Defence Service providing the bulk of legal aid assistance (73%) to this group of litigants.

All self-represented litigants across all jurisdictions deserve equal access to justice, however, this is not the case. Too “rich” to qualify for legal aid, but not “rich” enough to be able to afford a lawyer, the burgeoning civil litigant population falls through not just a justice gap, but tumbles into a justice canyon when attempting to secure meaningful access to justice.

Access to justice is a fundamental right, ensuring that every individual can seek and obtain a remedy through the legal system. However, many self-represented litigants face significant barriers, particularly those who do not qualify for legal aid but cannot afford a lawyer.

Mr Taylor proposes a suite of policies to improve access to justice for self-represented litigants across the family, civil, and criminal courts.

1. Proposal for Procedural Improvements

Simplification of Court Forms and Procedures

Simplifying court forms and procedures for self-represented litigants, including providing clear, step-by-step guides and checklists, would reduce the procedural burden on these individuals.

Additionally, offering workshops and training sessions on court processes can empower litigants to handle their cases more effectively.

Not all self-represented litigants are skilled in technology and online navigation, so having simplified documentation would greatly assist this population.

Enhanced Online Resources

Expanding online resources, such as the Ministry of Justice website, to include interactive tools, video tutorials, the development of a single online portal, and comprehensive FAQs can provide self-represented litigants with accessible information and guidance at their convenience.

2. Proposal for Enhanced Support Services

Expansion of Family Legal Advisory Service

The Family Legal Advisory Service provides critical guidance for self-represented litigants in family law matters. Expanding this service to offer more comprehensive support, including detailed procedural advice and assistance with document preparation, would help litigants navigate the complexities of family court proceedings.

This expansion would include additional funding to increase the availability of advisors and extend service hours.

Strengthening Community Law Centres

Community Law Centres offer free legal advice and assistance to those who cannot afford legal representation. Increasing funding for these centres would allow them to hire more staff, including lawyers and paralegals, and extend their outreach programs.

This would ensure that more self-represented litigants receive the necessary support and guidance to pursue their cases effectively.

Enhancement of Kaiārahi Family Court Navigators

Kaiārahi Family Court Navigators assist self-represented litigants by providing information on court procedures and helping them understand their legal rights and obligations. Expanding the navigator program to cover all family court locations and increasing the number of navigators would provide more tailored coaching and resource support to individuals, ensuring they are well-prepared for court appearances.

3. Proposal for Legal Aid Reform

Broadening Legal Aid Eligibility

The eligibility criteria and rates for legal aid in New Zealand need to be increased to catch up with inflation, as the current rates and eligibility thresholds have become long outdated and insufficient.

The Legal Services Agency is mandated to refuse legal aid to applicants whose disposable income or capital exceeds the prescribed thresholds unless there are special circumstances.

These thresholds, such as the maximum disposable capital for civil matters set at $3,500, have not kept pace with inflation and the rising cost of living.

The maximum disposable income thresholds for legal aid eligibility in New Zealand for civil matters are:

$22,366 per year for a single applicant.

$35,420 per year for an applicant with a spouse or partner, or with one dependent child.

$50,934 per year for an applicant with a spouse or partner and one dependent child, or with two dependent children

The current criteria fail to consider the full context of applicants' financial and personal circumstances, which can lead to unjust outcomes. For instance, applicants with disposable capital just above the threshold, despite having significant financial struggles, are often deemed ineligible.

This results in many applicants, who genuinely need legal aid, being denied assistance because their financial situation slightly exceeds these outdated limits.

Therefore, updating the legal aid eligibility criteria and rates to reflect inflation and the actual cost of living would ensure fairer access to justice for all individuals in need.

Flexible Legal Aid Grants

Implementing a more flexible approach to legal aid grants can ensure that individuals who require legal representation but do not meet the strict criteria can still receive support. This includes allowing partial grants or grants for specific stages of the proceedings where self-representation is particularly challenging.

4. Proposal for Pro Bono Scheme Expansion

Compensation for Pro Bono Work

To encourage more lawyers to provide free legal services, the government should introduce:

- A structured scheme offering tax deductions for pro bono work.
- A framework of standard rates to compensate lawyers for pro bono work through awards of costs, reflective of the lawyer's experience and the complexity of the case. Lawyers would be able to invoice clients for work done on a pro bono basis if a costs award is made, ensuring they are compensated for their time and effort.\
 This approach has been recognised as effective in ensuring that pro bono work does not disincentivize legal representation due to the inability to recover costs.

Formalizing Pro Bono Work

Formalizing and expanding the Te Ara Ture pro bono referral service would encourage more lawyers to participate in providing free legal services.

This could include structured programs where lawyers commit to a certain number of pro bono hours annually and receive recognition for their contributions.

5. Expansion of Family Dispute Resolution Service

On 1 July 2025, the Family Dispute Resolution (FDR) service was made free for up to 12 hours. This service is available to parents or guardians involved in disputes regarding the care and contact arrangements for their children, and aims to support children's welfare by facilitating constructive discussions and agreements between parents, ensuring that parents can maintain and strengthen their relationships with their children.

To access the service, individuals must meet specific criteria, including:

- Participation in prescribed programmes under the Domestic Violence Act and the Parenting Through Separation programme.
- Demonstrating a genuine need for mediation to resolve their disputes and being willing to engage in the process cooperatively.

The FDR service supports people to resolve disputes about parenting arrangements without needing to go to court. Removing financial eligibility thresholds for free sessions would encourage people to resolve matters outside of court.

Conclusion

Improving access to justice for self-represented litigants requires a multi-faceted approach, including expanding advisory services, reforming legal aid eligibility, simplifying procedures, enhancing online resources, and improving access to pro bono services.

These measures will ensure that every individual has the support they need to navigate the legal system effectively and fairly.
