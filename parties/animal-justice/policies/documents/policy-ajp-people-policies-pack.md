Policy framework
The AJP seeks to create a society where the welfare, and ability to thrive, of both
humans and other animals is at the forefront, guided by compassion, inclusivity, just
governance, and a commitment to sustainability. As responsible stewards for future
generations we must consider the impact of our actions today on animals, the planet
and by extension ourselves.

We acknowledge the intrinsic connection between humans and other animals,
recognising the welfare of one directly impacts the other. When animals lead better
lives, the result is not only reduced suffering and exploitation, but also an overall
improvement in the health of our ecosystems, contributing to a healthier planet, and
in turn, fostering healthier communities.

Practices such as factory farming and cruel treatment of animals not only raise moral
and ethical questions but can also desensitise and harm individuals, and lead to
environmental degradation creating further harm. We believe the wellbeing of people
can be enhanced through ethical and compassionate choices in how we use, protect
and interact with animals.

Te Tiriti o Waitangi holds profound significance for Aotearoa New Zealand, shaping
the nation's foundation and fostering principles of justice, inclusivity, and cooperation.

The AJP embraces this treaty and acknowledges its importance in guiding a
compassionate, just and equitable relationship with animals. The treaty's
commitment to non-violent conflict resolution aligns with the AJP’s advocacy for
compassionate conservation, emphasising ecological sustainability and social
responsibility.

A just transition to a plant-based Aotearoa New Zealand reflects the essence of Te
Tiriti o Waitangi, promoting harmony and respect for all beings within the nation's
diverse tapestry.

The AJP is committed to improving the well-being of people by creating a better
world for non-human animals.

The current economic system perpetuates harm to non-human animals whilst
contributing to the exploitation and detriment of human well-being within these
industries.

People policies summary
The AJP believes that creating a better world for animals begins with building a more
just, compassionate, and democratic society for all people. When humans are free
from economic exploitation, can participate meaningfully in democratic processes,
and are educated to value empathy and ethical decision-making, we create the
conditions necessary for extending that same compassion to all sentient beings.

These policies address the interconnected systems that currently perpetuate harm to
both humans and animals—from economic inequality that traps people in exploitative
industries, to democratic deficits that allow wealthy interests to block necessary
reforms, to educational systems that fail to nurture our natural capacity for
compassion.

Honouring Te Tiriti o Waitangi forms the bedrock of our political platform. Grounded
in kaitiakitanga (guardianship) and genuine partnership, our commitment to Te Tiriti
shapes and informs each of the five core policy areas below.

1. Democratic Reform and Participation

Creating a democracy that truly serves all beings by ensuring every voice can be
heard equally, regardless of wealth or political connections. These reforms will
strengthen democratic processes, improve representation for marginal communities,
and build the participatory systems needed to address complex challenges facing
our society.

2. Worker Rights and Just Transition

Supporting people to transition away from industries that harm both workers and
animals, while recognising the full humanity of all workers through comprehensive
rights and support systems. These policies create pathways to meaningful,
non-violent employment.

3. Economic Justice and Fairness

Building an economy that serves community wellbeing rather than enabling extreme
wealth concentration and corporate manipulation of public opinion. These policies
create ethical and legislative boundaries on wealth accumulation and corporate
influence while supporting the transition to a more compassionate society.

4. Protection and Advocacy Rights

Protecting vulnerable people and animals from harm while safeguarding the
democratic rights of those who speak up for others. These policies ensure that

Strengthening Partnership with Tangata Whenua
The Animal Justice Party Aotearoa NZ (AJP) recognises that our history is both rich and at
times turbulent. AJP accepts Te Tiriti o Waitangi as the founding document of Aotearoa, and
commits to the principles of non-violent conflict resolution, compassionate conservation for
ecological sustainability, social responsibility towards all of our peoples, and a just transition
to a plant-based Aotearoa.

We believe in compassion, equality and non-violence therefore, we believe Māori and
mātauranga Māori should be respected and embraced. While AJP predominantly focuses on
animal rights and issues, we recognise Māori have an important role as kaitiakitanga
(guardians) of the whenua (land), moana/awa/roto (seas/rivers/lakes) and kararehe
(animals) of Aotearoa. To foster strong and respectful relationships between our party and
the tangata whenua (people of the land) of Aotearoa, we are committed to upholding the
rights of Māori to have a meaningful voice in decisions that directly impact them and their
whenua (land). We recognise the importance of partnership and advocacy in ensuring that
issues affecting Māori are addressed with fairness, inclusivity, and respect.

We acknowledge the role of Māori as tangata whenua (people of the land) and recognise the
importance of kaitiakitanga, manaakitanga, and tino rangatiratanga in shaping a just
transition. We understand that decolonisation is essential for creating the conditions where
both Māori ways of being and environmental restoration can flourish, recognising that
colonial structures have harmed both tangata whenua and the natural world they are
connected to as kaitiaki.

Key Objective:

- Work towards establishing genuine partnerships with tangata whenua to create
robust policies that will heal the environment and restore animal habitats whilst
honouring mātauranga Māori.
- Support the establishment of strong kaitiakitanga frameworks to enable capabilities
for Māori to protect kararehe, whenua, and moana/awa/roto through culturally
appropriate conservation practices.
- Advocate for the protection efforts of tangata whenua in the use and care of natural
resources, ensuring Māori have decision-making authority over ancestral lands and
waters.
- Integrate mātauranga Māori into conservation science and animal welfare practices,
recognising the value of indigenous knowledge systems in creating sustainable
solutions.
- Restore degraded ecosystems through collaborative approaches that honour both
scientific understanding and traditional ecological knowledge.

Addressing Colonial Impacts on Māori Health and Food
Systems
The AJP recognises that colonisation and the establishment of intensive animal agriculture
have created systemic injustices that continue to impact Māori health, land rights, and food
sovereignty. We are committed to addressing these intersecting challenges through a just
transition that honours Te Tiriti principles and supports Māori-led solutions.

The colonial transformation of Aotearoa into "the dairy farm of the British Empire" displaced
traditional Māori food systems and imposed Western dietary patterns high in animal
products, contributing to disproportionate health inequities that persist today. 123Animal
farming continues to degrade waterways4 and ecosystems that are fundamental to Māori
identity and wellbeing, with many Māori communities bearing the brunt of environmental
pollution and land-use pressures.

We recognise that for many New Zealanders, including both Māori and Pākehā, there are
strong cultural and historical ties to animal farming. For Māori, these ties are often rooted in
whakapapa and a deep ancestral relationship with the land, while for Pākehā, the connection
has developed more recently through settler-colonial history. Understanding these different
histories is essential as we navigate a just transition away from animal-based systems.

AJP recognises that colonial systems have created structures of domination and exploitation
that affect multiple communities and beings. We will support systemic changes that address
these root causes whilst respecting the distinct nature of different liberation movements.

Key Objective:

- Ensure that Māori landholders are not disproportionately impacted by the transition
away from animal agriculture and are supported in exercising kaitiakitanga, whilst
prioritising Māori leadership in land-use decision-making to redress colonial injustices
and support tino rangatiratanga.(Transition to a Plant Based Economy policy
objectives)
- Support Māori food sovereignty through funding māra kai (community gardens),
traditional food systems, and indigenous plant-based industries such as kūmara and
native crops, recognising practices such as maramataka (Māori lunar planting cycles)
as integral to sustainable food production. (Transition to a Plant Based Economy
policy objectives)

Getting Money Out of Politics - Restoring Democratic
Equality
The AJP is committed to creating a democracy where all voices are heard equally,
regardless of wealth or corporate connections. We acknowledge that money
currently dominates our political system, giving wealthy industries—particularly those
that exploit animals—vastly superior access to decision-makers compared to
ordinary citizens and community advocates. We propose comprehensive reforms to
eliminate financial advantages in politics, ensuring democratic participation is based
on ideas and community support rather than economic power.

Unregulated lobbying creates a two-tier democracy where wealthy interests
dominate decision-making while ordinary citizens and advocacy groups are shut out.
The revelations in August 2025 about the then Fisheries Minister Shane Jones
receiving over $100,000 from fishing industry donors while rolling back
environmental protections illustrate this problem perfectly.5 Companies like Talley's
Group strategically split donations to avoid disclosure thresholds, while fishing baron
Craig Boote admitted to spending $30,000 across parties before securing exclusive
access to ministers.6 This isn't democracy - it's a system where money buys policy
outcomes.

Although Federated Farmers does not make direct political donations,7 influence
can flow through other channels. Andrew Hoggard’s career path illustrates the
concern: after nearly a decade in Federated Farmers leadership roles, including
serving as president until May 2023, he joined ACT New Zealand the very next day.8
Following the 2023 election, he was appointed Minister for Biosecurity and Food
Safety, and Associate Minister of Agriculture (Animal Welfare, Skills)9 and for the
Environment, portfolios directly affecting farming and animal welfare. This revolving
door between industry leadership and political office underlines the urgent need for
reform: mandatory lobbying registers, cooling-off periods, and stronger transparency
rules to ensure that public decision-making serves the wider public interest.

animals (factory farming, commercial fishing, entertainment using animals) rely
heavily on political influence to maintain harmful practices. By eliminating their

Democratic Representation for All Voices
The AJP is committed to reducing the MMP party vote threshold from 5% to 3.5% to
ensure that diverse voices, including those advocating for animals, environment, and
social justice, can meaningfully participate in parliamentary democracy.10 We
acknowledge that the current 5% threshold excludes significant portions of the
population from representation, particularly those supporting smaller parties focused
on ethical issues that major parties often ignore or compromise on.

An independent review of New Zealand's electoral system has concluded a major
shake-up of MMP is needed to ensure a fairer and more accessible democracy. The
current threshold means that parties receiving up to 4.9% of the vote—potentially
representing over 120,000 voters—receive no parliamentary representation
whatsoever. This creates a democratic deficit where substantial communities
advocating for animal protection, environmental justice, and other ethical concerns
are systematically excluded from decision-making processes.

International comparisons reveal New Zealand's threshold is unnecessarily high. In
Bolivia the threshold is 3%, in Germany 5% for federal parliament, while some
European nations operate effectively with thresholds as low as 2%. The Electoral
Commission recommended lowering the 5 percent party vote threshold to 3.5
percent as part of comprehensive electoral reforms designed to enhance democratic
participation.11

Reducing the threshold would enable parties focused on animal welfare,
environmental protection, and social justice to gain parliamentary representation with
community support levels that already demonstrate significant public backing. This
change would force major parties to engage seriously with ethical issues they
currently marginalise, knowing that specialist parties could hold balance-of-power
positions.

The AJP believes that lowering the party vote threshold is a fundamental democratic
reform that will amplify voices advocating for those without political power—including
animals. This change will ensure that ethical concerns receive proper parliamentary
consideration, create more responsive governance, and build the inclusive
democracy necessary for addressing complex challenges facing all beings in our
society.

Participatory Democracy for Complex Societal Issues
The AJP is committed to creating a democracy that truly serves all beings by
ensuring citizens can meaningfully participate in complex decision-making
processes. We acknowledge that current democratic systems often fail to address
challenging issues due to polarisation, partisan politics, and the dominance of vested
interests over genuine public deliberation. We propose strengthening participatory
democracy to build the foundations of a more thoughtful, inclusive, and
compassionate society.

Participatory democracy goes beyond traditional voting by involving citizens directly
in decision-making on complex issues. It includes methods like citizens' assemblies,
where randomly selected groups of people learn about an issue, hear from experts,
and deliberate together to make recommendations.

Ireland's Citizens' Assembly on abortion provides a powerful example.12 In 2016, 99
randomly selected citizens spent five months examining this issue, hearing from
medical experts, legal scholars and people with lived experiences. Their thoughtful
recommendations led to a referendum that legalised abortion - a result many thought
impossible through traditional politics alone.

Research shows deliberative democracy works. An OECD study of citizens'
assemblies found that over half of the recommendations were implemented by public
authorities in 76% of cases reviewed, and all recommendations implemented in 36%
of cases.13 In Aotearoa New Zealand, we already have 15+ examples of successful
deliberative democracy at local level, proving our communities are ready for this
approach.14

The AJP believes that empowering citizens to engage deeply with complex ethical
questions is an effective step that can be taken now to build a more compassionate
democracy. It will lead to better policy outcomes that consider the interests of all
affected beings, break through political polarisation and create the democratic
culture necessary for addressing the greatest challenges facing our society and
planet.

Comprehensive Wellbeing Data Framework
The AJP is committed to creating evidence-based governance that measures what
truly matters for thriving communities. We acknowledge that current data systems
focus primarily on economic indicators while ignoring social, environmental and
ethical dimensions of wellbeing, making it impossible to track whether our policies
actually improve lives for all beings. We propose establishing comprehensive
wellbeing data systems that capture the full spectrum of what makes communities
healthy and compassionate.

You can't improve what you don't measure. While New Zealand still has wellbeing
tools like the Living Standards Framework (including the LSF Dashboard)15 and the
He Ara Waiora framework,16 recent changes, such as removal of certain wellbeing
requirements in the Public Finance Act and the discontinuation of the branded
Wellbeing Budget1718 have weakened mandatory reporting and accountability. This
leaves gaps in our ability to track whether policies are improving people’s lives or to
identify where interventions could have the greatest impact.

A proposal for a comprehensive wellbeing data system is not without precedent. The
Kingdom of Bhutan, for instance, pioneered the concept of Gross National
Happiness (GNH), which deliberately elevated the wellbeing of its citizens above
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) as its central development philosophy. The resulting
GNH Index is a robust, multidimensional measurement tool that guides policymaking
across nine domains, including ecological resilience, cultural vitality and time use.19
This international example of embedding social, environmental and ethical
dimensions into a mandated policy-screening framework demonstrates a successful
model for the “compassionate governance” and full-spectrum measurement we are
committed to achieving in New Zealand.

The AJP believes that implementing comprehensive wellbeing data is an effective
step that can be taken now to build accountability for compassionate governance. It
will lead to better policy decisions that consider the interconnectedness of human,
animal, and environmental health, provide clear evidence of what interventions work
best and create the foundation for a society that values the wellbeing of all beings.

2. Worker Rights and Just Transition
Mental Health Support and Just Transition for Workers
in Violent / Exploitative Industries
The AJP is committed to creating a society where no person is forced into work that
causes psychological harm to themselves and violence to others. We recognise that
many industries founded on systematic harm, including, but not limited to, animal
exploitation (slaughterhouses, factory farming), industrial wildlife harvesting, and
other violent production systems, impose deep psychological burdens on workers.
These burdens often extend into families and communities, creating cycles of trauma
that affect both humans and non-humans.

We propose comprehensive mental health support and retraining programmes to
help workers transition away from these harmful industries towards meaningful,
non-violent employment.

While slaughterhouse work is among the most extreme cases, because of the nature
of killing and dismemberment, workers in industrial farming (e.g. intensive
confinement, culling, animal handling), pest control, fur or wildlife harvest industries
may face analogous harms and deserve equal attention in this policy.20

Recent research on farmer wellbeing highlights similar moral and psychological
strain within intensive animal agriculture, describing how livestock farmers
experience moral distress and moral injury arising from repeated exposure to animal
suffering and killing, contributing to elevated rates of mental ill-health.21 22This
underscores the need for trauma-informed support across all animal-use sectors, not
only in slaughterhouses.

Research consistently demonstrates the severe psychological toll of slaughterhouse
employment. A systematic literature review found that slaughterhouse workers
exhibit higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, and other psychological distress, as

billions convincing people that happiness comes from consuming animal products,

sustainability and compassion.

deliberately obscures the reality of factory farming, presenting false images of happy
animals while promoting products that cause immense suffering. By restricting this
misleading marketing, we create space for honest conversations about our food
choices and their impact on sentient beings.

households, representing a significant portion of the population who are directly

The AJP is committed to establishing veterinary care as a fundamental right for all
animals, recognising that access to medical treatment should never depend on their
guardian's wealth or their status as companion, free-living, or wildlife. We
acknowledge that the current system fails countless animals who suffer needlessly
due to financial barriers, geographic inequities, and a veterinary workforce pushed to
breaking point by commercial pressures and inadequate support. We propose
comprehensive reforms to create a universal veterinary care system that prioritises
animal wellbeing over profit, ensuring no animal is denied treatment because of
economic circumstances.

The veterinary care crisis in Aotearoa reflects broader societal failures to value
animal lives equally. Countless companion animals live in households struggling with
the cost of living, where a sudden illness or injury forces impossible choices between
veterinary bills and basic necessities. Meanwhile, injured wildlife, including our
precious native species, suffer without treatment, and free-living cats and dogs
continue breeding due to lack of accessible desexing services, perpetuating cycles
of suffering that overwhelm rescue organisations and community groups.

This crisis is not inevitable, it is a direct consequence of treating veterinary medicine
as a luxury commodity rather than essential care. The current system concentrates
services in wealthy urban areas while rural and regional communities face severe
shortages, leaving both animals and their human carers without local access to
critical treatment. Veterinarians themselves suffer under this model, with crushing
student debt, unsustainable workloads, and moral injury from having to deny care
based on payment capacity driving concerning rates of burnout and mental health
struggles within the profession.

treats animal healthcare as discretionary spending, while limited university places

4. Protection and Advocacy Rights
Comprehensive Whistleblower Protection and Peaceful
Protest Rights
The AJP is committed to protecting the fundamental democratic rights of
transparency, accountability, and peaceful protest that are essential for exposing and
addressing animal cruelty. We acknowledge that industries profiting from animal
exploitation are increasingly using legal strategies, such as ag-gag laws, to silence
critics, hide their practices from public scrutiny, and criminalise those who seek to
document or protest against animal abuse. We propose comprehensive protections
for whistleblowers and peaceful protesters to ensure that the voices of animals and
their advocates cannot be silenced.

Ag-gag laws are anti-whistleblower laws that forbid undercover filming or
photography of activity on farms without consent, specifically targeting
whistleblowers of suspected animal rights abuses. These dangerous bills, pushed by
the animal agriculture industry, are designed to silence whistleblowers revealing
animal abuses on industrial farms.

Since 2011, twenty-five US states have introduced ag-gag laws, with six passing
legislation that falls into three categories: agricultural interference laws (prohibiting
recording without consent), agricultural fraud laws (prohibiting obtaining access
under false pretences), and agricultural terrorism laws.67

The threat extends beyond the US. In Australia, ag-gag laws seek to hinder animal
advocates by preventing them from recording operations of commercial agricultural
facilities. Meanwhile, both NSW and Queensland governments, as well as the
Australian federal government, are ramping up laws against animal activists who
trespass on farming properties.68

The assault on democratic rights extends to peaceful protest. Five Australian states
(NSW, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania and Victoria) have introduced
anti-protest regulations, with South Australia's new laws increasing maximum fines
from $750 to $50,000 along with potential jail time. 69 The former Australian
