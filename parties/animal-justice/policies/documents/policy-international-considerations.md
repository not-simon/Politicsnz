Key trends in this evolving landscape include:

1. Science-Based Frameworks: Policy decisions are increasingly guided by robust

2. Global and Regional Agreements: International treaties and regional directives,

Fauna and Flora (CITES), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH)

across borders.
3. Integration into Broader Agendas: Animal welfare is being linked with
sustainability, public health, biodiversity, and climate change, ensuring it receives
attention in broader policy contexts.
4. Stakeholder Collaboration: Governments, NGOs, scientists, and industry
players are collaborating more actively to develop and implement welfare
policies.

his evolving framework not only reflects a growing ethical commitment to animals but
T
also acknowledges their role in sustainable development and human wellbeing. Global
animal protection is, however, a complex challenge requiring collaboration across
countries, cultures, and legal systems.

hile there are various treaties, conventions, and agreements addressing specific
W
aspects of animal welfare, there is no unified international instrument that broadly
regulates animal welfare across all contexts and provides holistic animal welfare
protections.

Key gaps include:

1. Limited Scope : Existing frameworks often prioritise conservation and
iodiversity rather than the welfare of individual animals. This leaves many
b
species, especially those not endangered or economically significant, without
legal protection.
2. Lack of Universal Standards and Jurisdictional Issues : Countries have
varying cultural, economic, and ethical views on animal welfare, making it difficult
to establish universally accepted standards. International frameworks may
conflict with national sovereignty, limiting their enforceability within domestic legal
systems.
3. Non-Binding Nature : Many international standards, such as the WOAH’s animal
welfare standards, are non-binding, leaving adoption and enforcement at the
discretion of individual nations.
4. Lack of Enforceability : Even binding agreements like CITES face challenges
with enforcement, as they rely on national governments to implement and
monitor compliance.
5. Species-Specific Gaps : Many frameworks focus on specific species or groups
(e.g., endangered species under CITES), leaving gaps in protection for other
animals, such as farmed or laboratory animals, despite these being the largest
groups of animals directly impacted by human activity.

1. Review of International Trade Law : Review New Zealand’s international trade
law and policy instruments and challenge any provisions that undermine animal

2. Harmonising Standards : Creating internationally accepted benchmarks that
accommodate regional differences whilst maintaining high welfare standards for
imported animal products. As New Zealand has some of the most robust animal
welfare practices, compared to our trading partners, we have set the benchmark
for our primary industry and a framework for continuous improvement in animal
quality of life:
- AJP will campaign for uniform standards when importing animal products.
- AJP will continue to advocate for discussions with trading partners to
improve practices or find alternative suppliers.
- AJP will continue to push for a fairer and more equitable system.
- AJP will use this issue as a global platform to advocate for animals used
for agriculture.
3. Developing Binding Agreements : Evaluate international conventions and
agreements, advocate for an international treaty on animal rights, and support a
transition from voluntary guidelines to binding treaties with clear accountability
mechanisms.
4. Promoting Education and Advocacy : Building public awareness and fostering
a cultural shift toward valuing animal welfare globally.
