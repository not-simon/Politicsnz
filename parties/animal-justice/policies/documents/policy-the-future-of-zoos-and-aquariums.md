AJP Policy Document

The Future of Zoos and Aquariums
The Animal Justice Party (AJP) advocates for a fundamental reorientation of zoos and
aquariums away from entertainment-driven models toward facilities that exist to serve
the interests of animals themselves. Facilities that currently exhibit animals for public
display must transition toward roles centred on rescue, veterinary care, rehabilitation
and, where necessary, the provision of lifelong sanctuary.

Where animals cannot be returned to the wild, their environments must be designed to
approximate natural conditions as closely as possible, prioritising autonomy, social
needs and behavioural expression. Public access, if permitted, must be strictly
managed to ensure that it does not compromise animal wellbeing.

The AJP recognises that many species are not suited to captivity under any
circumstances. As such, the long-term objective is to significantly reduce and, where
appropriate, eliminate the keeping of animals in traditional zoo settings. Technological
alternatives, particularly immersive and virtual experiences, should replace the use of
live animals for education and entertainment.

Key Objectives:

1. Transition to Animal-Centred Models
Phase out practices that prioritise exhibition and profit, supporting the evolution
of zoos and aquariums into rescue, rehabilitation and sanctuary-focused
facilities.

2. Restrict New Zoo Establishments
Prohibit the establishment of new private zoos and aquariums. In exceptional
circumstances where approval is considered, require rigorous pre-licensing
assessments, including detailed evaluation of operator suitability, financial
capacity and contingency planning for animal care.

3. End Breeding for Display
Ban captive breeding except where it forms part of independently assessed
conservation programmes that demonstrably benefit both species survival and
individual animal welfare.

4. Euthanasia Standards
Clear distinction between euthanasia conducted in an animal’s best interests and
that undertaken for management reasons, so that healthy animals are never
killed due to management failure or financial constraints.

5. Mandatory Exit and Transition Planning
Require all facilities to maintain legally enforceable exit strategies that specify
how animals will be rehomed, transferred or cared for in the event of closure or
operational change.

6. Financial Assurance Requirements
Introduce mandatory financial assurance mechanisms to ensure that adequate
funds are available for the ongoing care, relocation or rehabilitation of animals if
a facility ceases operation.

7. Develop Exit Pathways for Existing Animals
Implement coordinated strategies to relocate animals currently held in zoos and
aquariums to accredited sanctuaries or appropriate environments wherever
feasible.

8. Promote Non-Exploitative Alternatives
Support the development and accessibility of virtual reality and other immersive
technologies as humane alternatives to live animal displays.

9. Strengthen Transparency and Accountability
Mandate full disclosure of animal origins, health status and life histories and
prohibit the commercial import and export of live animals for profit.

10.Regulate Sanctuaries
Establish clear legal definitions and accreditation systems to distinguish genuine
sanctuaries from commercial enterprises, ensuring animal interests are
paramount.

11.Enhance Welfare Standards

Background and Rationale

Zoos and aquariums may unintentionally communicate that it is acceptable to confine
wild animals in small or unsuitable enclosures - sometimes in isolation from their own
species - for human education or entertainment. Yet such conditions can cause
significant harm, as many animals are unable to engage in their natural, species specific
behaviours.

The AJP’s approach is grounded in the moral significance of individual animals. Whilst
biodiversity and ecosystem protection are important, they must not be pursued at the
expense of the lives and wellbeing of individual sentient beings. Practices such as
forced breeding, confinement in inadequate conditions and the treatment of animals as
interchangeable components of conservation strategies are ethically indefensible.

Effective conservation must prioritise the protection and restoration of natural habitats.
Without suitable environments for animals to return to, captive breeding programmes
offer limited value and may, in some cases, perpetuate harm. Moreover, zoos tend to

The AJP envisions a future in which human engagement with animals is guided by
respect, responsibility and non-exploitation. Transforming zoos and aquariums is a
critical step toward this goal, ensuring that animals are no longer used for entertainment
purposes, but are instead protected as individuals with intrinsic value.

Defining Zoos and Sanctuaries

Facilities that house animals vary widely in purpose and practice, often using
overlapping terminology that obscures important ethical distinctions.

- Zoos and aquariums are typically commercial enterprises that display animals
to paying visitors, sometimes incorporating conservation or educational activities
alongside entertainment functions.

- Sanctuaries, by contrast, are dedicated to the protection and care of animals,
without breeding, trading, or exploiting them for profit. Whilst some may allow
visitors, animal interests must always take precedence.

Given the blurred boundaries between these categories, regulatory focus should be
directed toward the underlying purpose of the institution - whether it exists primarily for
animals or for commercial gain.

Animal Welfare Concerns

Confinement in artificial environments can lead to significant physical and psychological
harm. Many captive animals exhibit signs of distress, including repetitive behaviours,
self-harm, and abnormal aggression. In some cases, these symptoms are managed
pharmacologically rather than addressed at their source.5

Additionally, practices such as the killing of “surplus” animals highlight the
commodification inherent in zoo systems.6 Even in well-resourced facilities, captivity
restricts animals’ ability to engage in natural behaviours, form social bonds and exercise
autonomy.7

Education and Public Engagement
Claims that zoos provide meaningful conservation education are not strongly supported
by evidence.8 Visitor engagement is often driven by leisure and social interaction rather
than learning and any educational benefits are typically limited.

Emerging technologies offer a more effective and ethical pathway. Virtual and
immersive experiences can foster understanding of animals and ecosystems without
subjecting animals to captivity.9 These approaches also allow for broader accessibility
and adaptability.

To maintain credibility as educational institutions, facilities must also align their
practices, such as food offering, with principles of sustainability and animal protection.
