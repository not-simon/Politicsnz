# Ending the use of lethal biological agents and poisons for population control

> Archive note: Web text is the policy post body from animaljustice.org.nz/wp-json/wp/v2/policies; the PDF version linked from the page (if any) follows, extracted with pdftotext -layout.

## Full text (verbatim)

### Web page text

AJP opposes the use of lethal biological and poisons to control introduced species, for example Rabbit Haemorrhagic Virus Disease (RHDV) and poisons like 1080 and Brodifacoum. Instead, AJP advocates for humane, non-lethal, and species-specific methods of population management.

Poisons and biological agents used for population control frequently cause intense and prolonged suffering, with some poisons and diseases taking hours or days to kill, leading to unnecessary animal distress. Research has shown that some commonly used poisons, such as those with a welfare impact score over 5.51, result in slow, painful deaths and must be reconsidered as part of a humane transition in wildlife management.

**Key Objectives:**

1. Ban the use of all lethal biological agents, including Rabbit Haemorrhagic Virus Disease (RHDV).

2. Cease the use of all poisons, including 1080 and Brodifacoum aerial drops.

3. Fund research into ethical, non-lethal alternatives for managing introduced species, such as:
a. Immunocontraceptives for population control 
b. Fencing and habitat modification to prevent overpopulation 
c. Non-toxic deterrents and repellents

4. Ensure conservation efforts align with compassionate principles and recognise the sentience and intrinsic value of all animals.

Ending the use of lethal biological agents
and poisons for population control
AJP opposes the use of lethal biological and poisons to control introduced species, for
example Rabbit Haemorrhagic Virus Disease (RHDV) and poisons like 1080 and
Brodifacoum. Instead, AJP advocates for humane, non-lethal, and species-specific
methods of population management.
Poisons and biological agents used for population control frequently cause intense and
prolonged suffering, with some poisons and diseases taking hours or days to kill,
leading to unnecessary animal distress. Research has shown that some commonly
used poisons, such as those with a welfare impact score over 5.51, result in slow, painful
deaths and must be reconsidered as part of a humane transition in wildlife
management. For example:
- In 2018, a nationwide release of Rabbit Haemorrhagic Virus Disease (RHDV)
was carried out to reduce wild rabbit numbers.2 This virus induces acute
hepatitis, leading to fever, difficulty breathing, bleeding, convulsions, and
ultimately death from multiple organ failure over 2-4 days.3
- 1080, a deadly poison, is aerially dropped to kill possums, rats, stoats, wallabies
and rabbits,4 but frequently kills non-target species such as deer,5 pigs,6 mice,7

Many wild animals are referred to as ‘pests’ in the Animal Welfare Act and only have
limited protections under the Wildlife Act 1953. The use of these methods is
inconsistent with the intention of the Animal Welfare Act to protect nonhuman animals,
which by definition includes wildlife, from unnecessary or disproportionate harm. AJP
therefore advocates for the protections that the Animal Welfare Act affords to domestic
animals to be extended to all wildlife.
These animals, labelled as ‘pests’ in New Zealand by the Animal Welfare Act, are
sentient, and did not choose to be in New Zealand, but rather are themselves the
victims of past colonial actions. It is therefore both inhumane and unjust to subject them
to unnecessary extreme suffering.
