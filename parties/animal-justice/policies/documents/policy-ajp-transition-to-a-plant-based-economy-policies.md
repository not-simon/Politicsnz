Just transition to a plant-based economy
The AJP is committed to transitioning Aotearoa New Zealand to a wholly plant-based
economy, where animal farming has been eliminated and no animals are used for
economic gain. We acknowledge the role of Māori as tangata whenua (people of the
land) and recognise the importance of kaitiakitanga, manaakitanga, and tino
rangatiratanga in shaping a just transition. This transformation must be guided by
partnership with iwi and hapū, respecting mātauranga Māori and supporting Māori
economic aspirations through sustainable land-use practices. We want to create a more
prosperous, sustainable and secure economy that is underpinned by compassion for all.

A transformation approach is required. We recognise that our current economic model
is deeply reliant on the exploitation of animals for food, other products and exports. And
that for many New Zealanders, including both Māori and Pākehā, have strong cultural
and historical ties to animal farming. For Māori, these ties are often rooted in
whakapapa and a deep ancestral relationship with the land, while for Pākehā, the
connection has developed more recently through settler-colonial history and
generations of farming. Understanding these different histories is essential as we
navigate a just transition away from animal-based systems.

However, our current agricultural model has significant environmental impacts, including
biodiversity loss, freshwater degradation and greenhouse gas emissions, while also
inflicting unnecessary suffering on millions of sentient beings. It is also inherently risky
as an on-going economic strategy, as environmental tipping points, new technologies,
consumer preferences, societal concerns and changes in global trade threaten to
reduce long term demand for animal products alongside increasing regulatory burdens.
New Zealand is currently in a ‘chaos and emergence’ phase where existing systems are
being questioned and new alternatives are emerging.1

We appreciate transitioning to a plant-based economy will take time, and that to be
sustainable it must happen alongside support for farmers and the development of
attractive and affordable alternatives for both farmers and consumers alike. There are,
however, steps we can take now that will make a huge positive impact. These include
banning factory farming, tougher laws, regulations and enforcement mechanisms,
supporting farm livelihood transitions, engaging with Māori leadership, supporting
innovation in food tech, and promoting and incentivising more compassionate and

Shifting to a plant-based economy is the single most impactful change we can make to
reduce animal suffering whilst also addressing many of our most urgent economic,
social and environmental challenges. Our vision is for a radically different economy. Our
policy focuses on practical steps that can be implemented now to make this future
possible.

Four overarching objectives
Our policy for transitioning to a plant-based economy is focussed on shifting away from
animal agriculture and animal-based foods, and replacing these with sustainable and
compassionate alternatives. It is centered around four objectives, each supported by
specific proposals:

1. Increase consumption of plant-based foods. Use a variety of policy levers to
increase consumption and market demand for healthier, kinder, more sustainable
food options that align with kai ora (healthy food) principles

2. Use land and water sustainably and compassionately. Use regulation and
incentives to shift from animal to plant-based farming and other sustainable land
and water uses.

3. Support farmers, workers and communities to transition. Engage and
collaborate to support a fair and inclusive transition with economic opportunities
for all.

4. Develop long-term economic alternatives. Invest in new technologies,
products and markets - to replace animal farming and provide prosperity and
jobs.

To achieve these objectives, the Government should urgently develop a National Action
Plan to implement the proposals set out in this document. While animal agriculture
currently remains economically significant, there are both environmental and market
pressures that suggest the potential for transition. Given this context, transitioning to a
plant-based economy should be a national priority and the Government must play a
critical role in making this happen.

The ‘value’ of animal exploitation to New Zealand’s economy
Despite its well documented negative impacts, animal agriculture occupies a prominent
position in New Zealand's economy and national identity. While agriculture is often
described as the foundation of New Zealand's economic prosperity, contributing to GDP,

countries such as China prioritise food independence; and as innovation in food
technology increasingly provides cruelty-free alternatives, there is a very real possibility
that future market demand for New Zealand’s animal products will decline. Shifting
away from animal agriculture offers opportunities to diversify, build resilience, food
security, and secure long-term economic prosperity.

However, the impact on farmed animals cannot be captured by numbers alone. Poor

inadequate even when they are adhered to, but in practice we know that legal minimum

Impacts on the environment
Animal farming is a major driver of climate change. Globally it is responsible for
between 10% and 20% of global GHG emissions, and it is one of the leading causes of
deforestation.5 48.6% of New Zealand’s total emissions in 2022 came from sheep and
cattle, of which 25.7% was from dairy cattle, 12.4% from sheep and 10.5% from beef
cattle.6 The inefficiency of producing food through animals also means that shifting to
plant-based foods has the potential to release large tracts of land for ecosystem
restoration and reforestation - critical parts of the solution to climate change.7 In New
Zealand around half of all land is used for agriculture, the vast majority of which is for
grazing.8
Animal farming also has severe effects on rivers and waterways - degrading
ecosystems, killing animals and reducing water quality. Many Māori communities bear
the brunt of these effects, with degraded rivers, pollution, and land-use pressures
disproportionately affecting Māori land and resources. In New Zealand the Ministry for
the Environment has estimated that 95% of waterways whose catchments included
areas of farmland exceed guideline values for water turbidity or dissolved nutrients; and
that 24% of total river length in those catchments had E. coli levels that were greater
than would be expected under natural conditions.9 Algal growth in streams and rivers -
caused by fertiliser runoff - is widespread, leading to impairment of water for swimming,
drinking and fish and other wildlife.

Other voices for change

The AJP is not alone in advocating for a transition to a plant-based economy.
The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has said that shifting to more
sustainable diets is a major opportunity to reduce global GHG emissions, with a
theoretical global shift to wholly plant-based diets saving up to 8Gt CO2e p/a (~13% of
total current global emissions).17
At the national level some countries are now implementing measures to support a
reduction in animal based foods. For example the Netherlands has a goal to reduce
meat consumption by 50% by 2030,18 in October 2023 the Danish government
published a national plant-based action plan with its strategy to transition Denmark
towards a richer plant-based diet and boost plant-protein production 1920 and France’s
Climate and Resilience law (2021) encourages school canteens to serve more
vegetarian meals.21
At a regional level, California's Plant-Based School Lunch Act (2022) provides additional
reimbursements to schools serving plant-based meals, incentivising more plant-based
options in school cafeterias22 and New York City hospitals offer plant-based dishes as
the default option since 2022. 23 Most large schools and universities in the U.S., France
and Germany offer regular meatless meal options.24

A Treaty-Based Approach to Economic Transition
The AJP recognises that the transition to a plant-based economy must be grounded in
Te Tiriti o Waitangi me ngā mātāpono o te ao Māori (the Treaty of Waitangi and Māori
worldviews), ensuring that Māori are central partners in shaping Aotearoa’s future. By
integrating mātauranga Māori, supporting indigenous food sovereignty, and investing in
Māori-led agricultural transformation, we can create a truly sustainable, compassionate
and equitable economy for all. This includes recognising practices such as maramataka
(Māori lunar planting cycles) and māra kai (community food gardens) as part of broader
environmental restoration and food sovereignty initiatives.
The Government has a critical role in ensuring this transition is just, inclusive, and
reflective of Aotearoa’s unique bicultural identity. By working in genuine partnership with
Māori, we can create an economic model that protects the environment, enhances
well-being, and upholds the principles of Te Tiriti o Waitangi.
"Me whai kia whakamana te reo Māori, mātauranga Māori, me ngā tikanga tuku

Objective 1: Increase consumption of plant-based foods
Make plant-based foods the default option for public
institutions

The AJP proposes to increase the availability of plant-based foods in schools, hospitals
and other public institutions and events - by making nutritious plant-based foods the
default option.

At the current time our public institutions offer little in the way of alternatives to
animal-based foods, even in settings like hospitals where health and nutrition alone
should be a strong motive for change. Increasing the availability and visibility of
delicious plant-based foods is a low-cost high-impact way of increasing demand for
these products and supporting more sustainable, healthy and compassionate consumer
choices. This in turn has the potential to create a ripple effect beyond public institutions
by influencing broader community eating habits and private organisations.

Similar initiatives have been implemented with success elsewhere in the world. For
example in New York in 2022 plant-based meals were made the default option across
11 public hospitals. The initiative was introduced to address both health and
environmental objectives. By early 2024 over 1.2 million plant-based meals had been
served with an 80%+ satisfaction rate, and with discharged patients receiving a free
booklet of plant-based recipes to try at home. This also led to an estimated 36%
reduction in planet-heating greenhouse gas pollution, pointing to the contribution these
initiatives can make towards organisational carbon reduction efforts.26

To implement these initiatives in Aotearoa, we will also encourage the use of rooftops,
courtyards, and other green spaces in public institutions to create edible gardens.
These gardens can supply fresh, seasonal ingredients directly to institutional kitchens -
the ultimate in "farm to plate" - while also providing educational and therapeutic value.
Patients, students, staff, and visitors observing or participating in these gardens may be
inspired to grow food at home, creating a ripple effect beyond the institution. Globally,
edible landscapes are being successfully used in hospitals,27 schools,28 and community
centres29 to reconnect people with food, promote sustainability and enhance wellbeing.

Ensure fair and transparent labelling

The AJP proposes to mandate fair and transparent food labelling that provides
information about the environmental, health and animal welfare impacts of food and
drink products.

At the current time very little information is provided to consumers about the impacts of
the food and drink products they buy. Even when there is strong scientific evidence - for
example that processed red meats cause cancer - this is not communicated on
packaging, in contrast to other harmful products like tobacco. Furthermore, many of the
marketing terms used on food and drink products are not standardised (such as ‘natural’
and ‘less dairy’) - and thus can be misleading to consumers.

By introducing a new mandatory system of labelling we will ensure that consumers have
access to the information they need to make more sustainable, healthier and
compassionate choices. This will also support producers who achieve higher standards
and help to drive a broader transformation of food supply chains.

Looking internationally, there are examples of good-practice approaches to labelling that
communicate information to consumers in a simple, transparent and high impact way.
For example in the UK a red-amber-green traffic light system is used to show nutritional
information such as levels of salts, fats and sugars. Research has found that this type of
mandatory labelling can be effective in encouraging healthier consumer choices.30

The AJP believes that consumers have the right to information about the health, animal
welfare and environmental impacts of the food and drink products they buy, and that
standardised, fair and transparent labelling has an important role to play.

Key Objectives:
1. Adopt a nutrition-based traffic-light labelling system for food and drink products.
2. Mandate the use of clear warnings of health risks where robust evidence exists
e.g. cancer warnings on processed red meats as per the World Health
Organisation rankings.31
3. Introduce greenhouse gas emission labelling on all food and drink products.

Introduce plant-based eating initiatives and improve
guidelines

The AJP proposes to launch a range of plant-based, healthy eating initiatives that
reduce harm to animals by promoting the consumption of healthy plant-based foods and
decreasing consumption of animal-based foods. While improving public health is a
valuable outcome, our primary focus is on preventing animal suffering through dietary
change. These initiatives will be based on the best-available science, and communicate
information in ways that promote transparency and inspire change.

New Zealand's food environment poses challenges for promoting healthier, plant-based
choices. While the national dietary guidelines (last updated 2020) make some positive
recommendations around plant proteins and limiting red meat, they maintain a strong
emphasis on dairy products, likely reflecting industry influence. Educational initiatives
like ‘Garden to Table’ demonstrate the potential for teaching children about growing and
preparing fresh food, but operate in only a fraction of schools due to limited government
funding.

Meanwhile, the public faces a constant barrage of meat-heavy fast food marketing, with
major chains dominating the advertising landscape through sports sponsorship, outdoor
advertising and prime-time TV spots. Children in particular are vulnerable targets for
food companies, as they struggle to distinguish marketing from fact. While adults are
also susceptible to marketing influence, children represent both current customers
through their impact on household purchasing decisions and valuable future customers.
Adults ultimately control food budgets, but research shows children significantly
influence family food choices, making them strategic marketing targets despite not
having direct purchasing power themselves.32 The constant flow of advertising makes it
virtually impossible for young people to avoid unhealthy food marketing, effectively
normalising unhealthy consumption patterns.

We propose a three-pronged approach:

First, we will launch public media campaigns to build awareness about the health
benefits of plant-based foods and the animal welfare concerns associated with animal
products. These campaigns will disseminate recipes and information on how to cook
nutritious plant-based meals, highlight ethical issues with animal-based foods, and
reflect the diversity of New Zealand society and current health inequalities.

Second, we will review and update New Zealand’s dietary guidelines to align with the
latest science, including the negative impacts of many animal-based foods. We will
ensure it includes guidelines for wholly plant-based diets and clearly articulates the
evidence of the benefits these can bring. In recent years many countries have amended
their dietary guidelines to increasingly recognise the benefits of shifting away from
traditional meat and dairy heavy diets33. For example, Canada’s 2019 food guide
combined meat into a broader protein category and promoted water over cow’s milk,
and the USA revised it’s dietary guidelines (2025) to list pulses and beans as proteins
and not vegetables.34

Third, we will support educational programmes which increase knowledge and

Introduce tighter standards and enforcement for
agricultural pollution

The AJP seeks to address the environmental harm caused by agricultural practices
while supporting farmers to transition to more sustainable and plant-based alternatives.
Through a combination of regulatory reform, targeted taxation and funding mechanisms,
we will create a path forward that ensures polluters pay for the damage they cause.
However, this will be no substitute for action which other AJP policies will drive in order
to reduce and eliminate the pollution.

Animal farming is a major polluter in Aotearoa, on land and at sea. It is responsible for a
48.6% of our planet-warming greenhouse gas emissions.52 It also causes effluent and
fertiliser run-off which is polluting our seas and rivers, making many of our waterways
unfit for aquatic life as well as for human swimming and drinking. Nutrient enrichment,
from fish farm releases, leads to eutrophication, promoting excessive algae growth that
depletes oxygen levels and harms marine ecosystems. Additionally, animal farming has
resulted in the destruction of much needed habitats for endangered species. This
pollution is allowed to occur largely unchecked. Regulations are insufficiently stringent,
often ignored and rarely enforced.

The huge costs from this pollution are not borne by the polluter - the animal farming
industry - but by nature and society at large. This is in effect a subsidy for one of the
dirtiest industries in the country.

One of the most urgent measures needed is to include agriculture into an Emissions
Trading Scheme (ETS). We would adopt the Climate Change Commission’s advice that
the current NZ ETS should focus on fossil fuel emissions. A separate, new
cap-and-trade ETS for biogenic emissions (including forestry) should be established,
which lowers gross methane emissions to agreed long-term flow.53 This should be
implemented as quickly as possible. We will also set separate gross (total emissions
produced) and net (emissions after offsets like forestry are considered) emissions
reductions targets and reform the ETS to ensure that gross agricultural emissions are
reduced, not simply netted off by forestry plantations.

To address other forms of agricultural pollution such as nitrogen run-off, the AJP
proposes an urgent review and strengthening of current standards such as the National

Become a leader in alternative proteins

The AJP believes Aotearoa can become a world-leader in alternative proteins. The
Government should establish a National Centre for Alternative Proteins and dedicated
funding for research, innovation and commercialisation.

New Zealand must position itself proactively to avoid the economic vulnerability that
historically characterised its response to structural market changes, like the 1960s wool
price shock.68 Rather than treating the growing shift toward plant-based and cellular
agriculture as a temporary disruption, Aotearoa should leverage its existing agricultural
expertise, research capabilities and innovation systems to become a pioneer in this
emerging sector. Funding for alternative proteins grew by 85% in 2024 in Asia-Pacific69,

New Zealand has a long heritage of agricultural innovation and a strong foundation in
agricultural science and research. However at the current time much of this is being
directed towards the ongoing exploitation of animals, for example through the
development of vaccines, new genetic breeds and alternative feeds to reduce ruminant
methane emissions.

There is an opportunity for New Zealand’s science and research community to pivot
towards plant-based solutions that are better for the environment, the climate and
animals. Breaking free from the institutional inertia and path dependence that has
historically favored traditional primary sector activities70 noting New Zealand’s
institutional arrangements tend to favour the primary sector.71

The Vegan Society's Green Protein Report72 highlights that New Zealand has significant
untapped potential for plant protein production. According to the report, transitioning just
15% of animal farming land to plant protein crops could produce the same amount of
protein while reducing land use, greenhouse gas emissions, and water pollution.

Cultivated meat and precision fermentation offer a transformative opportunity to create a
