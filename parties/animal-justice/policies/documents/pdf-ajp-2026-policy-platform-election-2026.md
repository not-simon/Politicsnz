1. Ban the most intensive and harmful forms of
animal farming and fishing
The AJP proposes to implement an absolute ban on the worst animal farming and fishing
practices.

We believe that all forms of animal farming and fishing should be phased out. There are steps
we can take now to eliminate the cruellest and most intensive practices, helping to move
towards our longer term objectives.

New Zealand’s Animal Welfare Act sets out obligations on people in charge of animals
(including farmed animals) to meet an animal’s physical, health and behavioural needs, and to
alleviate pain or distress. However in practice this does not happen. Minimum standards
contained in regulations and Codes of Welfare are inadequate, often out-dated and reviewed
according to the whims of Ministers, and frequently these minimum standards are not met. In
addition, monitoring and enforcement is stretched - the Ministry for Primary Industries (whose
main goals are productivity, export opportunities and food safety1) employs around 30
inspectors for more than 160 million farmed animals.2

We would undertake an urgent review of existing legislation, regulations and Codes of Welfare
and update these to strengthen a wide-range of requirements and minimum standards. This
would result in the following activities being banned:
- Intensive winter grazing (including feedlots and permanent indoor housing systems)
- Breeding fast-growing chickens
- Intensive animal farming
- Live exports and long-haul animal transport
- Mutilations
- Bottom and conventional trawling

For more detail on steps the AJP would take to raise standards for farmed animals, see our
policies on Protecting the Rights of Farmed Animals and Ensuring the Welfare and Protection of
Marine Life.

The AJP believes there is no place in our society for intensive animal farming and industrial
fishing and that these activities should be banned with urgency. This will be a critical step in the
transition to a plant-based economy, and will result in less animal suffering.

Legislative Foundations
To create a comprehensive and effective Animal Protection Policy, a new legislative framework
must be established. This framework should address animal welfare, conservation, ethical
treatment, and the enforcement of laws to ensure that animals are protected from cruelty and
exploitation. Below is a proposed structure for legislative foundations that could guide the
formulation of such a policy:

1. Define animal sentience in legislation
2. Strengthen legal protections recognising animals as sentient beings
3. Propose a new legislative framework with positive welfare obligations
4. Constitutional rights for nature: advocate for legal personhood for ecosystems and species
5. Abolish the legal classification of animals as property and grant animals legal rights

This will be achieved through dialogue, research, analysis and solutions-based reform
proposals to inform legislative and policy change and to enable greater political accountability
and more inclusive and just governance.

Law and policy reforms will be secured to create an animal welfare governance system that is
transparent, in line with contemporary science and public expectations, and focuses on the
wellbeing and protection of animals. These reforms in New Zealand will have the potential to
set a precedent for the creation of large-scale change globally.

Sentience

The acknowledgement of animals as sentient in an amendment to the Animal Welfare Act 1999
(the “Act”) in 2015 was the first of its kind in a common law jurisdiction. However, its location in
the long title is telling – it simply states what the Act is about and is not defined or incorporated
into the offence sections. The New Zealand Government has openly stated it is a “symbolic”
change only and creates no legal or enforceable responsibility.3

Legislation and Regulation Reform for Animal
Protection
AJP supports legislation and regulation that prioritises the well-being, protection and rights of
animals as sentient beings, ensuring they are not subjected to exploitation, harm or cruelty. It is
imperative that animal welfare and protection legislation is adequately resourced and equipped
with robust compliance monitoring, appropriate penalties and regulatory enforcement
capabilities.

Animal welfare legislation should be drafted with the sole intention of recognising animal
sentience and protecting animals, without influence from industries that profit from their
exploitation. The principles enshrined in the Animal Welfare Act 1999 must be enforceable,
without loopholes that allow industries to evade responsibility by self-regulation. Until adequate
animal protection legislation is in place, AJP will also campaign to remove and prohibit
exemptions and inconsistencies that allow animal cruelty to occur (for example, codes of
practice for the “welfare” of farmed animals).

AJP supports a legal framework that reflects a societal commitment to valuing, protecting and
respecting animals, fostering responsible and compassionate treatment in all contexts and
industries. Without stringent, and enforceable, legal protection, animals remain vulnerable to
cruelty and exploitation, their welfare jeopardised by profit-driven motives. Empowering and
resourcing authorities to share information and enforce legislation and regulations is essential,
ensuring individuals and organisations are held accountable for infringements. Championing
animal-centric legislation paves the way to a more just and humane society where positive
animal welfare4 is legally recognised and animals are granted by the law the dignity and
protection they deserve.

Animal welfare has become a global concern, which first requires adequate regulation at a
national level.

There is an urgent need to overhaul New Zealand’s animal welfare governance system to
enable a paradigm shift in the value of animals and human responsibility towards them.5 It is
crucial that improved animal welfare and protection is prioritised by legislative reform and
strengthened regulatory enforcement to avoid ongoing exploitation and harm at human hands.

Purpose and Objectives of the Animal Protection Policy

AJP advocates for a new legal framework that recognises animals' right to live without suffering
harm from humans and ensures that their lives and well-being are protected by the
advancement of New Zealand’s legal system.

The core purpose of the animal protection policy is:

- To safeguard the welfare and well-being of animals
- To promote humane treatment of animals in all sectors (agriculture, entertainment,
research, etc.)
- To reduce instances of animal cruelty, neglect, and exploitation
- To foster public awareness and ethical responsibility regarding animal rights
- To protect endangered species and their habitats

AJP opposes all exploitation of animals and advocates for their ethical treatment in all
human-animal interactions. We stand against all practices that subject animals to stress and/or
harm. Our vision is for a society that places value on the well-being of animals and prohibits all
practices that exploit or harm them.

Our advocacy entails the development and promotion of policies that end harmful practices and
legally protect animals. Holistically, AJP policies promote a vision of a world where animals can
thrive in ways that respect their inherent needs and behaviours.

3. AJP Overview for a Plant-Based Economic
Transition
The AJP is committed to transitioning Aotearoa New Zealand to a wholly plant-based economy,
where animal farming has been eliminated and no animals are used for economic gain. We
acknowledge the role of Māori as tangata whenua (people of the land) and recognise the
importance of kaitiakitanga, manaakitanga, and tino rangatiratanga in shaping a just transition.
This transformation must be guided by partnership with iwi and hapū, respecting mātauranga
Māori and supporting Māori economic aspirations through sustainable land-use practices. We
want to create a more prosperous, sustainable and secure economy that is underpinned by
compassion for all.

A transformation approach is required. We recognise that our current economic model is deeply
reliant on the exploitation of animals for food, other products and exports. And that for many
New Zealanders, including both Māori and Pākehā, have strong cultural and historical ties to
animal farming. For Māori, these ties are often rooted in whakapapa and a deep ancestral
relationship with the land, while for Pākehā, the connection has developed more recently
through settler-colonial history and generations of farming. Understanding these different
histories is essential as we navigate a just transition away from animal-based systems.

However, our current agricultural model has significant environmental impacts, including
biodiversity loss, freshwater degradation and greenhouse gas emissions, while also inflicting
unnecessary suffering on millions of sentient beings. It is also inherently risky as an on-going
economic strategy, as environmental tipping points, new technologies, consumer preferences,
societal concerns and changes in global trade threaten to reduce long term demand for animal
products alongside increasing regulatory burdens. New Zealand is currently in a ‘chaos and
emergence’ phase where existing systems are being questioned and new alternatives are
emerging.9

We appreciate transitioning to a plant-based economy will take time, and that to be sustainable
it must happen alongside support for farmers and the development of attractive and affordable
alternatives for both farmers and consumers alike. There are, however, steps we can take now
that will make a huge positive impact. These include banning factory farming, tougher laws,
regulations and enforcement mechanisms, supporting farm livelihood transitions, engaging with
Māori leadership, supporting innovation in food tech, and promoting and incentivising more
compassionate and healthy dietary choices. He ara kai pai ake mō te tinana, te wairua me te
taiao (healthier and more sustainable food pathways for the body, spirit, and environment).

Shifting to a plant-based economy is the single most impactful change we can make to reduce
animal suffering whilst also addressing many of our most urgent economic, social and

Four overarching objectives
Our policy for transitioning to a plant-based economy is focussed on shifting away from animal
agriculture and animal-based foods, and replacing these with sustainable and compassionate
alternatives. It is centered around four objectives, each supported by specific proposals:

1. Increase consumption of plant-based foods. Use a variety of policy levers to increase
consumption and market demand for healthier, kinder, more sustainable food options that
align with kai ora (healthy food) principles

2. Use land and water sustainably and compassionately. Use regulation and incentives
to shift from animal to plant-based farming and other sustainable land and water uses.

3. Support farmers, workers and communities to transition. Engage and collaborate to
support a fair and inclusive transition with economic opportunities for all.

4. Develop long-term economic alternatives. Invest in new technologies, products and
markets- to replace animal farming and provide prosperity and jobs.

To achieve these objectives, the Government should urgently develop a National Action Plan to
implement the proposals set out in this document. While animal agriculture currently remains
economically significant, there are both environmental and market pressures that suggest the

national priority and the Government must play a critical role in making this happen.

The ‘value’ of animal exploitation to New Zealand’s economy
Despite its well documented negative impacts, animal agriculture occupies a prominent position
in New Zealand's economy and national identity. While agriculture is often described as the
foundation of New Zealand's economic prosperity, contributing to GDP, exports and
employment, the combined agricultural and fishing sectors represent approximately just 5% of
GDP.
The dairy sector accounts for 3% of this (>$11BN) and provides an estimated 55,000 jobs.
Dried milk powder is one of our most significant export commodities, with the dairy export trade
earning around $1 in every $4 of New Zealand’s foreign exchange income.10 However, the
expansion of dairy farming has led to economic vulnerabilities, including significant trade
imbalances and a high reliance on imports. It has also resulted in environmental challenges,
particularly regarding water quality.
Shifting away from these industries will be a significant challenge. New Zealand's agricultural
identity, established during colonisation as the dairy farm of the British Empire, continues to
shape its global economic position and national politics. A just transition requires redressing
these injustices by prioritising Māori leadership in land-use decision-making, ensuring economic
benefits are equitably distributed, and honoring Māori aspirations for self-determined food and
land systems. The close alignment between agricultural and state interests has historically been

Impacts on animals
Millions of farmed animals are slaughtered in New Zealand every year. Data from StatsNZ
reveals that around 27 million ‘livestock’ were killed in 2023, including 21 million lambs and
sheep, 4.6 million cows and 600,000 pigs, heifers, bulls, steers and calves. In addition, it is
estimated that 120 million chickens are slaughtered annually.12 Between 953 million to 5.94
billion wild-fish were killed for food (2016, Our World in Data), this estimate does not include
unrecorded fish capture, such as bycatch and discards. Farmed fish, including salmon, mussels
and oysters are killed in their hundreds of millions annually.
However, the impact on farmed animals cannot be captured by numbers alone. Poor conditions
and cruel practices are widespread. New Zealand’s animal welfare laws are inadequate even
when they are adhered to, but in practice we know that legal minimum standards are often not
met - with negative consequences for the sentient beings that are powerless to resist. Whilst
high welfare standards are better than low / no standards, ultimately the only ethically justifiable
course of action is to phase out animal farming altogether.

Impacts on human health

The rise in consumption of animal products over the past decades correlates with increasing
rates of chronic disease in New Zealand and globally. Research demonstrates clear
associations between high consumption of animal products and adverse health outcomes.
Specifically, red and processed meat consumption is linked to increased risk of colorectal
cancer, with the World Health Organisation classifying processed meat as a Group 1
carcinogen, alongside alcohol and tobacco.18 High consumption of animal products is
associated with increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases, certain cancers, and all-cause
mortality.19
Conversely, scientific evidence consistently shows that plant-based diets play both preventative
and therapeutic roles in managing major chronic conditions including cardiovascular disease,
type 2 diabetes, obesity, and certain cancers. Studies indicate that replacing animal-based
foods (such as red meat, processed meat, eggs, and dairy) with plant-based alternatives
(including legumes, nuts, whole grains, and olive oil) improves cardiometabolic health
outcomes.20
Plant-based diets are demonstrated to be safe and nutritionally adequate across all life stages,
from pregnancy and lactation through to elderly care, when appropriately planned.. Beyond
individual health benefits, transitioning toward plant-based agriculture could significantly reduce
the growing burden on our healthcare system. Shifting toward predominantly plant-based diets
is not only feasible but could substantially improve public health outcomes, reduce healthcare

Ensure fair and transparent labelling
The AJP proposes to mandate fair and transparent food labelling that provides information
about the environmental, health and animal welfare impacts of food and drink products.

At the current time very little information is provided to consumers about the impacts of the food
and drink products they buy. Even when there is strong scientific evidence - for example that

other harmful products like tobacco. Furthermore, many of the marketing terms used on food
and drink products are not standardised (such as ‘natural’ and ‘less dairy’) - and thus can be
misleading to consumers.

By introducing a new mandatory system of labelling we will ensure that consumers have access
to the information they need to make more sustainable, healthier and compassionate choices.

transformation of food supply chains.

such as levels of salts, fats and sugars. Research has found that this type of mandatory
labelling can be effective in encouraging healthier consumer choices.38

The AJP believes that consumers have the right to information about the health, animal welfare
and environmental impacts of the food and drink products they buy, and that standardised, fair
and transparent labelling has an important role to play.

3. Introduce greenhouse gas emission labelling on all food and drink products.
4. Develop standardised definitions and require mandatory labelling for terms such as

5. Ban the use of misleading terms such as ‘less dairy’.

The AJP proposes to improve access to nutritious food for all New Zealanders through a
package of policy measures. While improving access to healthy food is a valuable outcome, our
primary focus is on preventing animal suffering through dietary change. These measures will
reduce costs, improve access to plant-based foods, support communities to take a lead role,

Food access inequities exist across Aotearoa, disproportionately affecting Māori communities.46
Lower-income areas often have a higher concentration of fast food outlets and fewer fresh food
retailers, while transport barriers and distance to supermarkets create additional challenges,
particularly in rural areas.

Many communities rely on local dairies (convenience stores) which typically stock limited fresh
food at higher prices. These access issues are compounded by the high cost of healthy food.
Fresh fruit and vegetables are notably expensive with significant seasonal price fluctuations.

requires a substantial portion of a low-income household's budget, while processed and
energy-dense foods remain cheaper per calorie than fresh, nutritious options.

2023/24, 47.1% adults ate the recommended amount of fruit and only 9.1% ate the

2023/24 rates of food insecurity were higher in households where children were Māori, Pacific,
disabled, or living in the most deprived neighbourhoods.51

The AJP will support more sustainable and compassionate uses of land and water through

ecosystems, enhance food security and provide nutrient-dense foods.
- Payments for farmers who adopt practices that restore ecosystems and natural habitats,

build natural flood defences and resilience to climate change.
- Auditing and enforcement to ensure practices are enduring.

7. Universal Veterinary Care - Ensuring
Wellbeing for All Animals

The AJP is committed to establishing veterinary care as a fundamental right for all animals,
recognising that access to medical treatment should never depend on their guardian's wealth or
their status as companion, free-living, or wildlife. We acknowledge that the current system fails
countless animals who suffer needlessly due to financial barriers, geographic inequities, and a
veterinary workforce pushed to breaking point by commercial pressures and inadequate
support. We propose comprehensive reforms to create a universal veterinary care system that
prioritises animal wellbeing over profit, ensuring no animal is denied treatment because of
economic circumstances.

The veterinary care crisis in Aotearoa reflects broader societal failures to value animal lives
equally. Countless companion animals live in households struggling with the cost of living,
where a sudden illness or injury forces impossible choices between veterinary bills and basic
necessities. Meanwhile, injured wildlife, including our precious native species, suffer without
treatment, and free-living cats and dogs continue breeding due to lack of accessible desexing
services, perpetuating cycles of suffering that overwhelm rescue organisations and community
groups.

This crisis is not inevitable, it is a direct consequence of treating veterinary medicine as a luxury
commodity rather than essential care. The current system concentrates services in wealthy
urban areas while rural and regional communities face severe shortages, leaving both animals
and their human carers without local access to critical treatment. Veterinarians themselves
suffer under this model, with crushing student debt, unsustainable workloads, and moral injury
from having to deny care based on payment capacity driving concerning rates of burnout and
mental health struggles within the profession.

The economic barriers are deliberately constructed. GST on veterinary services treats animal
healthcare as discretionary spending, while limited university places and high fees create
artificial scarcity that drives up costs and restricts workforce growth. Emergency procedures can
cost thousands of dollars, forcing people to choose between insurmountable debt or
euthanasia, while preventative care like desexing remains financially out of reach for many
households, particularly in communities already facing economic hardship.

The connection to animal justice is fundamental and inseparable. Every animal who dies from a
treatable condition because their carer couldn't afford treatment represents a failure of our
society's values. Every injured kererū, kākā, or kiwi who suffers without veterinary intervention
because there's no guaranteed funding for wildlife care reveals the hollowness of our claimed
concern for biodiversity and our taonga species. Every free-living cat or dog who continues
breeding because desexing is unaffordable perpetuates suffering across generations and
threatens our native wildlife.

and introduced species control in Aotearoa New Zealand. While AI technologies offer potential
to improve animal welfare through enhanced monitoring and early disease detection, they also
risk entrenching harmful practices and creating new forms of harm if deployed without adequate
governance systems.

AJP does not endorse the continued expansion of animal agriculture. Any use of AI in these
systems must demonstrate responsible development and genuine prioritisation of animal
welfare and harm minimisation. We oppose AI technology systems designed purely for
economic efficiency that treat animals as units of production rather than as sentient individuals
with inherent value.

AJP calls for immediate integration of animal welfare and protection considerations into AI
governance frameworks before harmful practices become economically entrenched and
impossible to reform. The long-term policy direction of AI must prioritise transition away from
animal farming, not the technological optimisation of it.

Context
The Scale of AI Deployment
AI technologies are already deployed across Aotearoa New Zealand's animal management
systems at unprecedented scale. Over 400,000 cattle are currently managed using AI-powered
virtual fencing systems, whilst millions more animals in dairy, beef, sheep, chicken, aquaculture
systems and introduced species control are increasingly likely to be subject to AI-driven
management decisions.
AI systems are being rapidly integrated into the management of these animals, making
autonomous decisions about their welfare, health and lives.

Current AI applications include:
- Virtual fencing and herding: AI-integrated wearable technology using sounds and
vibration to control animal movement, with systems learning and predicting individual
behaviour patterns
- Slaughterhouse automation, Computer vision, machine learning and robotics for carcass
grading, meat inspection, automated restraint, and cutting, optimising yield and
throughput.
- Health monitoring: Computer vision and machine learning analysing gait patterns, body
condition and behaviour to assess welfare
- Automated feeding and milking: AI-optimised systems managing nutrition and dairy
production
- Aquaculture management: Machine learning for biomass measurement, feeding
optimisation and water quality monitoring

---

- Introduced species control: AI-powered cameras in traps using species recognition, and
autonomous drones identifying and poisoning target animals

The Regulatory Gap
The Animal Welfare Act 1999 was enacted before AI technology existed and contains no
provisions for AI-driven animal management and welfare. This creates a critical regulatory time
lag where technology is reshaping human-animal relationships faster than legislation and
regulation can adapt.

Aotearoa New Zealand's AI Strategy (July 2025), positions agriculture as a key adoption sector
but contains no animal welfare considerations. The accompanying Responsible AI Guidance for
Businesses covers cybersecurity, privacy and intellectual property whilst omitting animal welfare
entirely. The Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment has been explicit that “unlike EU
regulatory approaches, our approach is dispersed. The Government doesn't intend to put in
place an overarching AI Act. AI risks should be managed in relevant legislation as they arise”
(email correspondence Oct 2025).

This dispersed “light touch” approach is already being implemented in other sectors. 2025
changes to the Commerce Act, address AI-facilitated anti-competitive conduct, ensuring
companies are liable for AI actions. Additionally, a Members Bill was introduced (2025) to
amend the Harmful Digital Communications and Crimes Act 1961 to address AI-generated
harms such as deepfakes. These examples confirm that rapid, targeted legislative response is
possible.

Animal welfare requires the same urgency. Without explicit action to integrate welfare
considerations into relevant existing Acts, AI governance will continue to treat animal welfare as
a low-priority externality rather than a core consideration.

Without explicit regulatory requirements, AI systems will inevitably be designed solely for
economic efficiency rather than animal welfare and protection. Market forces alone cannot
ensure welfare and safety outcomes; only regulation can mandate that AI development
prioritises animals' behavioural, physical and mental needs rather than subordinating them to
economic and productivity metrics.

Case Study: Virtual Fencing Regulatory Lag
Virtual fencing technology demonstrates the pattern of regulatory lag that affects AI systems
deployment. Despite operating commercially since at least 2020 and affecting hundreds of
thousands of cattle:

- Circa 2021: The National Animal Welfare Advisory Committee (NAWAC) recommended
safeguards for virtual fencing in its Code of Welfare Evaluation Report on Dairy Cattle,
stating these "should have more generic application than only virtual fencing and apply to
any future technology proposed for animal management"
- Early 2024: A petition called for a parliamentary inquiry into virtual fencing technology

animaljustice.org.nz | party@animaljusticeparty.org.nz 56

---

- December 2024: Veterinarians for Animal Welfare Aotearoa announced development of a
code of ethical conduct for virtual fencing, demonstrating industry concern whilst
government action remained pending
- April 2025: The Petitions Committee recommended inclusion of guidance in the Code of
Welfare: Dairy Cattle update
- July 2025: The government noted that virtual fencing presents "an opportunity to review
the standards" but provided no timeline for implementation
- Late 2025: NAWAC's work programme plans to provide a revised recommendation, with
no certainty about when actual regulatory guidance will be implemented

This five-year lag between commercial deployment and potential regulatory guidance illustrates
the systematic failure to apply anticipatory regulation principles to animal welfare contexts,
despite on-going advocacy by the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (SPCA).
Without proactive intervention, each new AI technology will follow this same pattern: commercial
deployment, widespread adoption, entrenchment in industry practice, then belated regulatory
consideration of animal welfare and safety.

Global Context and Market Pressures
Globally, most AI governance frameworks have overlooked animal welfare. The OECD's AI
Principles and most national AI strategies focus exclusively on human-centred concerns such
as privacy, bias, transparency, data integrity, accountability and safety.

However, a significant breakthrough occurred in July 2025, when the EU's General-Purpose AI
Code of Practice explicitly listed “risk to non-human welfare” as a systemic risk, alongside
threats to democratic processes, financial stability and environmental harm. This represents the
first time a major jurisdiction has formally recognised that AI governance must consider animal
welfare, achieved through successful advocacy by animal welfare organisations during the
drafting stage.

Beyond the EU, Serbia's AI framework explicitly states AI systems “must be in line with the
well-being of humans, animals, and the environment.” These examples demonstrate that
inclusion of animal welfare in AI governance is both achievable and is increasingly being
recognised as necessary.

The AI animal health market is projected to grow from approximately $1.4 billion in 2024 to over
$8 billion by 2034, with the farmed animal sector expected to see the fastest growth. This rapid
investment is occurring without systematic regulatory oversight of welfare outcomes, creating
substantial risk that AI systems will entrench rather than improve current harmful practices.

Welfare Risks of Unregulated AI
- Intensification of harmful practices: AI technology makes existing harmful practices
more efficient rather than more humane. It integrates best with intensive, cage-based
systems that have the worst welfare conditions, enabling higher stocking densities and
more intensive confinement, whilst appearing to ensure individualised care.

animaljustice.org.nz | party@animaljusticeparty.org.nz 57

---

- Economic prioritisation: Data collection prioritises economic metrics over welfare
indicators. AI systems optimise measurable parameters (e.g. milk yield, growth rate, feed
conversion) whilst potentially missing qualitative welfare aspects such as emotional
wellbeing, behavioural freedom and social relationships.
- Reduced human oversight: Fewer human caregivers enable larger-scale operations,
removing human-animal relationships and reducing opportunities for animals to receive
individualised attention and compassionate care.
- Degraded human-animal relationship: humans become algorithm supervisors rather
than carers risking moral injury and loss of empathy. Sentient beings are viewed as data
streams.
- Technological dependency: System failures could leave animals without care. Farms
become vulnerable to hacking, sabotage and corporate espionage. Adverse weather
conditions may impact camera-based systems.
- Welfare washing: AI systems create the appearance of individualised care whilst
facilitating intensive systems. Companies can claim “precision welfare” or “AI-monitored
health” whilst maintaining fundamentally harmful practices.
- Automated lethal control: AI-powered introduced species control technologies operate
without direct human oversight of individual animal deaths, making life-or-death decisions
autonomously. This raises serious ethical questions about delegating such decisions to
algorithms designed for efficiency rather than compassion.

Economic Scale and Digital Divide
High AI technology implementation costs will create a digital divide where smaller operations
cannot access technologies that larger commercial operations use for both efficiency and
welfare monitoring. This risks creating a two-tiered system where animal welfare outcomes
depend on economic scale rather than consistent regulatory standards, with larger operations
able to demonstrate compliance through AI systems monitoring, whilst smaller farms lack
resources for equivalent compliance systems.

The Window for Action
Despite AI technology deployment already underway across Aotearoa New Zealand's animal
management systems, the window for proactive policy intervention still remains open. While
systems already affect hundreds of thousands of animals, harmful practices have not yet
become so economically entrenched that reform is impossible.

The government has demonstrated capacity for rapid legislative response when circumstances
require it. In May 2025, Parliament passed the Wildlife (Authorisations) Amendment Bill under
urgency to clarify authority for killing protected wildlife during development projects, responding
within months to a High Court decision. This swift action demonstrates that regulatory speed
depends on political prioritisation rather than legislative capacity.

10. Companion Animal Bereavement and
Parental Leave

The AJP is committed to recognising the profound emotional bonds between humans and
companion animals, acknowledging that these relationships provide immense psychological,
emotional and social benefits that mirror many aspects of human family connections. We
acknowledge that current employment law fails to recognise the grief and care needs
associated with companion animals, forcing people to use sick leave or holiday entitlement
during some of their most vulnerable moments.

We propose establishing statutory rights to bereavement and parental leave for companion
animal guardians. Leave entitlements should be flexible blocks that employees can use to care
for who they consider family, whether parents, children, or companion animals. This approach
avoids additional administrative burden for businesses while reflecting the reality of modern
family life.

Half of pet owners say their animal is a member of the family, equal to humans, reflecting the
deep emotional connections that have developed over thousands of years of co-evolution.
Anyone who has ever loved a pet knows: they're not "just animals" - they're family. They're the
wagging tail that greets us after a hard day, the purring companion during late-night work
sessions, and the loyal friend who never judges our worst moments.

Some forward-thinking employers have already recognised this reality. Google, Starbucks,
United Airlines and Walmart have embraced a so-called "pet-conscious workplace",107 while pet
ownership surged during the pandemic,108 and more workplaces have become pet-friendly.109
The concept of "pawternity leave", time off (paid or unpaid) for staff who have recently brought
home a pet, is gaining recognition internationally.110

Currently, pet leave isn't a legal right, but some businesses offer it.111 This creates inequality
where workers in progressive companies receive support while others must navigate grief and
companion animal care using inappropriate leave categories or risk job security. The absence of
statutory rights means many people cannot properly grieve the loss of beloved companions or
adequately care for newly adopted animals during critical bonding periods.

Policy Framework

- Bereavement Leave

 - Up to 5 days paid leave available as a single entitlement for use following the
death of a close human family member or a companion animal.
 - Flexible timing to cover funerals, tangihanga, memorials, veterinary appointments,
or grieving.
 - Self-declaration only, no burdensome paperwork.

- Parental (“Pawternity”) Leave

 - Up to 3 days paid leave available when a new family member joins the household,
whether a baby or an adopted companion animal.
 - Additional unpaid leave (up to 2 weeks) available for animals with special needs or
trauma histories.

- Emergency Care Leave

 - Employees may take reasonable time off to respond to urgent medical
emergencies, end-of-life decision-making, or intensive care needs - for human or
animal family members.

This policy reflects our core belief that recognising the sentience and emotional value of animals
creates a more compassionate society overall. When we acknowledge the grief people feel for
companion animals, we validate the emotional lives of those animals and reinforce that all
beings deserve consideration and respect.

The AJP believes that establishing companion animal bereavement and parental leave is a
compassionate step that can be taken now to support the millions of New Zealanders who
share their lives with animal family members. This policy will reduce workplace stress, improve
employee wellbeing, and strengthen a compassionate culture in Aotearoa New Zealand.

Key Objectives:

1. Establish statutory bereavement leave (up to 5 days) for family members, human or
nonhuman animals
2. Create "pawternity leave" entitlement of up to 3 days for new family members, including
companion animals
3. Extend emergency leave provisions to cover urgent care for both human and nonhuman
family
4. Ensure leave entitlements apply equally to all recognised forms of family life.
