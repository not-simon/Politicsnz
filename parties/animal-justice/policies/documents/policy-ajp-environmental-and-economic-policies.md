Urgently addressing Climate Change
The AJP is the only political party in Aotearoa New Zealand with a serious commitment to
tackling major sources of emissions in ways which are credible, compassionate and aligned
with climate science. We support a rapid reduction in animal agriculture (which is
responsible for around half of Aotearoa New Zealand’s emissions) by transitioning to a
plant-based economy, supporting farmers to grow produce instead of farming animals and
addressing the profoundly detrimental impact of climate change on animals. Additionally,
animals are extremely vulnerable to the storms, flooding and fires we continue to see in
Aotearoa New Zealand as a result of climate change.
We support policies that reduce emissions, avoid unnecessary animal suffering, improve
human and non-human animal health, and restore and expand our waterways, forests and
wildlife. We reject ineffective solutions that aim to perpetuate the status quo. We are
committed to supporting animals of all kinds to adapt to the changes in climate that are
already underway, and that which are predicted, in order to create a sustainable future
Core Principle
Climate change is a symptom of the metacrisis, a planetary emergency requiring a
whole-of-government response that addresses root causes, not symptoms. We must move
beyond the failed approach of leaving token climate action to environment ministries while
economic policy continues business-as-usual. This systemic approach protects animals by
preventing the habitat destruction, ocean acidification, extreme weather events, and
ecosystem collapse driven by climate change, while ending the economic pressures that
force expansion of industrial animal agriculture and fossil fuel extraction into remaining
wildlife territories.

Climate leadership
The AJP calls for climate leadership that protects sentient beings. Climate change threatens
all life in Aotearoa and across the Pacific as well as globally. Effective climate leadership
must embed sentient animal protection into all domestic frameworks, working to prevent
further climate change while strengthening enforcement and accountability. Climate risk
assessments and adaptation plans must explicitly consider the welfare of animals and the
health of ecosystems, alongside human infrastructure and communities, ensuring that policy
decisions reduce harm and build ecological resilience.
New Zealand has a responsibility to partner with Pacific Island nations as tangata whenua of
Te Moana-nui-a-Kiwa. Supporting Pacific-led solutions, including climate-safe migration
pathways and sanctuary networks, protects both human and non-human communities.
Ending fossil fuel financing, intensive agriculture, and creating regional climate coalitions
ensures biodiversity, marine life and habitats are safeguarded. By linking domestic climate
action with regional cooperation, New Zealand can lead in protecting animals, ecosystems
and communities from the escalating impacts of climate change.
Key Objectives
1. Enforce robust National Adaptation Plan commitments, with binding timelines and
penalties for departments that fail to protect animals and habitats.
2. Extend domestic climate risk assessments to regional impacts, ensuring wildlife,
marine ecosystems and nonhuman animals are considered in partnership with
Pacific Island nations.
3. Prioritise climate partnerships with Pacific Island nations, recognising their role as
tangata whenua and stewards of marine and terrestrial biodiversity.
4. Support Pacific-led solutions to climate displacement, including pathways that protect
both human and wildlife communities.
5. Expand climate risk assessments to consider ecological and animal welfare impacts
alongside human infrastructure and health.
6. Lead regional coalitions to end fossil fuel financing, intensive agriculture and
establish Pacific climate sanctuary networks protecting biodiversity, marine life and
ecosystems.

Reducing Climate-Harming Emissions Energy
The AJP recognises that reducing energy demand is the most immediate way to protect
animals and ecosystems. Lowering consumption and promoting shared resource solutions
reduces pressure on natural habitats, limits pollution and mitigates climate-driven disasters
that harm nonhuman animals.
Fossil fuels are a major driver of habitat destruction, pollution and climate change. Urgent
action to eliminate fossil fuels and accelerate renewable energy will further safeguard
animals while building resilient, community-led energy systems.
Intensive animal agriculture is one of New Zealand's largest sources of greenhouse gas
emissions, producing significant methane and nitrous oxide while causing direct suffering to
farmed animals. Reducing intensive farming operations addresses both climate impacts and
animal welfare.
Transport emissions continue to rise, driven by fossil fuel dependence and ongoing road
expansion that fragments wildlife habitats. Shifting to public and active transport reduces
emissions while protecting ecosystems from further development pressures.
Key Objectives
1. Reduce overall energy use and promote shared resource solutions, lowering
pressure on habitats and animals.
2. Reverse the lifting of the offshore exploration ban and reinstate the moratorium on
new fossil fuel permits, preventing habitat destruction and pollution.
3. Phase out existing fossil fuel infrastructure on a strict timeline, cutting emissions and
climate-related harm to animals.
4. End government underwriting and tax incentives for fossil fuel exploration,
particularly the current government's moves to subsidise offshore oil and gas
exploration
5. Set legally binding targets for 100% renewable electricity, reducing greenhouse gas
emissions that threaten biodiversity.
6. Fast-track decentralised, community-owned renewable energy projects, compatible
with wildlife corridors and urban green spaces.
7. Address emissions from intensive animal farming, see AJP Transition to a
plant-based economy policies for comprehensive agriculture reform measures.
8. Accelerate the transition to zero-emission public and active transport.
9. Prioritise rail and public transport infrastructure over new road construction.

Climate Change Adaptation
The AJP calls for sentient animal protection to be embedded at the heart of all climate
adaptation, land-use and emergency management decisions to safeguard lives, maintain
ecosystem resilience and create communities that thrive for all species.
Climate change is already causing harm to wild living animals, farmed animals and
companion animals across Aotearoa. Yet current adaptation and emergency planning
prioritises humans, leaving many sentient beings vulnerable to extreme weather, habitat loss
and resource scarcity. Protecting animals is an ethical imperative and essential for resilient
ecosystems.
Key Objectives
1. Mandate animal protection in all climate adaptation plans, requiring dedicated,
funded and measurable chapters on wildlife and domestic/farmed animals.
2. Require Sentient Animal Risk Assessments (SARA) for all major infrastructure and
land-use projects, assessing climate-related risks to nearby animal populations and
enforcing mitigation strategies.
3. Integrate animal welfare into emergency management, embedding rescue teams,
climate-resilient shelters and evacuation protocols for wildlife, farmed and companion
animals.
4. Establish rapid-response emergency systems for all nonhuman animals, including
flood, fire, drought and storm events, and fund community-based preparedness
programs.
5. Create climate refuges and urban wildlife safety networks, including habitat corridors,
heat refuges and stormwater management that protects native species.
6. Provide dedicated climate-resilience funding and incentives for initiatives that reduce
animal suffering and protect biodiversity during extreme weather events.
7. Fund research and monitoring programs to identify high-risk species and regions,
informing climate adaptation and emergency responses.
8. Implement cross-sector coordination mechanisms, linking environmental, agriculture,
urban planning and emergency agencies to integrate animal-focused adaptation
strategies at all levels.

Protecting Ecosystems and Biodiversity
The AJP envisions a world where ecosystems thrive, and biodiversity flourishes, fostering
harmonious coexistence between humans and other animals. We aspire to a future where
habitats are safeguarded, species abound, and the delicate balance of our planet’s natural
systems endures for generations. The undeniable detrimental impact of animal agriculture
and the current management of biodiversity and habitats necessitates urgent action.
We advocate for a paradigm shift in agriculture towards efficient and productive land use
without farming animals. This not only reduces ecological footprints but also creates
opportunities for rewilding farmland, allowing natural ecosystems to flourish, and carbon
sequestration. We support compassionate conservation measures, habitat restoration, and
the implementation of policies to mitigate the impact of pollutants and pesticides, including
ongoing and emerging environmental challenges.
In honouring the Māori worldview, we embrace the concept of Kaitiakitanga, recognising our
responsibility as guardians of the land. We look back to traditional practices that emphasise
respect for nature and integrate these principles as we seek to preserve biodiversity and
also respect the cultural connection between humans, non-human animals and the
environment. In doing so, we aim to forge a sustainable path forward, where ecosystems are
safeguarded, and the richness of biodiversity is cherished and protected.

Comprehensive Waste Minimisation at Source
The AJP is committed to eliminating waste creation at the source rather than managing
waste streams after environmental damage has already occurred. We acknowledge that
current approaches focus on end-of-pipe solutions while ignoring the resource extraction and
manufacturing processes that destroy wildlife habitats to create products destined to become
waste.

Every product manufactured requires raw materials extracted from ecosystems where
animals live. Mining operations destroy underground habitats for countless invertebrates and
soil organisms as well as the ecosystems at the surface. Forestry operations fragment and
destroy territories used by native birds, bats and ground-dwelling species. Manufacturing
processes consume enormous quantities of water that animals depend upon whilst
generating toxic byproducts that contaminate the environments where wildlife must survive.

The most effective way to protect animal habitats is to dramatically reduce the volume of
products manufactured in the first place. When we require manufacturers to design out
waste, extend product lifespans and take responsibility for entire product lifecycles, we
directly reduce the pressure on natural systems that sustain wildlife populations.

The AJP prioritises eliminating waste at its source to protect wildlife habitats and reduce
resource extraction. Every tonne of waste not created avoids trucks driving through wildlife
corridors, prevents processing facilities from being built on productive land and keeps
disposal sites from contaminating watersheds that native species rely on. By requiring
products to be designed for longevity, repairability and responsible end-of-life management,
these policies minimise environmental harm while keeping recovery systems as a back-up
for what cannot be designed out.

Key Objectives:

1. Ban single-use products where reusable alternatives exist, reducing demand for
resource extraction and habitat destruction
2. Mandate modular and repairable product design standards, including minimum
lifespan guarantees, spare parts availability, and legal rights to repair, eliminating
planned obsolescence.
3. Establish producer responsibility schemes requiring manufacturers to fund collection,
processing, and safe disposal of all products they place on the market
4. Impose penalties on companies whose products contaminate ecosystems or cause
wildlife mortality, internalising environmental and social costs
5. Require large businesses to report annually on material flows, meet binding
waste-reduction targets, and comply with resource efficiency standards

Single-Use Plastic Elimination
The AJP is committed to comprehensive legislation eliminating single-use plastics and
implementing circular packaging systems that protect marine life from the devastating
impacts of plastic pollution. We acknowledge that current voluntary approaches have failed
catastrophically, with New Zealand generating 252,000 tonnes of plastic waste annually2
whilst marine ecosystems face unprecedented contamination threatening the survival of
numerous species.

Plastic pollution kills over one million seabirds and 100,000 marine mammals globally each
year.3 In New Zealand waters, pilot whales, dolphins, and seals regularly die from plastic
ingestion whilst microplastics contaminate the entire marine food web. Every piece of
single-use plastic produced will persist in the environment for centuries, continuing to
fragment into smaller pieces that marine animals cannot avoid consuming.

Current recycling systems are inadequate, with much of New Zealand's plastic waste
exported overseas or sent to landfill. Only 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled,
whilst 12% has been incinerated and 79% remains in landfills or the environment.4 Voluntary
corporate commitments have delivered minimal progress whilst plastic production continues
accelerating globally.

Successful legislative approaches demonstrate alternatives work. The EU's Single-Use
Plastics Directive banned numerous problematic items whilst Rwanda's plastic bag ban
created thousands of jobs in alternative industries. Countries implementing comprehensive
plastic reduction policies consistently achieve dramatic pollution reductions whilst fostering
innovation in sustainable packaging.

The AJP believes that mandatory single-use plastic elimination is a critical first step in
source minimisation. Eliminating unnecessary single-use plastics tackles one of the most
harmful and avoidable waste streams, while recovery and reuse systems manage what
cannot be avoided. This approach protects marine ecosystems, creates opportunities for
innovative packaging solutions, prevents countless plastic-related deaths of marine animals,
reduces toxic contamination of seafood, and establishes New Zealand as a global leader in
marine protection.

Key Objectives:

1. Ban all non-essential single-use plastics including bags, straws, utensils, and food
containers within three years
2. Mandate producer responsibility schemes requiring manufacturers to fund collection
and processing of plastic packaging waste
3. Invest in reusable packaging infrastructure and refill systems that eliminate
disposable containers

Comprehensive Zero Waste Systems
The AJP is committed to implementing nationwide zero-waste infrastructure that eliminates
the environmental degradation caused by landfills and ocean pollution—protecting marine
life, native species, and ecosystems from the devastating impacts of waste. We recognise
that waste management systems are merely end-of-pipe solutions that address only part of a
system-wide problem that needs tackling at the start of the pipe, by reducing waste
production in the first place. We acknowledge that current consumer production systems that
produce so much waste are failing both our communities and our wildlife, with many councils
sending 60-100% of recycling to landfill5 whilst 4.6 million drink containers are littered or
landfilled daily.6 Much more litter is also uncollected as part of any disposal process or
scheme.

Ocean plastic kills over 100,000 marine mammals annually worldwide,7 whilst poorly
managed landfills leach toxins into waterways that native fish and birds depend upon. Every
piece of waste that is not produced or avoids landfill or ocean dumping represents habitat
preserved and wildlife protected. Container deposit return schemes, successfully operating
in 57 countries, achieve recycling rates of 85-95%8 compared to New Zealand's current 28%
for plastic bottles.9

Composting systems that work with natural cycles break down organic matter in oxygen-rich
conditions, avoiding the methane emissions released when the same material decomposes
anaerobically in landfills. They also create soil that supports native plant restoration. When
4.5 million tonnes of soil unnecessarily go to landfill annually,10 we are literally burying the
foundation for ecosystem recovery. Business support for zero-waste models creates
employment while reducing the resource extraction that destroys wildlife habitats.

Evidence from Australia shows container deposit systems create thousands of jobs whilst
dramatically reducing litter.11 Germany's comprehensive waste systems achieve 68%
recycling rates12 whilst generating significant economic benefits.13 These aren't theoretical
solutions, they're proven approaches that other jurisdictions use successfully.

The AJP believes that comprehensive zero-waste systems are essential infrastructure for
protecting wildlife and creating economic opportunities. By eliminating toxic pollution,
reducing habitat destruction from resource extraction and reintegrating unavoidable
materials into natural cycles, these systems prevent environmental harm while reinforcing
the priority of eliminating waste at its origin and building a circular economy where animals
and humans can thrive together.

Urban Biodiversity and Natural Infrastructure
The AJP is committed to restoring native ecosystems within urban environments, creating
corridors for wildlife movement and habitat regeneration that support both indigenous
species and community wellbeing. We acknowledge that urban development has
fragmented natural habitats, isolating native species populations and reducing their
resilience to climate change and other pressures.

Urban areas house 86% of New Zealand's population14 but often function as ecological
deserts where native wildlife cannot survive or move between remaining habitat patches. Yet
cities also represent a great opportunity for rapid biodiversity gains. Urban forests in New
Zealand, such as those in Auckland's parks, can sequester carbon at rates comparable to
natural vegetation. A study by Schwendenmann and Mitchell (2014) found that a 27-year-old
planted forest stored a total of 45.9 megagrams of carbon per hectare, equating to an
average annual carbon sequestration rate of 1.7 megagrams per hectare.15 This rate is
within the range reported for New Zealand shrublands. Despite challenges like poor-quality
soil and contamination, urban forests can sequester carbon at similar rates to natural
vegetation in New Zealand. By providing both carbon storage and connectivity, these forests
support biodiversity while contributing to climate mitigation.

Green infrastructure provides multiple benefits: native plantings filter stormwater whilst
creating habitat, tree canopy reduces urban heat whilst providing nesting sites, and
biodiversity corridors connect fragmented ecosystems whilst offering communities
opportunities to engage with native wildlife. Research shows that urban biodiversity supports
human mental health16 whilst creating resilient ecosystems that can adapt to climate change.

International examples demonstrate what's possible. Singapore’s “City in a Garden” initiative
has transformed dense urban areas into functional habitat networks. Its green corridors and
restored parks support over 60 bird species, as well as bats, butterflies, and other native
fauna, allowing them to move safely across the city and access feeding and breeding sites.17
These corridors turn urban spaces from ecological deserts into interconnected habitats that
sustain animal populations.

Melbourne’s Urban Forest Strategy, which plants over 3,000 trees annually, not only
improves air quality and urban resilience but also creates canopy networks and stepping
stones that allow native birds, bats, and insects to navigate and survive in the city
landscape.18 By integrating animals’ needs into city planning, these initiatives show that
urban greening is essential infrastructure for biodiversity, not just human amenity. Uk

Protecting Insects from Human-Caused Harms
Insects, particularly pollinators, are in global decline, with studies showing terrestrial insect
abundance dropping by about 9 % per decade.19 Although long-term monitoring is lacking in
New Zealand, around 54 % of assessed native insect species are currently listed as
threatened or at risk, underscoring the urgent need for conservation.20 Habitat loss,
monoculture farming, and pesticide use are major drivers of these declines.
There is also growing scientific evidence of sentience of different insects. For example, a
2022 review of the evidence for pain in insects found “strong evidence” for the orders
containing flies and cockroaches, “substantial” evidence for the orders containing beetles
and butterflies, and “no good evidence that any insects failed a criterion”.21 Since these
species are largely under-studied, a precautionary approach requires that we grant
protection even when such evidence is incomplete.22
Key threats to insects are:
- The expansion of urban areas and intensive farming has led to the destruction of
natural habitats, reducing the availability of nesting sites and food sources for many
insect species.
- The widespread use of chemical pesticides has had devastating effects on insect
populations, particularly bees, butterflies, and other pollinators. Many pesticides
disrupt the nervous systems of insects, leading to mass die-offs.
- Industrial agricultural practices, including large-scale monocultures, reduce plant
diversity and limit food sources for insects, further contributing to population declines.
- Rising temperatures, shifting weather patterns, and pollution disrupt insect life cycles
and reduce the availability of suitable habitats.
Protecting insects is essential for maintaining a thriving environment. AJP will continue to
advocate for policies that safeguard pollinators, restore ecosystems and promote
sustainable land-use practices.
Key Objectives:
1. Safeguard and restore natural ecosystems, such as forests, meadows, wetlands and
waterways, which are vital for the survival of bees, butterflies, and other insects.
2. Implement stricter regulations on pesticide use, including restrictions on high-toxicity
chemicals during key insect activity periods, in areas where insects forage, breed, or
migrate, and promote adoption of non-toxic alternatives.
3. Support research and development of non-toxic alternatives to chemical pesticides.
4. Promote mixed farming practices over monocultures to enhance habitat diversity and
insect-friendly landscapes.

Strengthening Environmental Enforcement in Aotearoa
The AJP is committed to robust enforcement of environmental protections to ensure
industries cannot profit from destroying ecosystems that animals depend on for survival.
While New Zealand has an Environmental Protection Authority (EPA), current enforcement
and penalties are often inadequate, and monitoring resources are limited. This can result in
some companies treating fines as a minor business cost rather than a meaningful deterrent
to harmful behaviour.

Weak enforcement allows ongoing degradation of marine habitats, freshwater ecosystems
and native wildlife populations. Some companies discharge pollutants, destroy sensitive
habitats or operate without full compliance because penalties are too low or violations go
undetected. When penalties represent only a small fraction of profits, destructive practices
become economically rational. The result is systematic environmental degradation that
threatens the survival of native species whilst communities bear the health and economic
costs.

International best practice demonstrates effective enforcement works. Countries with strong
environmental agencies, substantial penalties and comprehensive monitoring achieve
significantly better environmental outcomes. In Germany, the Federal Environment Agency
(UBA) imposes substantial fines and criminal penalties for environmental violations,
reinforced by public reporting and court decisions emphasizing climate and ecosystem
protection.23 In the Netherlands, environmental authorities enforce a “duty of care,” issuing
administrative sanctions and remedial orders to prevent harm, holding both companies and
individuals accountable.24 These approaches show that independent authorities, meaningful
penalties, and executive liability significantly improve environmental outcomes.

The AJP believes that robust environmental enforcement is essential for protecting the
ecosystems that sustain all life in Aotearoa. Strong enforcement will deter environmental
destruction, ensure polluters pay the full costs of their impacts and create market incentives
that reward companies protecting rather than exploiting natural systems.

Key Objectives:

1. Strengthen and expand the powers of the New Zealand EPA, enabling immediate
stop-work orders and higher penalties for serious environmental violations.
2. Implement penalty structures exceeding potential profits from environmental
violations, ensuring harmful activities are never economically rational.
3. Create personal liability provisions making corporate directors and executives
criminally responsible for serious environmental crimes
4. Fund comprehensive environmental monitoring systems, including satellite
technology, remote sensing and community reporting networks.

Economic Sufficiency Within Planetary
Boundaries
The AJP recognises the profound impact of our lifestyles, economic metrics of success,
dietary choices, and leisure activities on the environment. The core issue that urgently needs
to be addressed is the current unsustainable trajectory, particularly in terms of environmental
degradation.

The AJP advocates for a paradigm shift in economic models, enabling an inclusion of
externalities of the natural world, and a move away from short-term gain to long-term
ecological sustainability, prioritising wellbeing over relentless growth, and emphasising the
importance of sustainable and future focused change to ensure a balance between human
activities and planetary boundaries.

We promote the adoption of circular economy principles, community-led initiatives, and local
resilience measures to minimise negative impact on the ecological footprint. We envision a
society that measures success, not only by economic metrics, but also by the health of
ecosystems, the wellbeing of communities, and the ethical and moral treatment of
non-human animals, aligning with a holistic and sustainable approach to living and thriving.

Core Principle

The economy must operate within ecological limits, recognising that infinite growth on a finite
planet is impossible. We must transition from individual accumulation to community
abundance while ensuring everyone's basic needs are met. This shift directly protects
animals by reducing the resource extraction and habitat destruction driven by endless
manufacturing, eliminating urban sprawl that fragments wildlife corridors, and decreasing

Economy Within Ecological Limits
The AJP recognises that infinite economic growth on a finite planet is incompatible with
human wellbeing, wildlife protection, ecosystem resilience, and animal welfare. The
economy must operate within ecological limits, prioritising community abundance, meeting
everyone’s basic needs and safeguarding the habitats that sustain nonhuman animals.
Humanity has long exceeded the planet’s safe ecological limits. Scientific evidence shows
that at least six of the nine planetary boundaries (climate change, biodiversity loss,
land-system change, freshwater use, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, and chemical
pollution) are already beyond safe thresholds. This overshoot drives habitat destruction,
fragments ecosystems and reduces wildlife to vulnerable populations, while intensifying
suffering for farmed animals through industrial, resource-intensive systems.
Transitioning to a post-growth, sufficiency-based economy ensures that resource use stays
within planetary limits, protects ecosystems and prioritises the welfare of all sentient beings
while meeting human needs.

Key Objectives:

1. Adopt post-growth economic frameworks, replacing GDP with indicators that
measure ecosystem health, biodiversity recovery, animal welfare, and human
wellbeing.
2. Establish annual resource consumption caps, aligned with planetary regeneration
capacity, to prevent habitat destruction and species decline.
3. Mandate corporate environmental and animal welfare impact disclosure, requiring
businesses to show supply chains and operations do not contribute to animal
suffering.
4. Require large corporations (>$50 million revenue) to deliver measurable positive
outcomes for native wildlife and domestic animal welfare, alongside financial
performance.

Sharing Economy and Community Abundance
The AJP is committed to creating a society where everyone has access to high-quality
shared resources while reducing the environmental destruction caused by excessive private
consumption and accumulation. We acknowledge that current economic models promote
individual ownership of rarely-used items, driving resource extraction that destroys wildlife
habitats while leaving many people unable to access basic amenities. We propose
transforming our economy towards private sufficiency and public luxury ensuring everyone
has what they need privately while creating magnificent shared spaces and resources that
surpass what any individual could afford alone.

Private sufficiency means everyone having secure access to life's essentials: safe housing,
nutritious food, healthcare, education, and the resources needed for a dignified life. Public
luxury means creating shared amenities so extraordinary that they meet and exceed what is
available through private ownership: world-class libraries, community workshops equipped
with professional tools, beautiful public gardens, shared vehicles, communal spaces for
celebration and creativity.

This approach dramatically reduces environmental pressure while improving quality of life.
Instead of millions of households owning power tools used twice yearly, communities share
professional-grade equipment available whenever needed. Rather than everyone buying
individual cars, communities enjoy access to diverse vehicles, electric cars for daily
transport, cargo bikes for local trips, vans for moving house. The result is less environmental
destruction with far greater access to high-quality resources. UK example

Circular Economy and Community Resilience
The AJP is committed to fostering sharing economies and circular systems that reduce
waste, strengthen communities, and minimise our environmental footprint. We acknowledge
that current economic models based on individual ownership and constant consumption
drive the resource extraction and habitat destruction that devastate wildlife across Aotearoa
New Zealand.

Moving beyond ownership models towards community-based sharing reduces pressure on
natural systems whilst building stronger neighbourhoods. When communities share tools,
vehicles, and resources, we dramatically reduce the manufacturing demand that destroys
habitats for native species. Car sharing alone can remove up to 15 private vehicles from our
roads, freeing urban space for green corridors that native birds and insects desperately
need.25

Community repair initiatives extend product lifecycles, reducing the 252,000 tonnes of
e-waste New Zealand generates annually—much of which contains toxic materials harmful
to wildlife. Libraries evolved from book repositories to community hubs can become centres
for sharing everything from tools to toys, reducing the material throughput that drives
environmental degradation.

Research from the Ellen MacArthur Foundation shows circular economy approaches can
reduce material extraction by up to 80% while creating more jobs than linear systems.
Worker cooperatives focused on repair and reuse create meaningful employment that builds
community wealth whilst protecting the environment that sustains all life.

The AJP believes that sharing economies represent an immediately implementable
approach to reducing environmental harm whilst strengthening communities. These systems
will reduce habitat destruction, support local employment, and create the social infrastructure
necessary for building a society that values cooperation over competition.

Key Objectives:

1. Expand nationwide networks of community repair cafés and tool libraries to extend
product lifecycles and reduce manufacturing demand
2. Support car-sharing cooperatives to reduce urban vehicle numbers and free land for
native habitat corridors
3. Transform library infrastructure into multi-purpose community hubs that facilitate
resource sharing and reduce individual consumption
4. Fund worker cooperatives specialising in repair, reuse, and sharing services that
create meaningful local employment
5. Create public banking options offering preferential rates for cooperative enterprises
focused on plant-based production, animal sanctuary operations and habitat
restoration projects
6. Support community ownership of renewable energy systems, ensuring profits from
clean energy stay local rather than flowing to extractive corporations that often fund
habitat destruction
