Protecting the rights of farmed animals
The Animal Justice Party Aotearoa New Zealand (AJP) asserts that all forms of animal
farming are ethically indefensible, fundamentally unjust, and must end. Every animal,
regardless of the product they are exploited for, has the inherent right to live free from
suffering, exploitation, and commodification. AJP is committed to driving a rapid
transition to a plant-based economy, recognising the urgent need to dismantle systems
that cause the greatest harm to animals. While working towards the complete abolition
of animal farming, we demand immediate reforms to eliminate the most severe suffering
and uphold the dignity and rights of all sentient beings.

In alignment with Te Tiriti o Waitangi principles, AJP affirms that protecting the rights of
animals requires genuine partnership with Māori. We honour the unique relationship
between tangata whenua and the natural world and commit to ensuring that policy
development and implementation incorporates Māori perspectives and knowledge
systems. Concepts such as kaitiakitanga (guardianship), mauri (life force), and
whakapapa (ancestral connection) offer powerful frameworks that challenge extractive
models of agriculture and envision a future grounded in respect, care and justice for all
living beings.

Abolish all farming of animals
The Animal Justice Party Aotearoa NZ (AJP) advocates for the complete abolition of
animal farming. All animals used in farming, regardless of the intended product or
purpose, have an equal right to live free from suffering, exploitation and neglect. AJP
supports a transition to system that respects animal rights, protects the environment
and promotes public health through plant-based, cellular and sustainable agriculture.

The industrial farming of animals represents the largest source of animal exploitation
and cruelty globally. Every year in New Zealand, billions of animals, including fish, are
bred, confined and/or killed for food production. This includes, for example: 119.5 million
chickens, broiler chickens, (2022),1 4.67 million dairy cows (2023/24)2 and
approximately 72.7 million farmed fish and shellfish individuals (noting this is an
estimate as fish are not measured by number of individuals but by metric weight alone,
the stated figure of 109, 016 metric tonnes3 is equivalent to the weight of approximately
72,677 cars).

These animals endure significant physical and psychological suffering throughout their
abbreviated lives, treated as mere units of production. AJP recognises that these
animals are sentient, and have interests in living lives free from suffering, with
autonomy, and the freedom to express their natural behaviors. These interests are often
comparable to the similar interests of humans, and establish the legal personhood of
sentient animals, along with rights which must be protected in the law. These rights are
fundamentally incompatible with ownership or consumptive practices.

All forms of farming impose severe harms and infringe on the rights of animals. The
dairy industry, central to New Zealand's economy, subjects cows to annual pregnancies,
separation from their calves, and intensive milking that leads to painful conditions like
mastitis. Male calves, considered "surplus" to the industry, are typically killed within days
of birth. Chickens raised for meat (broilers) are genetically manipulated to grow at
unnatural rates, leading to painful skeletal disorders, while hens in the egg industry are
confined in crowded conditions and killed when their productivity declines. Male chicks
are killed by maceration within their first few days.

The fashion and textile industries also represent significant sources of animal
exploitation. In New Zealand, the wool industry alone involves approximately 25.3
million sheep (2022)4, while possum fur and leather production contribute to animal
suffering and death.

All farming operations, whether animals are raised for meat, milk, wool, leather, down,
or any other product, including emerging industries such as insect and eel farming,
prioritise profit over the welfare of sentient animals and infringe on their rights. While
different forms of farming vary in the severity of their cruelty, all animal farming
fundamentally denies animals their personhood, along with their rights to autonomy and
freedom from exploitation.

AJP also recognises that animal farming imposes an unsustainable burden on natural
ecosystems both in Aotearoa and globally. The environmental impact of animal
agriculture is far-reaching and deeply harmful. It is a major driver of greenhouse gas
emissions, particularly methane and nitrous oxide, which are far more potent than
carbon dioxide in terms of warming potential. Animal farming also requires vast
amounts of land for grazing and feed production, leading to deforestation and habitat
destruction that endanger native species and contribute to biodiversity loss. In New
Zealand, intensive dairying has been directly linked to the degradation of waterways,
with nutrient runoff and effluent polluting rivers and lakes, harming aquatic life and
undermining the health of entire ecosystems. Leather tanning creates toxic pollution and
wool farming contributes to land degradation and water pollution. Globally, the
conversion of forests to pasture and feed crops is one of the leading causes of
biodiversity collapse and a significant barrier to achieving climate goals. Transitioning
away from animal farming is therefore essential not only to reduce animal suffering but
to restore ecological balance and safeguard the planet's capacity to support life.

Furthermore, intensive animal farming presents a critical threat to global public health
by creating ideal conditions for the emergence of zoonotic diseases. This risk arises
through two major pathways. Firstly, the confinement of animals in overcrowded and
often unhygienic environments facilities the transmission and mutation of pathogens
that can jump from animals to humans. These facilities can act as breeding grounds for
new viral and bacterial strains, with the close contact between animals and workers
increasing transmission risks. Secondly, the routine use of antibiotics in animal
agriculture, often used preventatively due to unsanitary conditions and to promote rapid

Independent Animal Welfare Governance to Reduce Harm
The Animal Justice Party (AJP) envisions a New Zealand where compassion,
sustainability and ethical practices guide our treatment of animals. While we advocate
for a complete transition to a plant-based farming system, we recognise that meaningful
societal changes require time and a ‘Just Transition’.7 In the interim, we are committed
to reducing the most severe harm and mistreatment of animals by improving oversight
of farming practices and strengthening legislation to provide stronger protections and
ensure higher welfare standards for all animals in Aotearoa.

New Zealand's animal welfare system must be free from conflicts of interest and bias.
Currently, the role of Minister for Animal Welfare is embedded within the Ministry for
Primary Industries (MPI), an institution heavily tied to the animal agriculture sector. To
address this, AJP calls for the establishment of an independent Commissioner for
Animals.8 This role would ensure impartial oversight and monitoring of animal welfare
and protection in Aotearoa New Zealand and more effective enforcement of, and
compliance with, the Animal Welfare Act 1999. See Regulatory Oversight and
Enforcement Policy.

The Animal Welfare Act 1999 outlines several needs (known as the “Five Freedoms”)
which must be provided for farmed animals:
- Proper and sufficient food and water.
- Adequate shelter.
- The opportunity to display normal patterns of behaviour.
- Physical handling in a manner which minimises the likelihood of unreasonable or
unnecessary pain or distress.
- Protection from, and rapid diagnosis of, any significant injury or disease.
While the Five Freedoms are insufficient to protect all the rights that sentient animals
are entitled to (such as the right to life), they represent the minimum legislative
standards to be adhered to. However, current farming methods and the codes of welfare
frequently do not meet these standards, especially in intensive animal farming.
Additionally, there is limited action or motivation from Government decision makers or
regulators to initiate and drive positive welfare law reform9 to align current farming

Ensuring Humane Transport Conditions

The Animal Justice Party (AJP) envisions a New Zealand where compassion,
sustainability and ethical practices guide our treatment of animals. While we ultimately
seek a complete transition away from animal agriculture towards plant-based and
cellular systems, we recognise the urgent need to reduce the suffering of animals who
are currently trapped in this system. One of the most severe and widespread breaches
of the five freedoms11 occurs during transport, particularly when animals are moved
between farms, saleyards and slaughterhouses.

Every year, millions of animals in New Zealand endure long and stressful journeys that
cause significant physical and psychological distress. The process of transport exposes
animals to unfamiliar environments, loud noises, rough handling, overcrowding and
prolonged confinement, often without adequate rest, food or water. Vulnerable groups
such as bobby calves and dairy cows are especially at risk. Despite existing regulations,
the current system prioritises cost-efficiency over animal wellbeing, with inadequate
limits on journey length, stocking density and handling standards.

Live export is one of the most extreme examples of suffering associated with transport.
Although New Zealand banned the export of livestock by sea for slaughter in 2023, this
ban is under threat from Government proposals in 2025 to overturn it, a move AJP
strongly opposes, see AJP Live Export policy. Furthermore, other forms of live export
continue, including the export of farmed species like eels by air, which also subjects
animals to prolonged and distressing journeys.

Transport regulations must be equally stringent for animals farmed for non-food
products, including alpacas, angora rabbits and other fibre-producing animals. These
animals often face similar or greater transport risks due to less established welfare
protocols and monitoring systems.

AJP calls for a complete overhaul of animal transport regulations to recognise the
specific needs of each species, regardless of their intended use. While animal farming
continues, we demand far stricter controls on journey times, space allowances, rest
periods and handling practices, as well as the retention and strengthening of the live
export ban. Our long-term vision remains the complete replacement of animal farming

Extending the Live Export Ban
Animal Justice Party Aotearoa New Zealand (AJP) will campaign to prevent the ban on
sending steers, heifers, cows and bulls (‘cattle’) and sheep, deer and goats by sea from
being repealed.

AJP will also campaign for an end to exporting live animals by air, except in the case of
companion animals relocating overseas and coming into New Zealand with their
families, zoo animals and horses requiring relocation. Refer to AJP’s Policy regarding
Horse Racing.

The Animal Welfare Act 1999 was amended in 2022 to state that after 30 April 2023 no
livestock may be exported from New Zealand by sea.12 Federated Farmers, ACT
Party13 and National Party14 have all made public statements in support of repealing this
amendment.

Although it is not well known, we also export millions of animals every year by air,
namely, goats, llamas, pigs, alpacas, bees, deer, horses, cats, dogs and day-old baby
chicks. In 2022 more than 2000 goats and 3.4 million day-old chicks were sent offshore
by plane. We also export aquatic species such as longfin eels even though they are ‘at
risk’ native animals15 and crayfish.16

Further, regardless of the method of transport, once the animals are exported, New
Zealand no longer has any control over how our animals are treated in the destination
country. For example, boiling crayfish alive in a restaurant is an offence in New Zealand
but similar rules do not necessarily apply overseas.17

More about why AJP opposes a reinstatement of livestock export by sea and why
we will campaigning for further restrictions on live export by air

Live export by sea

5,867 cows and 41 crew tragically died when Gulf Livestock 1 sank at sea in September
2020 which led to a temporary suspension of livestock export by sea from New Zealand.
After an independent review by Michael Heron QC, exports resumed with tighter
controls including increased voyage reporting and reduced stocking density on vessels.

Another 128 cows also died in 2020 during the usual course of transport and as a result
of the terrible conditions on board. Animal Genetics Trade Association spokesman
Dave Hayman has said “Deaths were most often from a broken leg or a joint injury.”

MPI have stated that the minimum pen area per head for cattle exported by sea is
strongly recommended to be 2m2 for the average 600 kg cow. Australian Dr. Lynn
Simpson is a veterinarian who has travelled on 57 long haul live export voyages. Dr.
Simpson has said “They’re pretty tight. They’re right up against each other, and they
have to choreograph and jostle around to find space. Generally in my experience they
can’t all lie down at the same time”.

A New Zealand veterinarian has described their experience on board a live export ship:
“The cramped nature of the pens and the effluent buildup (both on the floor and on the
coats of the cattle) reduced air flow around the cattle, which added to the heat stress. I
found it very difficult to alleviate the distress I observed during this period of the
journey.”
“Despite our best efforts to get all the cattle across the equator alive, two cattle died of
heat stress on the journey. Although the two cattle written down on our trip report
represent a low mortality rate attributable to heat stress, the pain and distress they and
the surviving cattle experienced still haunts me.”26

Live export by air

3.4 million day-old chicks travel internationally by air annually causing variable welfare
compromises to the day-old chicks. Day-old chicks are not able to regulate their own
temperature and are subjected to handling, loading, and transporting stress.

This industry takes advantage of the day-old chicks’ ability to survive for up to 72 hours
without food and water due to their residual yolk-sac which provides nutrients and
hydration, in the absence of feed and water. The composition and size of the yolk-sac
depends on various factors and some will last longer than others. While the yolk-sac
can provide up to 72 hours of nutrition and hydration, the rate of use is also affected by
factors such as genetics and environmental conditions. Some individual chicks will not
have adequate sustenance during their journey meaning inadequate nutrition and
dehydration which will contribute to their death.

The Ministry for Primary Industries does not routinely report mortality statistics for
day-old chicks, so we do not even know how many could not survive the journey. In
addition, the Ministry only applies voluntary guidelines to the trade, which are not
publicly consulted.

Many will be slaughtered without stunning, which is illegal in New Zealand.29 China

Equitable Protection for Aquatic Animals
The Animal Justice Party believes that if we say animals matter, our laws need to protect them.
New Zealand already acknowledges in legislation that fish, crustaceans, and cephalopods are
sentient beings capable of suffering. What we don't yet have are the standards, the
enforcement, and the consistency to make that acknowledgement legally binding. We want to
close that gap, and ensure that aquatic animals receive the same protection under the law that
we extend to animals on land.
What is happening
New Zealand's Animal Welfare Act 1999 names fish, octopus, squid, crab, lobster, and crayfish
within its definition of 'animal', and since 2015 has explicitly recognised animal sentience in its
purpose. But recognition without enforcement is just words on a page. The Act and its codes of
welfare are applied selectively, with significant gaps in oversight and monitoring. For aquatic
animals, those gaps are particularly wide. There is no standalone code of welfare for fish in
commercial aquaculture or wild capture. Without species-specific standards or adequate
resourcing, aquatic animals are effectively left out of the protection that the law promises them.
The picture is even more woeful for decapod crustaceans such as shrimp and prawns, and
cephalopods such as cuttlefish, which currently fall outside the legal definition of 'animal' under
the Act. Scientists now have strong consensus that fish can feel pain,51 and the evidence for
sentience in many aquatic invertebrates continues to grow.52 Our law has simply not kept pace
with these developments.
This matters at scale. Globally, somewhere between 78 and 171 billion farmed fishes,53 1.1 and
2.2 trillion wild fin-fishes are killed each year for human consumption,54 alongside 25 trillion of
shrimp.55 What New Zealand does in its own waters, and the standards it holds its industries to,
is within our power to change.

Eliminating the Use of Animals in Science
Ending Animal Testing
The Animal Justice Party of Aotearoa NZ (AJP) aims to ban all harmful forms of animal
experimentation. AJP will commit to promoting the use of compassionate research.
Animal experimentation often utilises harmful and invasive processes that routinely
involve subjecting animals to painful and stressful procedures. The rate at which
animal-based models of experiments fail to translate to human-based models is high.
This creates an opportunity to find alternative methods that can prove to be more
effective, accurate and ethical. For example, there are emerging alternatives to many
forms of animal experimentation, such as cell cultures and computer simulations. By
requiring the use of alternatives to animal testing wherever possible, we can foster a

The environmental impacts of animal-based research
The Animal Justice Party of Aotearoa New Zealand (AJP) aims to ban all harmful forms of
animal experimentation. AJP commits to promoting the use of compassionate research.

Animal experimentation often utilises harmful and invasive processes that routinely involve
subjecting animals to painful and stressful procedures. The rate at which animal-based models
of experiments fail to translate to human-based models is high. This creates an opportunity to
find alternative methods that can prove to be more effective, accurate and ethical, for example,
there are emerging alternatives to many forms of animal experimentation such as cell cultures
and computer simulations. By requiring the use of alternatives to animal testing wherever
possible, we can foster a culture of innovation and advancement in the scientific field that does
not come at the expense of animals.

However, the traditional, and still dominant, ethical view, which is mirrored in most animal testing
legislation and regulations, is that the use of animals in research is acceptable in order to
achieve scientific, public safety and medical goals, provided that animal suffering is minimised
and that the human benefits gained could not be obtained by utilising alternative methods. By
its very nature, animal testing required by law is in tension with the goal of animal welfare
legislation, which is to protect animals from violations of their welfare.

Animal-based research isn't just an ethical issue. It also has significant environmental impacts
that demand attention. The environmental cost of maintaining these practices contradicts
commitments to sustainability and climate action.
The main environmental impacts of animal experimentation include:
5. High Resource Consumption: Animal research facilities use large amounts of water,
electricity, and specialised food for the animals. The need to maintain specific environmental
conditions (e.g., temperature control and ventilation) further increases energy use.
Animal research requires significant energy resources. Research facilities consume up to
ten times more energy per square metre than office buildings74 due to stringent
requirements, including controlled ventilation, temperature regulation, pathogen barriers,
specialised lighting, and power-intensive equipment.
6. Waste Production and Pollution: Research generates substantial biomedical waste that
requires careful management and disposal. This includes both biological waste (animal
carcasses, excrement, bedding, and uneaten food) and hazardous materials (syringes,
needles, contaminated materials, and chemical waste).
When animals used in research are disposed of, their remains may be classified as
hazardous waste, depending on the type of studies they were involved in. This can include
exposure to chemicals, infectious agents, or radioactive materials.

A key concern is the gap in awareness and safety measures between laboratory personnel,
who are trained to handle such materials, and external waste management or transportation

third-party handlers are unaware of potential hazards, improper disposal could lead to
environmental contamination or public health risks.
Proper disposal methods often involve incineration or other resource-intensive processes.
During incineration, particulate matter, organic compounds, pathogens, and radioactive
materials may be released into the surrounding environment, posing significant human health
risks, including cancer and respiratory illnesses. Environmental concerns include air and
water pollution, acidification, and greenhouse gas emissions.
7. Carbon Footprint: The transportation of animals, supplies, and research materials
contributes to carbon emissions. Additionally, maintaining breeding colonies and research
facilities adds to the overall carbon footprint.

purposes.
9. Chemical and Pharmaceutical Pollution: Animal testing is particularly used for safety

also used to assess the effectiveness of sanitation, disinfection, and sterilisation processes.
A key concern is that some chemicals used in these experiments may have unknown
hazardous or carcinogenic properties, posing risks not only to the animals but also to
researchers, waste handlers, and the environment. Many chemicals tested in
laboratories may not yet have fully understood toxicological profiles, potentially exposing
both animals and humans to unknown dangers.
Industries that conduct toxicity and pharmaceutical testing on animals can contribute to
groundwater and soil contamination if their waste management practices are
inadequate. Improper disposal of chemical agents, pharmaceuticals, and biological
waste can lead to hazardous substances leaching into the environment, causing

Background and Rationale

Zoos and aquariums may unintentionally communicate that it is acceptable to confine
wild animals in small or unsuitable enclosures - sometimes in isolation from their own
species - for human education or entertainment. Yet such conditions can cause
significant harm, as many animals are unable to engage in their natural, species specific
behaviours.

The AJP’s approach is grounded in the moral significance of individual animals. Whilst
biodiversity and ecosystem protection are important, they must not be pursued at the
expense of the lives and wellbeing of individual sentient beings. Practices such as
forced breeding, confinement in inadequate conditions and the treatment of animals as
interchangeable components of conservation strategies are ethically indefensible.

Effective conservation must prioritise the protection and restoration of natural habitats.
Without suitable environments for animals to return to, captive breeding programmes
offer limited value and may, in some cases, perpetuate harm. Moreover, zoos tend to

Defining Zoos and Sanctuaries

Facilities that house animals vary widely in purpose and practice, often using

AJP opposes the trapping and hunting of wild animals as a part of so-called ‘pest
control’ activities. For AJP’s policy on hunting for entertainment, see: Hunting and
Fishing policies.
Common trapping practices include:
- Leg-Hold Traps: These devices are designed to capture animals by the limb,
restraining them until they are dispatched by trappers. The use of leg-hold traps
can result in severe injuries, including fractures, dislocations, and lacerations.

pain before being euthanised.106,107

harm, allowing for relocation or euthanasia. Animals may experience stress,
dehydration and exposure to harsh environmental conditions while confined for

actively enforced.109

trapping and hunting methods that cause unnecessary suffering to animals.

activities without sufficient age restrictions.110 This exposure raises ethical concerns
regarding the normalisation of violence towards animals and potential desensitisation to
animal suffering. It also poses safety risks, as handling traps and captured animals can

Protect and restore natural habitats
AJP supports greater protection and restoration of natural habitats and ecosystems. We
recognise that the destruction or degradation of habitats and ecosystems causes direct
harm to wild animals, and that both humans and wild animals are sustained by natural
habitats and ecosystems.
63% of Aotearoa’s land area has now been converted for human use, with animal
agriculture being a primary historical cause of habitat destruction.111 By 1930, 39% of
New Zealand’s land area had been cleared and transformed to make way for pastoral
farming.112 This transformation of land involved destroying forests, draining wetlands
and disrupting waterways, causing significant harm to natural ecosystems and free
living animals, whose habitats were obliterated or disrupted. As of 2021, this remains
largely unaddressed, with 42% of land converted for farming and 8% converted for
forestry.113 In order to reverse this process, AJP would enable greater protection of

Protect wildlife from anthropogenic harms
Wild animals are frequently harmed due to the design of urban structures, roads, and
the activities of people who are often unaware of their impacts. To address these issues,
AJP wishes to see greater integration of wildlife considerations into urban planning,
road design and signage by way of impact assessments. This would require greater
collaboration between local councils, urban planners, conservation groups and
communities to develop wildlife-friendly infrastructure.
Many wild animals, native and introduced, inhabit towns and cities and are frequently
harmed by their human-centric design. For example, windows can be made with glass
that is more visible to birds, thereby reducing collisions.119 Human-wildlife conflicts can
be addressed in nonviolent ways, such as through nonviolent population control of
pigeons, rather than using spikes to restrict perching.120 Generally speaking, by
considering animals in the design of urban structures and areas, harms to wild animals
can be reduced or avoided altogether. This may often require collaboration between
local councils, urban planners, conservation groups and communities.
This is particularly important in the design of roads, which frequently kill and injure wild
animals. Measures such as wildlife crossings, barriers, and lower speed limits can
reduce these harms,121 and research into wildlife movement patterns can inform road
and urban planning that prioritises animal safety. While the Wildlife Act 1953 requires
permits for developments that may disturb or kill protected species, its focus remains on
species conservation and habitat protection rather than the welfare of individual
animals, leaving a gap between conservation law and animal welfare.
An important aspect of making urban areas friendly to wild animals is by the integration
of habitat. This may involve planting native species, connecting fragmented habitats
through wildlife corridors, or creating green roofs and pocket parks. Such measures not
only provide shelter and food for animals but also improve the quality of life for people.
Lastly, signs warning people of sensitive wildlife, or educating people about how to
behave around them, need to be informative, highly visible, and strategically placed to
maximise their effectiveness in both rural and urban areas.

Legislation and Regulation Reform for
Animal Protection
AJP supports legislation and regulation that prioritises the well-being, protection and
rights of animals as sentient beings, ensuring they are not subjected to exploitation,
harm or cruelty. It is imperative that animal welfare and protection legislation is
adequately resourced and equipped with robust compliance monitoring, appropriate
penalties and regulatory enforcement capabilities.

Animal welfare legislation should be drafted with the sole intention of recognising animal
sentience and protecting animals, without influence from industries that profit from their
exploitation. The principles enshrined in the Animal Welfare Act 1999 must be
enforceable, without loopholes that allow industries to evade responsibility by
self-regulation. Until adequate animal protection legislation is in place, AJP will also
campaign to remove and prohibit exemptions and inconsistencies that allow animal
cruelty to occur (for example, codes of practice for the “welfare” of farmed animals).

AJP supports a legal framework that reflects a societal commitment to valuing,
protecting and respecting animals, fostering responsible and compassionate treatment
in all contexts and industries. Without stringent, and enforceable, legal protection,
animals remain vulnerable to cruelty and exploitation, their welfare jeopardised by
profit-driven motives.

Empowering and resourcing authorities to share information and enforce legislation and
regulations is essential, ensuring individuals and organisations are held accountable for
infringements. Championing animal-centric legislation paves the way to a more just and
humane society where positive animal welfare136 is legally recognised and animals are
granted by the law the dignity and protection they deserve.

Legislative Foundations

To create a comprehensive and effective Animal Protection Policy, a new legislative
framework must be established. This framework should address animal welfare,
conservation, ethical treatment, and the enforcement of laws to ensure that animals are
protected from cruelty and exploitation. Below is a proposed structure for legislative
foundations that could guide the formulation of such a policy:

1. Define animal sentience in legislation
2. Strengthen legal protections recognising animals as sentient beings
3. Propose a new legislative framework with positive welfare obligations
4. Constitutional rights for nature: advocate for legal personhood for ecosystems and
species
5. Abolish the legal classification of animals as property and grant animals legal rights

This will be achieved through dialogue, research, analysis and solutions-based reform
proposals to inform legislative and policy change and to enable greater political
accountability and more inclusive and just governance.

Law and policy reforms will be secured to create an animal welfare governance
system that is transparent, in line with contemporary science and public expectations,
and focuses on the wellbeing and protection of animals. These reforms in New
Zealand will have the potential to set a precedent for the creation of large-scale
change globally.

Sentience

The acknowledgement of animals as sentient in an amendment to the Animal Welfare
Act 1999 (the “Act”) in 2015 was the first of its kind in a common law jurisdiction.
However, its location in the long title is telling – it simply states what the Act is about

International Considerations
The shift in the international animal welfare and protection policy environment highlights
the growing recognition of animal welfare as a critical issue on the global stage.

It is no longer viewed as peripheral but has become integral to discussions in regional
and international policy forums.

This change is underscored by the emergence of an expanding framework of
science-based standards, conventions, treaties, regulations, directives, and
agreements.

Key trends in this evolving landscape include:

1. Science-Based Frameworks: Policy decisions are increasingly guided by robust
scientific research, which ensures that animal welfare measures are grounded in
evidence and best practices.
2. Global and Regional Agreements: International treaties and regional directives,
such as the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild
Fauna and Flora (CITES), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH)
standards or EU directives on animal welfare, set benchmarks for practices
across borders.
3. Integration into Broader Agendas: Animal welfare is being linked with
sustainability, public health, biodiversity, and climate change, ensuring it receives
attention in broader policy contexts.
4. Stakeholder Collaboration: Governments, NGOs, scientists, and industry
players are collaborating more actively to develop and implement welfare
policies.

This evolving framework not only reflects a growing ethical commitment to animals but
also acknowledges their role in sustainable development and human wellbeing. Global
animal protection is, however, a complex challenge requiring collaboration across
countries, cultures, and legal systems.

While there are various treaties, conventions, and agreements addressing specific
aspects of animal welfare, there is no unified international instrument that broadly
regulates animal welfare across all contexts and provides holistic animal welfare
protections.

Key gaps include:

1. Limited Scope: Existing frameworks often prioritise conservation and

Artificial Intelligence and Animal Welfare
Introduction
The Animal Justice Party of Aotearoa New Zealand (AJP) recognises that artificial intelligence (AI) is
rapidly transforming how millions of animals are managed across farming, aquaculture and introduced
species control in Aotearoa New Zealand. While AI technologies offer potential to improve animal
welfare through enhanced monitoring and early disease detection, they also risk entrenching harmful
practices and creating new forms of harm if deployed without adequate governance systems.

AJP does not endorse the continued expansion of animal agriculture. Any use of AI in these systems must
demonstrate responsible development and genuine prioritisation of animal welfare and harm
minimisation. We oppose AI technology systems designed purely for economic efficiency that treat
animals as units of production rather than as sentient individuals with inherent value.

AJP calls for immediate integration of animal welfare and protection considerations into AI governance
frameworks before harmful practices become economically entrenched and impossible to reform. The
long-term policy direction of AI must prioritise transition away from animal farming, not the
technological optimisation of it.

Context
The Scale of AI Deployment
AI technologies are already deployed across Aotearoa New Zealand's animal management systems at
unprecedented scale. Over 400,000 cattle are currently managed using AI-powered virtual fencing
systems, whilst millions more animals in dairy, beef, sheep, chicken, aquaculture systems and introduced
species control are increasingly likely to be subject to AI-driven management decisions.

AI systems are being rapidly integrated into the management of these animals, making autonomous
decisions about their welfare, health and lives.

Current AI applications include:

- Virtual fencing and herding: AI-integrated wearable technology using sounds and vibration to
control animal movement, with systems learning and predicting individual behaviour patterns
- Slaughterhouse automation, Computer vision, machine learning and robotics for carcass grading,
meat inspection, automated restraint, and cutting, optimising yield and throughput.
- Health monitoring: Computer vision and machine learning analysing gait patterns, body
condition and behaviour to assess welfare
- Automated feeding and milking: AI-optimised systems managing nutrition and dairy production
- Aquaculture management: Machine learning for biomass measurement, feeding optimisation
and water quality monitoring
- Introduced species control: AI-powered cameras in traps using species recognition, and
autonomous drones identifying and poisoning target animals

The Regulatory Gap
The Animal Welfare Act 1999 was enacted before AI technology existed and contains no provisions for
AI-driven animal management and welfare. This creates a critical regulatory time lag where technology is
reshaping human-animal relationships faster than legislation and regulation can adapt.

Aotearoa New Zealand's AI Strategy (July 2025), positions agriculture as a key adoption sector but
contains no animal welfare considerations. The accompanying Responsible AI Guidance for Businesses
covers cybersecurity, privacy and intellectual property whilst omitting animal welfare entirely. The
Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment has been explicit that "unlike EU regulatory
approaches, our approach is dispersed. The Government doesn't intend to put in place an overarching AI
Act. AI risks should be managed in relevant legislation as they arise."(email correspondence Oct 2025).

This dispersed “light touch” approach is already being implemented in other sectors. 2025 changes to
the Commerce Act, address AI-facilitated anti-competitive conduct, ensuring companies are liable for AI
actions. Additionally, a Members Bill was introduced (2025) to amend the Harmful Digital
Communications and Crimes Act 1961 to address AI-generated harms such as deepfakes. These
examples confirm that rapid, targeted legislative response is possible.

Animal welfare requires the same urgency. Without explicit action to integrate welfare considerations
into relevant existing Acts, AI governance will continue to treat animal welfare as a low-priority
externality rather than a core consideration.

Without explicit regulatory requirements, AI systems will inevitably be designed solely for economic
efficiency rather than animal welfare and protection. Market forces alone cannot ensure welfare and
safety outcomes; only regulation can mandate that AI development prioritises animals' behavioural,
physical and mental needs rather than subordinating them to economic and productivity metrics.

Case Study: Virtual Fencing Regulatory Lag
Virtual fencing technology demonstrates the pattern of regulatory lag that affects AI systems
deployment. Despite operating commercially since at least 2020 and affecting hundreds of thousands of
cattle:

- Circa 2021: The National Animal Welfare Advisory Committee (NAWAC) recommended
safeguards for virtual fencing in its Code of Welfare Evaluation Report on Dairy Cattle, stating
these "should have more generic application than only virtual fencing and apply to any future
technology proposed for animal management"
- Early 2024: A petition called for a parliamentary inquiry into virtual fencing technology
- December 2024: Veterinarians for Animal Welfare Aotearoa announced development of a code

Global Context and Market Pressures
Globally, most AI governance frameworks have overlooked animal welfare. The OECD's AI Principles and
most national AI strategies focus exclusively on human-centred concerns such as privacy, bias,
transparency, data integrity, accountability and safety. However, a significant breakthrough occurred in
July 2025, when the EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice explicitly listed "risk to non-human
welfare" as a systemic risk, alongside threats to democratic processes, financial stability and
environmental harm. This represents the first time a major jurisdiction has formally recognised that AI
governance must consider animal welfare, achieved through successful advocacy by animal welfare
organisations during the drafting stage.

Beyond the EU, Serbia's AI framework explicitly states AI systems "must be in line with the well-being of
humans, animals, and the environment." These examples demonstrate that inclusion of animal welfare
in AI governance is both achievable and is increasingly being recognised as necessary.

The AI animal health market is projected to grow from approximately $1.4 billion in 2024 to over $8
billion by 2034, with the farmed animal sector expected to see the fastest growth. This rapid investment
is occurring without systematic regulatory oversight of welfare outcomes, creating substantial risk that AI
systems will entrench rather than improve current harmful practices.

Welfare Risks of Unregulated AI
- Intensification of harmful practices: AI technology makes existing harmful practices more
efficient rather than more humane. It integrates best with intensive, cage-based systems that
have the worst welfare conditions, enabling higher stocking densities and more intensive
confinement, whilst appearing to ensure individualised care.
- Economic prioritisation: Data collection prioritises economic metrics over welfare indicators. AI
systems optimise measurable parameters (e.g. milk yield, growth rate, feed conversion) whilst
potentially missing qualitative welfare aspects such as emotional wellbeing, behavioural
freedom and social relationships.
- Reduced human oversight: Fewer human caregivers enable larger-scale operations, removing
human-animal relationships and reducing opportunities for animals to receive individualised
attention and compassionate care.

- Degraded human-animal relationship: humans become algorithm supervisors rather than carers

- Technological dependency: System failures could leave animals without care. Farms become

- Welfare washing: AI systems create the appearance of individualised care whilst facilitating

maintaining fundamentally harmful practices.
- Automated lethal control: AI-powered introduced species control technologies operate without
direct human oversight of individual animal deaths, making life-or-death decisions
autonomously. This raises serious ethical questions about delegating such decisions to
algorithms designed for efficiency rather than compassion.

Economic Scale and Digital Divide
High AI technology implementation costs will create a digital divide where smaller operations cannot
access technologies that larger commercial operations use for both efficiency and welfare monitoring.
This risks creating a two-tiered system where animal welfare outcomes depend on economic scale rather
than consistent regulatory standards, with larger operations able to demonstrate compliance through AI
systems monitoring, whilst smaller farms lack resources for equivalent compliance systems.

The Window for Action
Despite AI technology deployment already underway across Aotearoa New Zealand's animal
management systems, the window for proactive policy intervention still remains open. While systems
already affect hundreds of thousands of animals, harmful practices have not yet become so economically
entrenched that reform is impossible.

The government has demonstrated capacity for rapid legislative response when circumstances require it.
In May 2025, Parliament passed the Wildlife (Authorisations) Amendment Bill under urgency to clarify
authority for killing protected wildlife during development projects, responding within months to a High
Court decision. This swift action demonstrates that regulatory speed depends on political prioritisation
rather than legislative capacity.

protection remains central to technological development
- Sentience and Legal Status: AJP's existing advocacy for abolishing the classification of animals as
