Thriving Marae policy 2026

Te Waonui a Tāne, the Green Party policy
for thriving marae and communities,
recognises that marae are not simply
buildings—marae are living ecosystems
of people, whenua, culture, knowledge,
and communities.
For many New Zealanders, Māori and non-Māori
alike, marae are part of the shared cultural
landscape that shapes our national identity
and contributes to what makes Aotearoa
unique in the world. Marae are essential and
intergenerational community partners to local
councils, government agencies, schools,
community and health organisations, to
improve outcomes for all whānau.
Marae have also repeatedly demonstrated
their importance during times of crisis.
Time and again, marae have opened their
doors to support entire communities at the
worst of times.
Marae have played a vital role in civil
defence and community responses from the
Christchurch and Kaikōura earthquakes, to the
severe weather events impacting Te Tai Rāwhiti,
Te Tai Tokerau, Canterbury, and other regions
affected by states of emergency.
Despite their importance, marae across
the motu continue to face a compounding
number of challenges that threaten both their
operational capability and cultural integrity.
Funding programmes are often short-term,
capped, and prescribed rather than communityled. Many are fragmented across multiple
agencies, and difficult to access for marae
without the resources to navigate complex
application and reporting processes.
This makes it difficult for marae committees
to undertake long-term planning, restore
important infrastructure, build capability,
or invest in projects that strengthen their
communities for future generations.
Despite the enduring wealth and advantage
the Crown has obtained through historic

greens.org.nz

injustice and dispossession of Māori, the Treaty
Settlement process makes the Crown the
wrongdoer, the negotiator, and the rule maker.
The Crown continues to set the terms of
negotiations – deciding which resources may
be returned, which assets are available, and
how much compensation iwi can receive. This
does not reflect natural justice or a partner
acting in good faith.
Many marae are managing ageing buildings
and failing infrastructure, financial constraints,
limited opportunities to generate sustainable
income, and declining numbers of ahikā to
uphold tikanga and care for marae.
By restoring and sustaining marae, we
strengthen the foundations that allow
communities to thrive.

Together, we can ensure future
generations inherit a vibrant,
resilient, and self-determining
community and Aotearoa.
Marae should have the support to plan for
generations, not election cycles. Te Waonui a
Tāne creates a permanent solution to access
funding for short-term and long-term needs,
while building local capability, and providing
outcomes that benefit everyone in Aotearoa.
The Green Party will establish
Te Rangatiratanga Trust with an initial
funding commitment that will be transferred
into the ownership of hapū and iwi of
Aotearoa to create the capital base for the
permanent trust.
Te Rangatiratanga Trust will restore and
sustain marae as the living heart of our
communities by creating a permanent,
independent source of funding for the full
marae ecosystem—the people, whenua,
buildings, culture, and relationships
that keep marae and their surrounding
communities thriving.

Closer partnerships,
better outcomes

Marae resilience for
safer communities

Many community services in rural
and regional areas such as education,
housing support, food security, health
and youth programmes operate in
isolation from one another.

During and after times of crisis such
as climate charged weather events,
marae have repeatedly demonstrated
their importance for keeping people
safe, connected, and cared for. Time
and again, marae have opened their
doors to welcome and support entire
communities.

Funding contracts are often short-term,
fragmented, and tied to different agencies
with separate reporting requirements. This
makes it difficult for marae, councils, and
community organisations to work together
in a coordinated way, even when they are
serving the same whānau.
How Te Waonui a Tāne responds: Flexible
funding through Te Rangatiratanga Trust
would support marae-led, cross-sector
community initiatives that bring together
local councils, service providers, schools
and social service organisations. Regional
commissioning bodies would help reduce
administrative burden, align funding
requirements and support shared planning,
delivery, and evaluation. The long-term
feature of Te Tōtara Haemata ensures
successful partnerships can continue and
scale over time.
The outcome: Marae become reliable
partners for coordinated community action,
where services work together rather than in
isolation. Whānau experience more seamless
support across health, education, and social
needs, while local organisations achieve
better outcomes through shared resources
and reduced duplication.
Importantly, Māori-led approaches
strengthen outcomes for the whole
community, not only Māori. When services
are designed around holistic wellbeing,
non-Māori also benefit from more
accessible healthcare, stronger emergency
preparedness, improved social support,
and better connection to local services. This
creates more resilient, cohesive communities
where improved wellbeing is shared across
everyone living in the area.

03

But one in three marae are at risk of flooding,
one in three are situated in landslide-prone
areas, one in five are in tsunami evacuation
zones and two in five are susceptible to
liquefaction in the event of an earthquake. At
the same time, marae are increasingly relied
upon as emergency response hubs, despite
often lacking resilient power, water supplies
and emergency infrastructure.
How Te Waonui a Tāne responds:
Te Rākau Whakarauora Restoration
Fund would provide immediate funding
for projects such as solar power and battery
storage, emergency water supplies,
communication systems, and secure
storage for emergency equipment. It could
also support climate adaptation initiatives
including flood mitigation, erosion control,
vulnerable infrastructure relocation,
improving stormwater management, and
other projects that strengthen the long-term
resilience of marae and surrounding whenua.
Regional commissioning bodies would assist
with planning, technical advice, and project
delivery, while Te Tōtara Haemata would
provide an ongoing source of funding to
maintain and enhance resilience
options over time.
The outcome: Marae are better prepared to
withstand the impacts of climate change and
natural disasters while continuing to provide
shelter, food, coordination and support when
communities need them most. Investing in
resilience before disasters occur helps reduce
future costs, protects places of cultural
significance, and strengthens the safety and
wellbeing of the wider community.

Thriving Marae policy 2026

Rural health hubs for
local wellbeing
Many rural communities face long travel
times, limited transport options, and
limited access to healthcare. Marae have
become trusted locations for delivering
drive-through, pop-up, and mobile
health services during times of need, yet
many lack the facilities needed to provide
regular, preventative healthcare.
As a result, many people delay seeking
treatment until their health needs become
more serious and require hospital care.
How Te Waonui a Tāne responds: Funding
from Te Rākau Whakarauora or Te Tōtara
Haemata would support marae to establish or
upgrade spaces where they can host regular
primary healthcare services, including GP
clinics, nurses’ services, mental health

greens.org.nz

support, screening programmes, midwives,
immunisation clinics, and kaupapa Māori
health providers. Regional commissioning
bodies would support marae to coordinate
with healthcare providers and deliver these
projects.
The outcome: Whānau can access
preventative healthcare closer to home,
helping to identify and address health
concerns before they develop into more
serious conditions. This strengthens the
health and wellbeing of communities,
reduces pressure on the healthcare system,
and supports marae in continuing to serve
as trusted, locally led centres that support
healthier futures for all of us.

Tino rangatiratanga
and self-governance
Before the Trust is established, the Green
Party will work with key groups to outline
and timeline the move from aspiration to
reality. We will facilitate nationwide wānanga
informed by constitutional transformation
processes, such as Matike Mai, to support
collectively designing the Trust’s governance
structure. The outcome of this process will
become the foundation of the Trust Deed.
This process will facilitate hapū
and iwi to determine:
• How collective ownership is exercised
• How trustees are appointed and removed
• Regional representation
• Tikanga alignment
• Dispute resolution process
• Succession arrangements
The governance model can draw from
successful Māori-led arrangements already
operating across Aotearoa, including Whānau
Ora commissioning approaches, hapū and
iwi governance structures, and Māori-owned
organisations that manage significant
resources while remaining accountable to
their communities and tikanga.
Once established, Te Rangatiratanga
Trust will operate independently through
governance arrangements designed to
protect its intergenerational purpose,
maintain transparency, and ensure
accountability to marae, hapū, iwi,
communities, and the public.
The role of the Crown will be limited to
establishing the Trust, supporting the
collective design process, and transferring
the agreed capital contribution to
establish the Trust.
The Crown will have no authority to direct
investments, appoint trustees, alter funding
decisions, or interfere with the operation
of the Trust beyond ordinary law applying
equally to all trusts.

Thriving Marae policy 2026

Te Rangatiratanga Trust
Te Rangatiratanga Trust will govern a
permanent source of funding for marae,
hapū, and the communities they serve.
A $2.44 billion Crown funding input will
establish the Trust and its two
complementary funds:
Te Rākau Whakarauora Restoration Fund,
which provides for urgent and priority needs,
and Te Tōtara Haemata Intergenerational
Fund, which protects and grows capital for
future generations.
Together, these funds will provide immediate
and long-term support for the marae
ecosystem, remedy inequities derived
from historic and ongoing Crown actions,
enhance marae capability and community
partnerships, and create intergenerational
wellbeing independent of political cycles.
Te Rangatiratanga Trust is designed as a
permanent capital base for a funding model
that provides both immediate support for
marae and long-term funding certainty for
future generations. Instead of relying on
short-term funding cycles, the Trust creates
a permanent source of funding that grows
over time and supports marae, hapū, and
communities to lead their own aspirations.
The Trust combines immediate restoration
investment with an intergenerational
investment fund that protects and grows
wealth for the future.
Unlike traditional government funding
programmes, decisions about how funding
is invested and distributed will be made
independently of Ministers and successive
governments. This provides certainty for
marae and communities while ensuring
investment decisions remain focused on
long-term aspirations rather than changing
political priorities.
Te Rangatiratanga Trust is separate from
the Treaty settlement framework. It does not
replace, affect, or extinguish any existing
rights or obligations under

greens.org.nz

Te Tiriti o Waitangi. Rather than providing
Treaty settlement redress, the Trust
establishes a forward-looking investment
mechanism that supports the long-term
resilience and prosperity of marae and the
communities they serve.

Te Rākau Whakarauora
Restoration Fund
Intergenerational funds take time to mature,
but many marae and communities require
investment now.
Te Rākau Whakarauora Restoration Trust is a
time-limited establishment fund that provides
immediate investment in marae resilience
and restoration while Te Tōtara Haemata
Intergenerational Fund is established. It will
address urgent priorities such as restoring
buildings and infrastructure, strengthening
capability, improving climate resilience, and
supporting projects that support marae and
their surrounding communities to thrive.
Funding can support projects such as:

• Restoring marae buildings, fixtures,
and infrastructure.
• Strengthening emergency preparedness
and response capability.
• Improving climate resilience and
adaptation.
• Supporting capability development
and project delivery.
• Protecting taonga, wāhi tapu, and
cultural heritage.
• Enabling marae-led partnerships that
improve community wellbeing.
Te Rākau Whakarauora will also provide
an opportunity to establish and refine the
Trust’s funding pathways, ensuring future
investment through Te Tōtara Haemata
is effective, accessible, and responsive to
community aspirations.

Te Tōtara Haemata
Intergenerational Fund
At the heart of Te Waonui a Tāne is Te Tōtara
Haemata Intergenerational Fund, a permanent
intergenerational fund designed to preserve
and grow the Trust’s capital through
long-term investment.
Rather than spending the capital itself,
investment returns will be distributed to
support the aspirations of hapū and iwi of
Aotearoa, while protecting the capital for
future generations. This creates an enduring
source of funding that grows over time and
provides long-term certainty independent
of political cycles.
Through responsible investment, Te Tōtara
Haemata will create enduring intergenerational
prosperity and strengthen Māori economic
capacity while aligning with environmental
sustainability, social wellbeing, and the longterm success of future generations.
Investment opportunities may include:
• Renewable energy and climate transition
• Investment in whenua Māori
• Housing and regional development
• Māori enterprise and business growth
• Emerging industries and technologies

Decisions made closer
to communities
The Green Party recognises that marae,
hapū, and local communities are best placed
to determine their own priorities and define
what success looks like.
Funding distribution can follow a similar
approach to Whānau Ora models, using
regional commissioning bodies to ensure
decisions remain close to communities.

This approach reduces barriers to
accessing funding, supports smaller
marae that may have limited administrative
capacity, and allows communities to
determine their own priorities.
Funding criteria can also be broadened to
support a wide range of aspirations that are
usually not accounted for when determined
by Government, including:
Te Pūtake – Acknowledging the Past:
Supporting projects that recognise
whakapapa, protect cultural heritage,
and acknowledge the achievements and
contributions of tūpuna.
Te Puāwai – Acting Today and Investing
in Tomorrow: Supporting the holistic
revitalisation of marae and the aspirations
of hapori Māori, hapū, and iwi.
Te Poipoi – Community Partnership
Projects: Supporting marae to partner
with wider communities, councils, and
organisations on projects that benefit
the rohe.
Accountability can be maintained through
transparent reporting and whānaucentred outcome measures developed
with communities. Instead of quantitative
reporting, success can be measured
through outcomes including:
Cultural wellbeing – strengthening
whakapapa, tikanga, mātauranga Māori,
and identity.
Social wellbeing – supporting whānau
resilience, connection, and community
strength.
Commercial activity – employment
and training opportunities, tourism levels,
cost of living.
Environmental resilience – protecting
whenua and supporting kaitiakitanga.
Long-term wellbeing – number of
generations that have benefitted from
the funding.

Thriving Marae policy 2026

Funding the future

The funding is allocated in the first two years
from the Crown, and the Trust will determine
the investment and expenditure based on its
priorities. We have modelled that the Trust
will for the first four years spend $10 million a
year on establishment and associated costs,
$100 million a year on Te Rākau Whakarauora
Restoration Funding, and invest $2 billion in
seed funding for Te Tōtara Haemata.

The Green Party proposes an initial Crown
funding commitment of $2.44 billion with any
further Crown contributions to be determined
during the negotiation process. This amount
is aligned with other comparable long-term
public funds, including the New Zealand
Superannuation Fund’s initial $2.4 billion
contribution in 2003 and the Aboriginal and
Torres Strait Islander Land and Sea Future
Fund’s $2 billion contribution in 2019 in
Australia.

Using the Treasury investment return
assumptions in the 2026 Budget Economic
and Fiscal Update we can also estimate:
• The $2 billion invested through Te Tōtara
Haemata to grow to approximately $3.8
billion by 2039/40 before distributions.

The $2.44 billion investment is intended for the
establishment and maintenance of the Trust’s
components including Te Rākau Whakarauora
Restoration Fund and the seed funding for Te
Tōtara Haemata Intergenerational Fund for at
least 4 years until it has matured enough to
access returns.

• Annual distributions could provide between
$47 million and $118 million in ongoing
support once the fund has initially matured,
while maintaining the long-term capital base.
