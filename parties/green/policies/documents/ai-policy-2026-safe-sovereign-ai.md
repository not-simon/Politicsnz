AI policy 2026

AI regulation is needed to protect New Zealanders’
creativity, data and jobs and ensure that the benefits
of AI are shared and not captured by the massive
technology companies running the AI sector.
The Green Party will make sure the right
AI protections are in place.

The growing pressures to adopt
and embrace AI are unprecedented,
but AI is not a force of nature that we
have to surrender to. Government
has a role to safeguard the interests
of our creatives, our workers, our
environment, and the New
Zealand public.

This growing bubble presents risks
to jobs and economic stability.

New Zealanders are understandably
cautious about AI. The Green Party
shares this caution. When AI is
used thoughtfully and with
well-designed safeguards there
can be legitimate benefits, but the
rapid and unregulated explosion of
AI technologies presents significant
risks across our society and our
economy.

The best way to protect us, our
economy, and our sovereignty
from an uncertain future is to ensure
that AI is regulated properly. We
should and will implement the right
safeguards, before it’s too late to
put it back in the box.

We don’t have to surrender control
of our data, our privacy and the
things we create to massive overseas
tech companies that don’t support
the public interest.

The enormous investments
being made in AI are cause for
concern. Trillions of dollars have
been invested in new AI tools, and
companies cannot hope to see the
returns they expect.

AI policy 2026

AI must serve people, not replace
human judgement or democratic
decision-making
Artificial intelligence should support human judgement, not
replace it. Decisions that affect people’s rights, welfare, health,
education, employment, or access to public services must remain
subject to meaningful human oversight and appeal.

Policy implications
• No fully automated decisions
for anything that could make a
substantive difference to people’s
lives in any public services. Humans
managing AI tools must be able to
make decisions with integrity and
appropriate workloads.
• People are accountable and will be
held accountable for AI decisions.

• Everyone has a right to challenge
AI-assisted decisions, and to opt
out of being subject to AI assisted
decision- making.
• New Zealand should refuse to
purchase or deploy autonomous
weapons systems and support
an international ban on these, in
the same way the international
community has banned chemical
and biological weapons.

• Require public registers of AI systems
used by government agencies – full
transparency around AI use, including
where in policy processes it has been
used, and where or how it has been
used to inform decisions.

AI policy 2026

Data is a taonga: protect
data sovereignty and
community consent
The raw material of AI is data. New Zealanders, iwi, hapū,
communities, and creators should retain meaningful control over
how data about them is used. Language in particular is crucial to
identity and culture, and must be protected.

Policy implications
• Explicit consent standards for AI
training data.
• Appropriate remuneration for any
IP used for training AI models, and
genuinely deterrent fines for breaches.

• Strengthen the ability of the Privacy
Commissioner to enforce our privacy
laws, including codes relating to
biometric processing and facial
recognition technology, and introduce
penalties for serious privacy breaches.

• Strong protection of Māori data
sovereignty – specific regulation
requiring co-governance and codevelopment of Māori data and IP.
• Transparency regarding datasets used
by government AI systems.
• Neither people’s private information
which they have entrusted to
the government, nor classified
government data, shall be sent to third
parties, particularly for model training
or inference. Onshoring public data
should be prioritised.

AI policy 2026

AI must operate within
environmental limits
AI is not a virtual technology. It consumes large amounts of electricity,
water, land, and materials. New infrastructure should only proceed
where environmental and community benefits are clear.

Policy implications
• Mandatory environmental impact
assessments for hyperscale AI data
centres.
• New AI facilities must provide new
renewable generation to cover full
energy use rather than competing for
existing electricity supply.
• Water-use limits and public reporting
requirements.
• Community consultation and local
consent requirements.
• A pause on consenting new hyperscale
data centres until the regulatory
framework is established.

AI policy 2026

Ensure AI benefits are shared,
not concentrated
AI is creating unprecedented concentrations of economic power,
as well as enormous risks to the stability of the financial system.
Government should ensure gains benefit workers and communities
rather than being captured by global tech giants, and that the risks
are not socialised.

Policy implications
• Strong enforcement of tax law,
including application of withholding
tax rules so tech companies do not
offshore their profits.
• Update and pass the Fair Digital
News Bargaining Bill to ensure that
AI companies pay local media outlets
for data used in their AI summaries.
• Copyright and licensing protections
– in the first instance enforcing
current ones but look to develop
more if required.
• Use public-interest and open-source
alternatives wherever these are
available and viable.

• Worker participation in decisions
about workplace AI adoption.
• Children should not be forced to use
AI, particularly at school, and protect
the rights of children in line with the
UNCRC’s Joint Statement on Artificial
Intelligence and the Rights of the
Child.
• Collaborate globally with like-minded
countries to align AI regulation
and governance, establishing New
Zealand as a moral leader, building on
our work on the Christchurch Call.
• Government will encourage research
towards the development of Aotearoa
based and focused LLMs to avoid
dependence on foreign vendors.

• Prevent vendor lock-in by mandating
multiple and interchangeable AI
providers when using third-party
models.

AI policy 2026

Require transparent, trustworthy,
and accountable AI
People have a right to know when they are interacting with
AI and how it is being used.

Policy implications
• Labelling requirements for
AI-generated content.
• Establish an AI office, in the first
instance sitting within DPMC, and
looking over time to establish an
independent AI Commission with
appropriate powers to conduct
assurance, audits, and education.
• Algorithm audits for high-risk systems
– public oversight of some algorithms
through the AI regulator.
• Align and cooperate with international
best practice, including key aspects
of the EU AI Act, around disclosure
requirements, labelling requirements,
and audits of AI systems.
