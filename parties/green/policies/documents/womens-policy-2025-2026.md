# Women’s Policy 1 (Green Party policy document)

<!-- page 1 -->

Women’s Policy 1
The Green Party will prioritise Papatūānuku and foster harmony between communities, the
economy, and nature. The Greens will create an equitable and inclusive leadership landscape,
including for wāhine Māori. The Greens will protect women and girls from harm at home, at work,
in the community, and online, and strive for gender equity in all areas and at all times.

Vision
Women thrive, lead, and uplift our communities, and our connection to Papatūānuku is honoured.

Values and Principles
"He wāhine, he whenua, e ngaro ai te tangata"2
- Kaitiakitanga - Women often play nurturing and leadership roles in maintaining the
balance between people and nature. Papatūānuku, as whenua and nurturer, must herself
be nourished.

- Mana wāhine - Women’s dignity must be upheld and our collective wisdom respected.

- Manahau - Women's collective wellbeing and vitality must be supported. Addressing the
intergenerational impact of colonisation on wāhine Māori is a priority.

- Mana motuhake - Women’s right to self-determination must be protected and realised
and equitable opportunities provided. Women must have agency at all levels and our
voices must be heard. The tino rangatiratanga of wāhine Māori must be honoured.

- Manaakitanga - Women must be safe from discrimination and gender-based violence.

- Whanaungatanga - Women's wellbeing and that of our whānau and community are
interdependent. Our heart relationships must be supported, as individuals, as a collective,
and with other species.

- Kotahitanga - The interconnected and intersectional nature of women's experiences
across diverse backgrounds and identities must be recognised. We stand in solidarity with
all oppressed genders.

Policy Positions
1.

Kaitiakitanga

Issues
Many people have become disconnected from Papatūānuku, whenua and nurturer, an illusion of
separation that is enabled by advancing technologies. Colonisation imposed a patriarchal
worldview of domination over nature, suppressing cultures like te ao Māori that recognise
interconnection, and distancing people—by force or urbanisation—from the land of their
ancestors. This disconnection both enables and hides from view the damage to people and planet
that our industrial society is causing.

3.

Mana Motuhake

Issues
Gender disparities persist in leadership roles with wahine Māori, Pasifika women, and women in
other marginalised groups particularly under-represented. Wāhine Māori, in particular, have
suffered from the effects of colonisation and inter-generational violence and trauma which has
undermined their and whānau wellbeing, leadership, spirituality, knowledge, and rights, including
the loss of moko kauae. This is ongoing as wāhine Māori are often ignored and unacknowledged
by the Crown and others.
Women frequently face challenges in achieving pay parity with their male counterparts. Womendominated industries tend to receive lower pay, and women are more likely to work part-time or
take career breaks due to caregiving responsibilities. For women of Māori and Pasifika heritage,
the disparity is even more pronounced. Lower earnings for women are reflected in lower savings
for retirement and weaker economic resilience of families.

common problems. For women who experience multiple forms of discrimination—including
based on age, class, disability, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, gender identity, or migration
status—the obstacles to equality are even greater. Too many vulnerable women and their
children are trapped in violent situations, often at home.

5.

Whanaungatanga

Issues
Women often play an important caregiving role in our families and communities. In our current
economic system, this too often costs us time and money that we cannot afford and impacts
employment opportunities.
Interruptions to our relationships can cause harm that echoes across generations. For example,
there is a well-established pathway from colonisation to state care to gangs and/or prison, with
no clear pathway out.

Actions
5.1.

Enable women to care for themselves and their dependents, including by:
5.1.1.

Legally recognising and supporting informal caregivers as part of the care
system;

5.1.2.

Taking caring roles into account when providing leave from work;

5.1.3.

Providing comprehensive, appropriate support services for pregnant people; and

5.1.4.

Covering all birthing injuries to parents and babies under ACC.

5.2.

Uphold the value and wellbeing of mothers as a “circuit breaker” preventing
intergenerational harm, including by:
5.2.1.

Expanding programmes that prioritise wellbeing for women in rural, gang and
other “hard-to-reach” communities;

5.2.2.

Reforming the justice system to prioritise restorative, trauma-informed
approaches and maintain whānau connections; and

5.2.3.

Support whānau- and community-led care for at-risk children.

See also our Accident Compensation Policy, Children’s Policy, and Youth Policy.

6.

Kotahitanga

Issues
New Zealand’s feminist movements have historically centered Pākehā (white) women, but some
women face compounded inequities, including Māori, Pasifika, migrant, disabled, and queer
women.

Actions
6.1.

Ensure that policies supporting women centre wāhine Māori and the most
marginalised women, including by:
6.1.1.

Respecting that trans women are women and upholding their rights;

6.1.2.

Requiring all policy decision-making that affects marginalised women be made
with our direct participation and leadership; and

6.1.3.

Supporting organisations that recognise and respond to intersectionality.
