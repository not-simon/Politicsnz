# Land Use and Soils Policy (Green Party policy document)

<!-- page 1 -->

Land Use and Soils Policy
The Green Party will improve the ways we use and care for land, for the sake of current and
future generations, other species, and ecosystem health. We will ensure that land use and land
cover are managed in accordance with Te Tiriti o Waitangi. Protecting soils and minimising the
impacts of mining will be priorities.

Vision
Land and soil ecosystems are protected and regenerated for present and future generations, to
nurture our wellbeing and livelihoods, and provide food, medicine, and spiritual fulfilment.

Values and Principles
- Honour Te Tiriti o Waitangi: We affirm tino rangatiratanga of iwi, hapū and whānau as
kaitiaki of this whenua. The perspectives, approaches, needs, and aspirations of tangata
whenua are embedded in the philosophy and approach to land and soil management.

- Ecological Wisdom: We must respect land and soil as part of the ecosystems we rely on.
Soil ecosystems have intrinsic value, and humanity needs to steward the natural world by
understanding and working within the carrying capacity of the land.

- Social Responsibility: The need for equity in the sustainable use of land and soil resources
is acknowledged.

- Appropriate Decision-Making: Good governance (i.e., the processes by which institutions
and other parties make and implement decisions in order to manage resources efficiently
and ensure human rights), and strong institutions should underpin land and soil
management.

- Non-Violence: Conflict resolution over land and soil management should ensure no
violence is felt by te taiao, the creatures that call it home, and always honour Te Tiriti.

- The mauri of land - its cultural, spiritual, historical, conservation and ecological aspects are prioritised. At the same time, agricultural land is valued for its ability to produce
healthy food, fibre and wood and is regenerated and managed sustainably to ensure that
its productive potential is protected and enhanced.

Connected Policies
This policy discusses land use and soils within the context of Biodiversity and Environmental
Regeneration, alongside our Freshwater and Marine Policies. Land health is impacted by human
activity, including Agriculture, Forestry, Housing and Sustainable Communities, Waste and
Hazardous Substances, and Climate Change. Resource extraction from land can be minimised
through resource and Waste management, including circular economy practices.

Land Use Management

Issues
Extractive land uses that emphasise productivity and economic potential have dominated since
colonisation. Land used for farming and commercial forestry, urban areas, and infrastructure has
expanded by clearing or reclaiming vast areas of indigenous forest, wetlands, and other
ecosystems. This loss is ongoing, especially in lowland areas, as is the loss of highly fertile land
and soil to forestry and urban development. Water quality, watershed hydrology, habitat and
species composition, climate and carbon storage have all been negatively affected by changes
in land cover.

Soil Protection

Issues
Soil quality and health is being degraded by intensive practices in primary industries and urban
development and pollution. Common issues include soil compaction and over-fertilisation. The
past use of hazardous substances in industry, agriculture, and horticulture has left a legacy of
soil contamination. Naturally high rates of erosion have accelerated due to deforestation, grazing
animals, intensive land use, and climate change, causing hundreds of millions of tonnes of soil
to enter rivers each year, valuable topsoil to be lost, and stored carbon to be released.

Mining1

Issues
Mining is an exploitative process, which adversely impacts the ecological, historical,
archaeological and/or cultural significance of the land and sea. These impacts arise not only from
the physical disturbance and noise caused by extraction, but also from toxic substances released
by the extraction process. Mining often extracts materials that directly contribute to the climate
crisis through their greenhouse gas emissions. Furthermore, the profits usually go to large
corporations, while local communities pay for the clean-up. Mined materials are too often
exported out of our country, representing a loss to current and future generations.

Ensure that the regulatory framework for mining avoids and mitigates harms,
including:
3.1.1.

Honouring Te Tiriti o Waitangi, including upholding the right of Māori to exercise
tino rangatiratanga over their lands and resources, including minerals and
ecosystems and species impacted by mining;

3.1.2.

Limiting mining to essential materials:

3.1.2.1.

that are required for our transition to a just and sustainable circular
economy, meet essential needs, or serve another socially essential
purpose (as determined by public consultation);

3.1.2.2.

for which renewable alternatives are not available; and

3.1.2.3.

for which maximum recovery and recycling of available materials has been
achieved, but is proven insufficient for the needs of Aotearoa New Zealand;
and

3.1.3.

Only allowing mining methods that minimise the risk of environmental and social
damage;

3.1.4.

Taking a precautionary approach to new mining technologies, especially for
offshore mining;

3.1.5.

Prioritising the needs of the natural world and future generations and providing
strict environmental protections relating to both the location of the extraction,
so that mining in significant ecological areas is avoided, and the environmental
impacts of extraction techniques; and

3.1.6.

Requiring those responsible to rapidly remedy any adverse effects.

Ensure that resource consent decision-making in relation to mining:
3.2.1.

Protects ecological, social, cultural, and landscape values;

3.2.2.

3.2.3.

Considers the greenhouse gas contribution of mining activities and other

3.2.4.

Provides New Zealanders with equitable opportunities to be involved in decisions
about mining;

3.2.5.

Enables communities, whānau, hapū, and iwi to protect areas of historical,
archaeological, and/or cultural significance from mining, including the foreshore

3.2.6.

Upholds the right of landholders, including Māori, to deny access for mining on
