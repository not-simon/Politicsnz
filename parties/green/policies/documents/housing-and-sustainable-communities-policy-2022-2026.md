# Housing and Sustainable (Green Party policy document)

<!-- page 1 -->

Housing and Sustainable
Communities Policy
The Green Party will ensure people’s right to healthy, secure housing. We will focus on:
launching a massive public housing project that is funded, managed and run by the
government; providing for a substantial increase in Māori housing and the removal of barriers
to Māori housing; making housing accessibility and safety for disabled people a high priority;
reducing building-related greenhouse gas emissions; and creating frameworks to ensure
regenerative urban development that connects people within their communities.

Vision
Accessible, healthy, secure and sustainable housing for all in thriving communities.

Values and Principles
This housing policy must be consistent with the following values and principles:
- Honour Te Tiriti o Waitangi: Article 2 of Te Tiriti o Waitangi guarantees tangata whenua
tino rangatiratanga over their taonga, which includes their whenua. Honouring this
guarantee is fundamental to enabling housing developments, including papakāinga.
Funding should be prioritised for Māori housing developments, with ownership and
decision-making by Māori. Mātauranga Māori principles of design for housing and
communities should be broadly applied across the whole housing sector to build
adequate housing for all.

- Ecological Wisdom: Our communities must be life-sustaining. They should be designed,
built or renovated using principles, materials and processes that are regenerative and
minimise negative environmental impacts.

- Social Responsibility: People should have access to healthy, affordable and secure homes
that support wellbeing and enable connected, inclusive communities. Housing needs to
be designed as universally accessible so that all groups in the community have equitable
access to appropriate and affordable housing.

- Appropriate Decision-Making: Communities should be actively involved in decisions that
affect the places they live. Decision-making should particularly consider the interests of
those who have been marginalised, and actively ensure their inclusion in community
engagement processes.

- Non-Violence: Vested private interests that seek to maintain control of housing should
be mitigated by the government through legislation and engagement to neutralise the
power imbalance. No one should be forced to move out of their home without a suitable
alternative being provided.

Decommodification: Housing should be seen as a fundamental human need and right,

- not something that is traded for capital gains and profits that serve to perpetuate
inequality. Basically, houses should be homes.

Housing security: People should be able to live in their home or community for as long

- as they wish, regardless of ownership. Having generations of family/whānau living
together contributes to stable and healthy communities.

Housing for disabled people: Disabled people have the right to live in accessible housing

- both wherever and with whomever they choose to live.

Strategic Priorities
The Green Party’s strategic goals include:

“Healthy homes within thriving neighbourhoods will be available to everyone. People will
be part of caring communities with a strong heritage fabric and easy access to the
natural environment.”
Actions in this policy that will help achieve this include:
- Sufficiently resourcing papakāinga and other Māori-led housing solutions and Māori
community housing providers (1.2.1)

- Establishing a right to housing in legislation that requires the State to ensure all New
Zealanders are adequately housed in safe, healthy, accessible, affordable housing. (2.1.1)

- Establishing a Ministry for Green Works to ensure supply of suitable housing for all New
Zealanders (...) (3.4.1)

- Establishing representative housing committees in all local communities that reflect both
homeowners and renters (4.6.2)

- Creating regulatory structures, including affordable housing requirements, that require
developers to provide for equitable developments. (4.3.2)

Connected Policies
To address the housing crisis, alongside the actions below the Government in the Economy must
incentivise productive Business investment and savings to support Livelihoods, and
disincentivise property speculation. Sustainable development is an important part of protecting
Biodiversity and Environmental Regeneration, including restoring urban Freshwater ecosystems.
Key to sustainable development are minimising suburban sprawl in favour of regenerative Land
Use and Soils, reducing the impacts of Climate Change, minimising Energy use, enabling and
prioritising low-carbon Transport, and focusing on Community in the Economy. See our Kaupapa
Māori Policy for pathways for returning Māori ancestral lands to Māori ownership.

2.

Housing as a Public Good and Basic Right

Issues
House buying and renting are increasingly unaffordable for many. Thousands of mainly lowincome and other housing-marginalised New Zealanders do not have adequate housing, living
unhoused or in cars, motels, camping grounds, or overcrowded or substandard housing. Housing
is often inappropriate for marginalised groups, including disabled people and people from
communities in which large, extended families live together. Inadequate housing leads to adverse
health outcomes. This housing crisis, rooted in the commodification of housing, is exacerbated
by land banking and vacant, underutilised housing stock.

Actions
A. Housing as a Human Right
2.1. Treat housing as a human right by:
2.1.1. Establishing a right to housing in legislation that requires the State to ensure all New
Zealanders are adequately housed in safe, healthy, accessible, affordable housing;
and
2.1.2. Supporting those at high risk of facing housing disadvantage to be fully housed in
the communities of their choice.
2.2. Ensure that homelessness is rare, brief, and non-recurring by:
2.2.1. Providing healthy, safe and accessible permanent housing to unhoused people in a
way that upholds autonomy and dignity;
2.2.2. In cases where permanent housing is not appropriate, providing adequate shelters
and facilities; and
2.2.3. Adopting a housing-first approach with wrap-around support for people who have
both housing needs and other complex needs, including those who have been
evicted or had to leave their home due to domestic violence.
2.3. Provide disabled people with equitable choices about where they live, including by:
2.3.1. Providing support to transition from residential services to living in the community;
and
2.3.2. Providing timely, transparent, needs-based funding for housing modifications,
including for people living in papakāinga.
2.4. Decommodify the housing market by:
2.4.1. Investigating capping the number of houses that may be bought by an individual,
private company, or family estate; and
2.4.2. Introducing a value uplift levy where rezoning, the provision of public transport
and/or other public works result in increased land development potential.

B. Public housing
2.5. Ensure that everyone who does not own their own home has access to adequate public
housing by:
2.5.1. Protecting, enhancing and expanding public housing stock;
2.5.2. Phasing out means-testing and background-testing for public housing as more
public housing stock is built;
2.5.3. Giving priority for public housing to people who are living in poor accommodation
and those at high risk of extremely poor outcomes;
2.5.4. Making Kāinga Ora a government department rather than a Crown entity to ensure

public housing supply;
2.5.5. Opposing any privatisation of public housing, including public housing land; and
2.5.6. Creating public, purpose-built, rent-assisted living units for older people in all areas
that maintain community inclusiveness.
2.6. Ensure the approach to developing new public housing:
2.6.1. Fully honours Te Tiriti;
2.6.2. Uses high-quality urban design and built environments, centering new housing
developments around pre-existing communities and enabling social connections in
public housing areas; and
2.6.3. Maximises the proportion of housing supply from public and Māori housing, by
increasing the availability of government, council, community and iwi-provided
housing, aiming for universal public housing for all.
2.7. Support and partner with community housing groups whose objectives and methods
align with the principles outlined in our policy, including by:
2.7.1. Providing direct government funding for public and community housing projects,

3.

Sustainable Buildings

Issues
Since the early 1990s, housing construction has trended toward larger, expensive homes, while
failing to provide enough affordable, accessible homes. Construction produces high carbon
emissions and waste, and supplier monopolies exacerbate supply and affordability issues. Land
has been used inefficiently, causing urban sprawl, rising land prices, and greater infrastructure
needs. Many of the 1.8 million existing houses, especially rentals, are substandard and lack
resilience to climate change, natural hazards and utility disruptions.

Actions
3.1. Ensure, through regulation and standards, that buildings and their surroundings are
designed, constructed, renovated and repurposed to provide:
3.1.1. Efficient and effective heating and cooling of buildings;
3.1.2. Water conservation;
3.1.3. Resilience against natural hazards, including identifying and preparing for climate
change-related threats, and outages of network utilities;
3.1.4. Universal access and design;
3.1.5. For the physical and mental wellbeing of communities and residents of buildings,
including the safety of disabled people in an emergency;
3.1.6. Safe access by active and public transport, including at night;
3.1.7. Minimal embodied and operational carbon and regenerative approaches towards
energy and waste; and
3.1.8. Long life expectancy for buildings and infrastructure, using regenerative
architectural principles.
3.2. Ensure that public financial support for the renovation and repurposing of existing
buildings and the construction of new buildings is efficient, equitable, and sufficient to
achieve the above goals, with priorities for funding being:
3.2.1. Māori and Pasifika housing solutions;
3.2.2. Providing healthy buildings;
3.2.3. Reducing climate change impacts;
3.2.4. Ensuring universal access and use for disabled people and older people;
3.2.5. Improving resilience against natural hazards (including managed retreat) and
network utility outages;

4.

Sustainable Communities

Issues
Poor quality urban environments reduce mobility, community engagement and belonging, and
environmental sustainability. Gentrification of urban areas too often forces low-income residents
out of the area or into substandard housing.

Actions
4.1. Support urban development that:
4.1.1. Creates vibrant, connected streets and public spaces at a human scale1;
4.1.2. Equitably adopts universal urban design principles to ensure accessibility for
disabled people;
4.1.3. Prioritises active and public transport over cars by design, with walkable2 urban
fabrics;
4.1.4. Promotes ecosystem health and community access to green space; and
4.1.5. Protects the rural-urban boundary and valuable food-growing soils.
4.2. Protect and restore nature in and around our cities by:
4.2.1. Taking an ecosystem-based approach to urban development;
4.2.2. Planting and protecting urban trees and urban ngahere;
4.2.3. Establishing guidelines for indigenous planting in urban areas, such as unused road
strips, railway corridors, esplanade reserves and other areas;
4.2.4. Establishing green networks and ecological corridors through developed areas,
including waterways; and
4.2.5. Prohibiting covenants that undermine positive environmental or social outcomes.
4.3. Equitably increase urban density by:
4.3.1. Reviewing and reducing the extent, scope and significance of ‘special character
areas’, with a refocus that prioritises Māori heritage, natural heritage, and providing
a strong heritage fabric;
4.3.2. Creating regulatory structures, including affordable housing requirements, that
require developers to provide for equitable developments; and
4.3.3. Enabling a localised assessment of urban density and form with leadership by mana
whenua and local government organisations.
4.4. Ensure that all New Zealanders, regardless of where they live, have access to all basic
services in close proximity3 including by:
4.4.1. Developing regional networks of towns and villages that are well connected by rail
and have equitable internal transport;
