# Green Party: track record, 2008–2026

> How reliable have the Greens been? What did they promise, what did they deliver in government, where did they
> compromise, and do their actions match their values? Current to 23 October 2026.
> Data: [`data/promises/green.json`](../../data/promises/green.json) (32 entries) · Profile: [README](./README.md) ·
> 2026 policies: [./policies/README.md](./policies/README.md)

## How to read this

- **kept**: delivered substantially as promised. **partially-kept**: delivered in a smaller or later form.
  **broken**: not delivered, or the party acted against the commitment. **abandoned**: the party dropped or
  scaled back the commitment. **not-yet-due**: a 2026 pledge.
- "Broken" here means **not delivered**, whatever the reason. Each entry's `detail` says whether the Greens
  actually had the power to deliver. Greens have only ever been a **support partner**. They never held a Cabinet
  seat or a majority, so most of their wins depended on Labour agreeing. Where a Green-backed measure was carried by a
  Labour minister, we say so.
- The tracker is a sample of the most significant, verifiable commitments. It is not every manifesto line.

Of the 22 commitments that can be assessed, **15 (68%) were kept or partially kept**. Most of these date from
2017–20, when the Greens had a written agreement and Labour needed their votes. Two of the six "broken" items were
cases where the Greens acted against their own stated principles (waka-jumping, Green School). The other four were
outcomes they could not force.

## 2. Term by term

### 2008–2011: opposition, with a policy MoU with National
- After the 2008 election the Greens signed a memorandum of understanding with John Key's National government
  (April 2009). It covered policy cooperation, not confidence and supply, and lapsed in 2011
  ([Fifth National Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_National_Government_of_New_Zealand)). This is the
  only formal Green arrangement with National. We could not retrieve the MoU text in this pass: the Beehive PDF
  link returns 404.
- **Assessment:** shows the Greens could work across the aisle on specific issues while keeping their independence.

### 2011–2014: opposition
- The Greens' strongest opposition term, with 11.06% and 14 MPs. Russel Norman named his main wins as forcing the
  government to reverse its plan to open Schedule 4 conservation land to mining, and leading the campaign for the
  citizens-initiated referendum on asset sales. 67.3% voted against the sell-downs, but the referendum was not binding
  and the sales went ahead
  ([RNZ profile](https://www.radionz.co.nz/news/political/264907/profile-russel-norman);
  [Fifth National Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_National_Government_of_New_Zealand)).

### 2014–2017: opposition; the Turei crisis
- Labour and the Greens went into the 2017 election under a memorandum of understanding to campaign together. NZ First later used this as a reason not to negotiate directly with the Greens ([Herald](http://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/news/article.cfm?c_id=1&objectid=11931588)).
- In July 2017 Metiria Turei disclosed past benefit fraud to make the case for welfare reform. Support briefly rose to
  15%. Then two MPs quit the caucus and on 9 August 2017 she resigned, and the vote fell to 6.27%. This is the clearest
  example of a "values" campaign that went wrong electorally
  ([Herald](http://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/news/article.cfm?c_id=1&objectid=11901089);
  [Stuff](https://www.stuff.co.nz/national/politics/95549623/greens-in-crisis-metiria-turei-stays-on-despite-mps-quitting)).

### 2017–2020: confidence & supply, three ministers outside Cabinet
**Delivered**
- **Zero Carbon Act (2019)**: net-zero 2050 target, Climate Change Commission and five-yearly emissions budgets.
  Passed with almost unanimous support; National backed it from the first reading. Biogenic methane was given a
  separate target (24–47% below 2017 by 2050) rather than net zero.
- **Cannabis referendum** held in 2020; it narrowly failed, with 50.7% voting no.
- **Medicinal cannabis** legalised (December 2018) through the government's bill. The Green member's bill had been
  voted down.
- **End to new offshore oil and gas exploration** (April 2018).
- **Healthy Homes Guarantee Act 2017**; **Mental Health Commission** re-established (Te Hiringa Mahara).
- **Refugee quota** raised to 1,500 from 2020, the Greens' "double the quota" pledge.
- **Renters' rights** (2020): rent rises limited to once a year, no-cause evictions ended, rental bidding banned. No
  rent cap.
- **Benefits**: up $25 a week from April 2020. The bigger increases came in 2021–22.

**Not delivered or compromised**
- **Light rail to Auckland Airport**: not started by 2020, and still not built when Labour left office.
- **Waka-jumping law**: the Greens voted for it in 2018 against their long-held position, because of their duty to
  let the Labour–NZ First deal be implemented. A spokesperson said negotiators "forgot" to register their opposition.
  Shaw called it the party's "most difficult decision". In July 2020 they voted to repeal it, and Peters called them
  "untrustworthy".
- **Green School grant** (August 2020): $11.7m for a private school, against Green policy. Shaw apologised for an
  "error of judgment" and the grant became a loan.
- **Iraq and Afghanistan deployments**: the Greens had been the most consistent critics in Parliament, but did little
  in government to oppose extensions (Hansard). Peters played down Green claims that the 2019 Iraq withdrawal was
  their win ([Herald](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/winston-peters-a-bit-hard-to-argue-decision-to-bring-kiwi-troops-home-was-a-green-win/WHWKCWTU2CI5CJIN3QWPCIEPZY/)).

### 2020–2023: cooperation agreement, two ministers outside Cabinet, Labour majority
**Delivered or advanced (under Green ministers or with Green support)**
- **2030 Paris target raised** from a 30% to a 50% cut in net emissions (October 2021, Shaw).
- **Clean Car Discount** (July 2021 to December 2023). EVs rose to about 27% of new car sales by the end of 2023.
- **Benefits indexed to wages** and lifted by $32–55 a week (Budget 2021). RNZ reported this reversed the 1991 cuts in
  real terms.
- **Drug checking** made permanently legal (2021), a world first, through a government bill.
- **Discounted public transport** for Community Services Card holders made permanent from July 2023. This is a late,
  partial version of the 2017 "Green Transport Card".

**Not delivered**
- **Wealth tax**: ruled out by Ardern during the campaign. A majority Labour government did not need Green votes.
- **Agricultural emissions pricing** (pledged for 2025): not implemented before Labour lost office. The coalition
  dropped it in June 2024.
- **Light rail**: about $228m spent with no track laid. Cancelled in November 2023.
- **The 2023 "policy bonfire"**: Hipkins dropped or delayed several measures the Greens wanted, including hate-speech
  law and lowering the voting age to 16
  ([RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/483875/watch-tvnz-rnz-merger-scrapped-income-insurance-and-hate-speech-laws-delayed);
  [1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2023/03/13/pms-policy-bonfire-voting-age-bill-transport-schemes-reprioritsed/)).

**Internal cost.** Members and former MPs (Norman, Bradford, Delahunty) said the agreement gave the party too little
leverage. Shaw survived two leadership challenges, in 2021 and 2022
([RNZ, Jan 2022](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/459250/green-party-discontent-members-walk-ex-mps-criticise-leadership)).

### 2023–2026: opposition
- **Legislation passed from opposition:** Teanau Tuiono's bill restoring citizenship to Samoans born 1924–49 passed
  unanimously (November 2024).
- **Member's bills that failed:** Shaw's environmental Bill of Rights bill (defeated at first reading, April 2024).
  Davidson's right-to-repair bill passed its first reading but stalled after NZ First withdrew support.
- **Reversals of Green-era gains** by the National-led government, all opposed by the Greens:
  - Clean Car Discount repealed (December 2023);
  - Auckland light rail cancelled;
  - agriculture kept out of the ETS;
  - methane target lowered (October 2025);
  - 90-day no-cause evictions restored (December 2024);
  - Te Aka Whai Ora abolished (February 2024).

  Much of what the Greens won in 2017–23 has proved easy to reverse.
- **Votes:** consistent opposition to the coalition's Treaty Principles Bill, Fast-track Approvals Act, Māori wards
  referendums, three strikes and the Electoral Amendment Act 2025 (enrolment cut-off, prisoner voting ban). One
  notable cross-party vote: the Greens voted for the coalition's online casino bill at its first reading in July 2025
  ([RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/political/566995/online-casino-bill-greens-vote-with-coalition-govt-to-pass-first-reading)).
  We did not find their stated reasons in this pass.
- **Conduct:** more MPs lost or disciplined this term than any other party. See §5 and the [profile](./README.md#6-caucus-departures-and-discipline-20232026).

## 4. Did they stick to their word?

- **On coalition partners: yes.** The Greens have only ever backed Labour-led governments. They have said so before
  each election and kept to it.
- **On their core agenda: largely yes, when they had leverage.** The 2017 agreement was mostly honoured. Zero Carbon,
  the referendum, Healthy Homes and the Mental Health Commission were all delivered.
- **On process principles: not always.** The 2018 waka-jumping vote and the 2020 Green School grant broke stated Green
  positions to keep the government arrangement working or to win a local project. In 2024 the party used the
  waka-jumping law itself to remove Tana, a law it had joined National in trying to repeal in 2020
  ([Te Ao Māori News, 17 Oct 2024](https://teaonews.co.nz/2024/10/17/greens-unanimously-vote-to-remove-darleen-tana-from-parliament)).
  Three senior Pasifika members resigned in protest (see the [profile, §6](./README.md#6-caucus-departures-and-discipline-20232026)).
- **On economic promises: scaled back over time, then pushed forward again.** The 2020 wealth tax and the 2023
  Guaranteed Minimum Income were never achieved. The 2026 tax plan is noticeably more modest: the wealth tax
  threshold rose from $2m to $10m. The 2026 platform adds new state intervention in markets: KiwiMart, rent caps and
  Kiwipower.

## 6. Trends

1. **Better at the ballot box in opposition than in government.** The vote fell from 10.7% to 6.3% at the 2017 crisis
   election, recovered only to 7.9% after the first government term, then jumped to 11.6% in opposition. 2026 polling
   is at a record 12–16%.
2. **Leverage drives delivery.** The 2017–20 term, when Labour needed Green votes, delivered far more than 2020–23,
   when it did not.
3. **Gains are easy to reverse.** Much of the 2017–23 legacy has been rolled back since 2023: Clean Car Discount,
   light rail, farm pricing, the methane target, tenancy rules. The Zero Carbon Act's framework has survived.
4. **The caucus has become less stable.** Five MPs from the 2023 caucus or their replacements left early:
   Ghahraman, Collins (died), Shaw, Tana and Doyle. Kerekere had quit in 2023. Three MPs this term faced conduct
   findings or criminal charges: Ghahraman, Tana and Genter.
5. **The platform has moved from "green growth" to economic populism.** Under Shaw the party courted business and
   fiscal credibility (the 2017 Budget Responsibility Rules). Under Swarbrick and Davidson it campaigns on wealth taxes,
   a state supermarket and public ownership, while dropping the most expensive 2023 designs.
