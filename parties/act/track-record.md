# ACT New Zealand: track record, 2011–2026

This page covers promises against delivery, reliability, how ACT's actions in government lined up with its stated principles, and trends. Each entry, with its sources, is in [`data/promises/act.json`](../../data/promises/act.json). The party profile is in [README.md](./README.md) and 2026 policies are in [policies/README.md](./policies/README.md).

## How to read this

**Status definitions** (applied identically to every party in this archive):
- **kept**: delivered substantially as promised.
- **partially-kept**: some of it delivered, or delivered late or watered down.
- **broken**: not delivered, for whatever reason (ACT's own failure, a coalition partner's veto, or a lack of power).
- **abandoned**: ACT itself dropped or traded away the commitment, usually in coalition negotiations.
- **in-progress**: underway but not finished.
- **not-yet-due**: a 2026 promise.

**Context matters.** ACT had a single MP from 2011 to 2020 and was in opposition from 2017 to 2023. Being a junior partner limits what it can deliver. Many items below were *coalition-agreement* commitments, so they are a fair test of whether ACT secured and then delivered what it negotiated. Others were *campaign* promises that ACT traded away. The sample covers ACT's headline promises; it is not every manifesto line.

**Summary.**
- Of the commitments ACT secured in the **2023 coalition agreement**, most were delivered, and quickly: the Ministry for Regulation, the Regulatory Standards Act, charter schools, three strikes, interest deductibility, the Arms Act rewrite, 90-day trials, the Reserve Bank mandate, repeal of s7AA, and the Deputy PM rotation. The clear coalition failures were the 2017-size public service and the live-export reversal.
- ACT's biggest **campaign** promises fared worse when they met coalition partners. The Treaty-principles referendum was dropped in negotiations, and the Treaty Principles Bill was killed by National and NZ First. ACT's tax plan gave way to National's, and the abolition of demographic ministries, repeal of the Zero Carbon Act and abolition of the Māori seats were left out of the deal.
- In short, ACT is **highly reliable at delivering what it negotiates**, but negotiated away a good part of what it campaigned on.

## Alignment: did ACT's actions match its stated principles?

ACT's stated values are individual freedom, personal responsibility, "smaller, smarter government", equality before the law regardless of race, and freedom of expression "without restriction other than for incitement, criminal nuisance or defamation" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACT_New_Zealand)).

**Strongly consistent:**
- **Regulation and law-making quality.** ACT pursued this from Hide's 2006 bill through to the 2025 Act, and set up a dedicated ministry. It is the clearest example of a long-held policy turned into law.
- **"One law for all" / equal treatment.** ACT carried the Treaty Principles Bill to a vote despite record opposition, pushed the "need not race" directive, repealed s7AA, and removed ACC's ethnic targets. ACT presents these as applying its stated policy; critics, including the Waitangi Tribunal and Te Pāti Māori, see a breach of Treaty obligations. Either way, the actions match the stated policy.
- **Liberty-based dissent inside the coalition.** ACT voted against or withheld support from measures it saw as intrusive or protectionist: the Fair Digital News Bargaining Bill (agree to disagree), the under-16 social media ban (2025 and 2026), the modern slavery bill, and gift-card expiry rules. It also opposed forced break-ups of supermarkets and power companies.
- **Earlier record.** Seymour was the lone vote against the 2019 firearms ban and campaigned on the Arms Act rewrite he later helped deliver. He also championed End of Life Choice.

**Tensions:**
- **Small government vs outcome.** ACT promised to return the public service to its 2017 size. Thousands of roles were cut, but full-time-equivalent numbers in June 2026 (63,912) were about where they were in June 2023, and far above 2017. ACT ministers were part of the government that set the spending path. ACT's 2026 fiscal plan in effect concedes this by re-promising 2017 spending levels.
- **Free expression vs gang patch ban.** ACT's principles allow only narrow limits on expression, yet it voted for the 2024 gang patch ban, a National policy.
- **Good law-making vs urgency.** ACT champions careful, principled law-making through the Regulatory Standards Act. Yet the government passed the 2025 pay-equity changes, led by ACT's van Velden, under urgency, without a select-committee stage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_Pay_Amendment_Act_2025)).
- **Immigration.** ACT's 2023 agreement leaned towards liberalising (for example a higher RSE cap and easier family visas). Its 2026 platform has turned restrictive, with benefit stand-downs, a work-visa surcharge and abolition of the permanent-resident category.
- **The referendum "condition".** In 2022 ACT said a Treaty referendum would be a condition of coalition. It dropped that condition in 2023, which shows the gap between campaign framing and negotiating reality.

## Trends (2011 → 2026)

1. **From vehicle to force.** ACT went from 1.07% (2011), 0.69% (2014) and 0.50% (2017), surviving only through the Epsom arrangement, to 7.58% (2020) and 8.64% (2023). It polls about 6–11% in 2026. Its influence shifted from a single member's bill to three Cabinet ministers and a Deputy PM.
2. **Delivery improved with seats.** With one MP, ACT's main win was a conscience-vote member's bill (End of Life Choice) and a few concessions in support agreements (charter schools). With 11 MPs it secured and largely delivered about 20 coalition commitments within three years. Several of them (charter schools, the Regulatory Standards Act, three strikes) revived policies that a previous government had reversed.
3. **Reversals.** ACT's signature policies have been reversed by Labour before: charter schools in 2018 and three strikes in 2022. Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori opposed the 2024–25 versions, so their future depends on the 2026 result.
4. **Consistent priorities.** Smaller government, deregulation, longer sentences, firearms-owner rights and "equal citizenship" run through every campaign since 2014. The Treaty and co-governance became the dominant theme from 2022.
5. **Party organisation.** ACT has gained donors and staff: it overtook Labour in 2025 donations, held a 2024 campaign-culture review, entered local government (ACT Local, 2025), and changed its deputy (McKee replaced van Velden in 2026).
