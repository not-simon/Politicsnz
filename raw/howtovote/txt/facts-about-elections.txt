# 2026 General Election Facts about New Zealand elections
New Zealand has an electoral system voters can trust. There are many checks and balances to make sure parliamentary elections are fair and the results are accurate.
We’ve put together these facts about New Zealand’s parliamentary elections to answer some common questions and misconceptions about our electoral system.
If there’s anything you think is missing, or if there are any questions about elections you’d like answered, please contact us.

## Before you vote
If you’re eligible to enrol to vote, and you live in New Zealand, then you must do so.
If you’re eligible to enrol to vote, but you do not live in New Zealand, then it’s your choice whether you enrol or not.
Are you eligible to enrol and vote? [Are you eligible to enrol and vote? -> https://elections.nz/enrolling/get-ready-to-enrol/are-you-eligible-to-enrol-and-vote]
To vote in a general election you need to be enrolled by midnight, 13 days before Election Day.
For the 2026 General Election this means you can't enrol or update your details after midnight, 25 October 2026.
If you’re of New Zealand Māori descent, you can choose between the general roll and the Māori roll.
You will only be put on the roll you choose – there’s no ‘default’ roll for Māori, and you will not be put on any roll automatically.
You can change rolls at any time, except:
- in the 3 months before a general election,
- in the 3 months before the local elections, or
- during a parliamentary by-election if the change would move you into the electorate where the by-election is occurring.
If you’re not of New Zealand Māori descent, you must enrol on the general roll.
No matter which roll you’re on, you get to choose from the same list of parties with your party vote.
What is the Māori Electoral Option? [What is the Māori Electoral Option? -> https://elections.nz/maori-enrolment/about-the-option/about/what-is-the-maori-electoral-option]
Enrolling to vote isn’t something you do once – you need to keep your enrolment up to date to ensure you’re ready to vote.
We need your current address, as this determines which electorate you’ll vote in for parliamentary elections.
If you’ve moved house since you last voted (or since you last updated your enrolment) then you need to let us know. Updating your address isn’t something that happens automatically.
If your address is not kept up to date, it could mean you are eventually removed from the electoral roll. If we have your phone number or email address we will text or email you to update your details before this happens.
If we receive returned electoral mail from the address we have for you, we will try and contact you using the other information we have on record. This may include email or phone.
If we’re unable to reach you, you’ll be placed on the dormant roll. When you’re on the dormant roll, you’re still eligible to vote but won’t appear on the printed roll at voting places. This means you’ll have to make a special vote. If your new address is in a different electorate to your old address, it may also mean that only your party vote will count.
Being on the dormant roll does not affect your roll type (general or Māori).
Once you’ve been on the dormant roll for 3 years, you will be removed from the electoral roll. We’re required to do this under the Electoral Act 1993.
To come off the dormant roll you just need to update your address details – but be aware that you need to do this before voting starts.
To make sure you’re ready to vote for the 2026 General Election, check and update your details before midnight, Sunday 25 October 2026.
The only other circumstances where we remove someone from the electoral roll would be if they have passed away, if they have moved overseas, or if they have become ineligible for another reason such as being sentenced to a prison term.
We never change people from the Māori roll to the general roll or from the general roll to the Māori roll without their permission. If you’ve checked your details and you don’t think you’re on the correct roll, please get in touch with us on 0800 36 76 56 or enquiries@elections.govt.nz and our team can investigate. [enquiries@elections.govt.nz -> mailto:enquiries@elections.govt.nz]
## In the voting place
When you vote in a parliamentary election, your voting paper will be just that: paper.
You vote by marking your voting paper with a pen, then putting it in a ballot box.
There are two exceptions:
- If you’re blind, or you have a physical disability which means you can’t mark your voting paper, you can use our telephone dictation service.
- If you’re voting from overseas, you can download your voting paper – but you still have to manually mark it – e.g. with a pen or stylus, or using a touchscreen.
Voting machines aren’t used at New Zealand elections.
You don’t need to take your driver licence, your passport, or anything else with you to a voting place. No ID is required.
Your EasyVote card, if you have one, will make voting faster – but you can vote without it.
When you go to vote, you’re either marked off the printed roll at the voting place, or your details are recorded. During the official count, we compare all the rolls from all the voting places in each electorate to make sure everyone has only voted once.
The voting place worker who issues your voting paper won’t write anything on it.
A stamp and a sticker will be added to the voting paper, but these don’t affect whether or not your vote is counted. We will count your vote as long as your intention is clear.
If you’re not able to mark your voting papers independently, a voting place worker can do this for you. They will only ever do this at your request.
Candidates and parties can appoint scrutineers to be observers in voting places and when votes are counted. Scrutineers play an important role in our elections. They provide independent assurance to voters that elections are being conducted fairly.
Scrutineers are usually given seats along one side of the voting place. They may be wearing a rosette showing which party they represent and may also take notes.
Scrutineers may wear a rosette with the logo and colours of the party they represent. Scrutineers are not voting place staff and are not employed by the Electoral Commission.
During a general election more than 20,000 people work at voting places across the country. These people are paid workers, not volunteers. They’re employed from the local community to work during the election period.
You’ll be able to recognise our voting place workers because they’ll be wearing orange vests. They’ll also have name tags identifying them as Electoral Commission employees.
Voting place workers do not wear party rosettes in the voting place. If you see someone in a voting place who is wearing a rosette, they are most likely a scrutineer. Scrutineers are appointed by candidates or parties to observe voting and the counting of votes.
Every voter at a general election gets to choose from the same list of parties with their party vote.
This is true no matter which roll you’re on (Māori or general) or which electorate you’re enrolled in.
Everywhere in New Zealand is covered by both a Māori electorate and a general electorate.
Every voting place in New Zealand will have an electoral roll for both the Māori electorate and the general electorate it is located in.
Some voting places may also have electoral rolls for neighbouring electorates, especially if they're close to the boundary of two or more electorates. This won't be the case for all voting places, however - and if you vote outside your electorate you may have to make a special vote.
When you go to vote, you’ll be marked off the roll at the voting place. If you can’t be marked off the roll, you’ll make a special vote.
During the official count, we compare all the rolls from all the voting places in each electorate to make sure everyone has only voted once. We also check all the special votes.
If someone appears to have voted more than once, we investigate. If it is determined to be a case of multiple voting, all their votes are removed from the count. In some cases, they’ll be referred to the Police.
We have systems in place to keep track of every voting paper we issue. Every voting paper and every ballot box have unique identifiers, so we know exactly how many votes have been issued, how many ballot boxes there are, and how many votes we expect to be in each ballot box when we start counting votes after the close of voting.
We store the ballot boxes containing voting papers in secure locations. This may mean moving ballot boxes from voting places to secure storage at the end of each day.
## Counting the votes
All votes at a general election are counted by hand. This includes votes cast before election day.
We don’t use vote counting machines.
How are general election votes counted? (elections.nz) [How are general election votes counted? -> https://elections.nz/democracy-in-nz/about-elections/how-are-general-election-votes-counted/]
On election day, we do the ‘preliminary count’. The preliminary count includes all the ordinary votes cast on or before election day. This gives us the preliminary results, which are released on election night.
Immediately after election day, we start the official count. During the official count, we count all of the ordinary votes again, and also check and count special votes.
When counting is complete the results are recorded manually and then entered into our Election Management System. The system adds up the totals to produce the overall results which are published online.
The Election Management System was developed and is hosted in New Zealand. It regularly undergoes independent security testing and certification.
We publish the preliminary and official results from each voting place for transparency, and so people can add up the totals themselves if they wish to.
The number of votes for each party and candidate is recorded, but we do not know or record who people voted for.
Votes of no confidence are not a feature of our elections. Any voting paper where we can’t determine the intention of the voter is recorded as an informal vote. We record and publish the number of informal votes, but they don’t count towards the election result.
Even in cases where a voting paper has been drawn on, we’ll count votes for parties and candidates if the intention of the voter is clear.
If the party you voted for doesn’t make it into Parliament, your vote doesn’t go to another party.
The official results show how many party votes each party got and their share of the party vote. These results determine which parties win seats in Parliament.
To calculate how many seats in Parliament each party will get, we only use the votes for parties who make it into Parliament, either by winning an electorate seat or receiving 5% or more of the party vote. This means the share of seats in Parliament each of these parties gets will be higher than their share of the overall party vote.
There are two types of votes at a general election: ordinary votes and special votes.
If your name is on the printed electoral roll at the voting place when you go to vote, you’ll cast an ordinary vote.
If your name is not on the printed electoral roll at the voting place, you’ll cast a special vote.
You might not be on the printed electoral roll at the voting place you go to vote at if:
- you’re voting from outside your electorate (or from overseas)
- you’re on the unpublished roll
- you’re not enrolled or need to update your enrolment details
- you enrolled after Writ Day and before the enrolment deadline (midnight, 13 days before Election Day)
Both sorts of votes are worth the same when it comes to the final result!
The only real difference is that if you’re making a special vote, you’ll need to fill out an extra form. This means it may take you a little bit longer to complete your vote at a voting place.
Special votes are counted during the official count. Before special votes are counted, we check each voter is enrolled.
## Election advertising and campaigns
On election day all election signs, posters and billboards need to be taken down. Election flyers or pamphlets can’t be distributed that day, and any digital or social media advertising must stop.
In addition, during advance voting, election advertising can’t be placed within 10 metres of the entrance to a voting place.
Election advertising can be published at any other time. Parties and candidates have spending limits which apply in the 3 months before election day.
All election advertising must have a promoter statement, telling you the name and address of the person who initiated the ad.
About election advertising (elections.nz) [About election advertising -> https://elections.nz/guidance-and-rules/advertising-and-campaigning/about-election-advertising/]
Although election advertising may be published at any time, councils and local authorities may have their own rules about the placement of election signs. To find out the rules in your area, you should contact your local council.
Election signs (elections.nz) [Election signs -> https://elections.nz/guidance-and-rules/advertising-and-campaigning/election-signs/]
Registered political parties may get funding to pay for advertising on TV, radio and the internet. This is called the broadcasting allocation.
Parties can apply for funding even if they’re not registered – but they’ll only get the funding if they’re registered before Parliament dissolves.
Parties can use their broadcasting allocation for placement and production costs for TV, radio and internet advertising.
Parties can also use their own funds (within their election expense limits) to pay for production costs for TV, radio and internet advertising, and for placement of internet advertising. They cannot use their own funds for placement of TV and radio advertising.
The Electoral Commission allocates funding to each eligible party according to statutory criteria.
## After the election
The preliminary results on election night are from the first count of ordinary votes cast before and on election day. Special votes are not included in these results.
It takes 20 days to complete the official count and release the official results. Ordinary votes are counted a second time and special votes, including overseas votes, are checked and counted.
The election night results can change once the special votes are counted and included in the official results.
The official election results can be challenged for a limited time after a general election through applications to the courts for an electorate recount or election petition.
Election recounts and petitions (elections.nz) [Election recounts and petitions -> https://elections.nz/democracy-in-nz/about-elections/election-recounts-and-petitions/]
During the official count, we compare all the rolls from voting places and compile a master roll to make sure everyone has only voted once.
We make the master roll for each electorate available for public inspection after the election. It can only be viewed by a person who is enrolled in the electorate.
The master roll shows who has voted, but not who they voted for. Who you vote for is secret to you.
The process of compiling the master roll is called 'scrutinising the rolls', and it identifies voters who may have voted more than once.
Voting papers are kept for six months after an election in case they are needed for a legal challenge. They are held in secure storage, in sealed boxes, and no one has access to them. Secrecy provisions mean that requests cannot be made to see a person’s voting papers.
After six months, voting papers are destroyed securely. The papers are shredded and prepared for recycling.

## The Electoral Commission’s role
We’re responsible for running New Zealand’s parliamentary elections - general elections, by-elections and referendums - and for keeping the electoral rolls up to date.
We work to provide an effective and impartial electoral system that New Zealanders understand and trust.
We advise political parties, candidates, and third parties on how to comply with electoral rules.
Our role does not include regulating the content of campaign advertising, broadcasts or media activity.
We can't enforce laws or prosecute offenders. If we believe a person or group has committed an offence under the Electoral Act, we'll report the facts to the Police.
