# Social media
During an election there are rules about what you can and can’t do on social media. Here’s how you can follow the rules – and what you can do if something doesn’t seem right.
### What to do if something doesn't look right
If you see something on social media that doesn’t look right, there may be some things you can do.
If someone is trying to influence your vote, they should be telling you who they are.
Candidates, parties and third-party promoters are required to include a promoter statement in any election and referendum advertising – or, for social media posts they haven’t paid to boost, on the About or Profile page of their social media account. A promoter statement must include the name and address of the person responsible for the advertising.
If you don’t know who’s behind a post you see on social media, then you don’t know whether you can trust it.
If you see something you think is misinformation or disinformation, don’t share it, even if your intent is to correct it or to highlight that it’s not true. This is because by sharing misinformation, you could be helping it spread. A better approach is to post or share the correct information instead.
If something doesn’t seem right, you should take steps to check the source of that information and make sure it’s legitimate.
If it’s a statement by a candidate or party, check that it’s from their real social media account, or their real website.
The Electoral Commission is the authoritative source for information about when, where and how to vote, and about how New Zealand’s parliamentary elections work. You can always find the correct information on our website, or if you’re unsure, contact us.
Contact us [Contact us -> https://elections.nz/contact]
The Electoral Commission doesn’t regulate the content of election advertising, including the content of social media posts.
However, some social media posts may violate the terms and conditions or community standards of the social media platform, and can be reported to the platform on that basis.
Some examples include:
Meta Community Standards for Facebook, Instagram, Messenger and Threads [Meta Community Standards for Facebook, I -> https://www.facebook.com/communitystandards/]
The X Rules [The X Rules -> https://help.twitter.com/en/rules-and-policies/twitter-rules]
TikTok community guidelines [TikTok community guidelines -> https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines?lang=en]
In addition, the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) will review complaints about paid advertising like boosted or sponsored posts on social media. They won't consider complaints about unpaid or organic posts.
Advertising Standards Authority [Advertising Standards Authority -> https://www.asa.co.nz/]
Finally, if you see election or referendum advertising that you think is missing a promoter statement, or if you see some misleading information about when, where, or how to vote, you should contact the Electoral Commission.
### Using social media on election day
On election day additional restrictions apply when you’re posting on social media.
From midnight before election day until 7pm on election day, you can’t post or share any content that’s likely to - or is intended to - influence someone’s candidate vote or party vote.
Paid online election advertising is prohibited on election day.
The election day rules make no exemption for the expression of personal political views online.
Election advertising does not have to be removed from social media as long as:
- it was published before election day
- it’s only made available to people who voluntarily access it, and
- no advertisements promoting the page or site are published on election day
- you don’t repost the material on election day, even if the information was posted before election day.
On election day you can remind people to get out and vote, and even tell them that you’ve just voted – but you should take care not to post anything that encourages other voters to vote (or not to vote) for a particular candidate or party.
We recommend you don’t use profile pictures, filters, or frames that support a candidate or party, to avoid the risk of committing an offence if you post using the picture or frame on election day. Photos, filters and frames saying you’ve voted or which encourage others to vote are fine.
Posting a photograph of a completed voting paper online on election day could breach the election day rules, because the Electoral Act prohibits publishing a statement on election day that is likely to influence how another voter should vote.
