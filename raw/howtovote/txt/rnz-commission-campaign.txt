# Electoral Commission campaign looks to educate voters after enrolment changes
The Electoral Commission is hoping staff training and resources will help prevent potential disenfranchisement after enrolment changes brought in by the government.
The non-partisan, independent Commission launched its advertising campaign on Monday, including a new TV advert featuring the return of the 'Orange guy' and 'Orange pup' CGI mascots superimposed on real-world scenes.
CEO and chief electoral officer Karl le Quesne said that was different to previous campaigns, and "that's really just to grab people's attention".
"There'll be other more tailored and targeted messages, we'll be doing this on radio, television, social media, streaming platforms, digital billboards, every way we can," he said.
"Everyone can play a role in enrolment whether you're a parent, a grandparent, an uncle, an auntie, a sports coach - particularly for younger people, you can all check in with those young people."
The law has changed and if you're not enrolled by 25 October, your vote won't be counted, Karl le Quesne says.
RNZ / Russell Palmer
Board chair Simon Moore said the main message was that New Zealanders needed to get enrolled before the cutoff date of 25 October - one of a series of "significant changes" under the new enrolment rules . [new enrolment rules -> https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/politics/568340/voting-reforms-prisoner-bans-and-enrolment-changes-what-you-need-to-know-about-the-electoral-amendment-bill]
"Right now we've got 3.6 million people enrolled, so that's round about 89 percent of eligible voters. Not bad, but we don't think it's good enough," Moore said.
He said of the roughly 440,000 people unenrolled, about 200,000 or 45 percent were under age 25, "so that's the cohort that we really need to be getting the attention of".
Le Quesne also highlighted the youth enrolment rate, saying reaching 18 year olds "where young people are out in the communities" was "the key message".
"The other really important message, and I keep repeating this every time I possibly can, be enrolled by 25 October if you want to vote. Our second message is, do it by 4 October if you can, because that means you make it on the printed roll and it'll go a bit easier in the voting place for you."
The Electoral Commission's TV campaign features the return of the 'Orange guy' and 'Orange pup' CGI mascots superimposed on real-world scenes.
He also defended the Commission's plan not to record the number of people turned away from voting places due to not being enrolled as reported by Newsroom last week. [Newsroom -> https://newsroom.co.nz/2026/08/03/tens-of-thousands-could-be-turned-away-from-voting-but-no-one-is-counting-them/]
"When we had a look at that question, it became clear that it's quite difficult. There's quite a lot you would need to work through to be clear about what you were recording so our judgement is, the best way to get a handle on that is our post-election survey," he said.
"The law has changed and if you're not enrolled by 25 October, your vote won't be counted.
"If there's any doubt at all we will offer a special vote, but I actually think a lot of people when they realise 'I'm not enrolled, my vote won't be counted', they'll decide 'okay, well, I'm not going to vote this time'. But we'll make sure people get information around what they need to be do to be enrolled for next time."
Electoral Commission board chair Simon Moore.
Asked if he was concerned that approach could turn people off voting - including in the target youth vote age group - he said: "that's why we'll be putting a lot of emphasis on our staff and their training and some resources and tools that they'll have to work through those questions".
"We're always concerned around youth enrolment and turning out to vote ... there has been a law change this year. We have to follow the law, but we're doing a lot to get that message out to people and give them that opportunity."
"The government's been clear that they ... want the process to be sped up as quickly as possible. Part of that involves reducing the number of special votes because that's what takes so long to count."
He denied feeling pressured by the government to turn people away and speed up the process, saying it was "not part of any discussions I've had".
Le Quesne said the Commission "can't put our finger on one particular thing" causing the steady decline in youth enrolment rates over recent years, but noted the rate did tend to increase as the election date neared. [steady decline in youth enrolment rates -> https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/politics/868515/things-are-screwed-anyway-why-try-youth-voter-enrolment-rate-plummets]
"One of the things we're going to be doing from next year is automatic enrolment updates, and that's where we use data matching with other government agencies to identify people who aren't enrolled and to contact them, and again get that message around enrolling."
