NZ First Announces Immigration Reform Policies

Friday, 11 September 2026, 7:15 am
Press Release: New Zealand First Party

New Zealand First has today announced a number of campaign policies that will make sweeping changes to current immigration laws.

New Zealand's immigration system must serve New Zealand and New Zealanders.

For too long, governments have relied on immigration to drive headline economic growth and cheap labour. The result has been greater pressure on housing, infrastructure and public services, increased competition for entry-level jobs, and declining public confidence that immigration is being managed in the national interest.

Population growth must not outpace our ability to build the country.

New Zealand First will substantially reduce non-essential immigration and restore the principle that immigration is a privilege, not a right.

New Zealand is more than an economy. It is a nation with its own history, institutions and way of life.

Immigration policy should strengthen social cohesion and encourage integration into a shared national community.

The foundation of any proper immigration system in our country should be the value placed on New Zealand citizenship.

New Zealand needs to start viewing our citizenship as most other countries around the world do - it is a privilege that comes with special and unique rights and identity.

We need to understand the importance of a country’s citizenship and the reasons why we must protect and enhance what it means to be a New Zealander.

This does not take away from the ability for migrants looking to move here, contribute, and help build our country from showing total commitment and patriotism for New Zealand by becoming a citizen.

Even the most basic of privileges and rights in any country like voting has become more like an expectation for all, rather than a unique right for New Zealanders.

In addition to the already announced policies of citizen’s only voting and citizen’s only NZ Super, New Zealand First will introduce the following policies in the next term of government:

· Birthright Citizenship – New Zealand First will update birthright citizenship requirements, so that only the children of citizens will be automatically entitled to New Zealand citizenship at birth.

· Asylum Seeker Changes - New Zealand First will reform the asylum seeker system, updating current settings so that they are fair, consistent, and fit for purpose. Protected person status will become temporary by design, subject to ongoing assessment.

o Any person who seeks asylum while in New Zealand on a working-class visa will be ineligible to do so.

o Asylum claimants will not be able to use the temporary work rights granted during the assessment period as a stepping stone to another visa.

· Permanent Resident and Citizenship Qualifying Period Adjustments - New Zealand First will double the required period to qualify for citizenship from five years to a minimum of ten years. The qualifying period for permanent residence will be extended from two to five years.

· Citizenship and Permanent Residence Application Fees – New Zealand First will increase the cost of applying for New Zealand citizenship and permanent residence to reflect its value and to better fund the wider immigration system.

· Welfare Stand-Down Period - New Zealand First will implement a 10-year welfare stand-down period for all visa holders, meaning non-citizens will be ineligible for jobseeker, accommodation supplement, or any other benefits during their first 10 years in New Zealand.

· Broadened Ability to Deport - New Zealand First will widen the criteria for deportation liability to cover any offence for which imprisonment is available as a sentence - with automatic deportation for those found guilty of violent or sexual crimes, found guilty of fraud, participating in an organised criminal group, money laundering, aiding and abetting under the Immigration Act, or migrant exploitation.

· Resident Visa Cap Referendum - New Zealand First will hold a referendum on the introduction of a legislated annual cap on the number of resident visas issued each year. Included in the Resident Visa Cap Referendum will be:

o A resident visa cap of 20,000 per year.

o Linking immigration settings directly to infrastructure capacity.

o Requiring major population growth to occur only where infrastructure already exists or is fully funded and scheduled for delivery.

o Stopping councils being forced to adapt to central government planning where local infrastructure cannot support it.

o Development of a 30-year Population Plan to coordinate immigration, housing and infrastructure planning.

· Social Housing for Citizens First – New Zealand First will ensure that social housing will be made available to citizens first and foremost.

· Chronic Overstayers – New Zealand First will implement a 24-month amnesty for overstayers to self-deport with no restrictions on applications to return.

o Any overstayer found past the clemency period has lapsed will be deported and permanently ineligible to apply for any visa in the future.

o In recognition of our commitment to our enduring Pacific relations, we will allow for a 24-month clemency period to facilitate any Pacific overstayer to apply to remain in the country legally on a valid visa. This will apply only to overstayers of Pacific origin.

· Student Visa Holders – New Zealand First will ensure student visa holders intending to transition to another visa will be required to leave before reapplying for another visa from abroad.

· Removal of Automatic Work Rights for Student Visa Holders – New Zealand First will remove the automatic work rights for international students and stop our kiwi jobs being taken by foreign students.

o Export education was designed to allow foreign students to come here, their economy pay for their education, and go back. It was not designed to allow them to take jobs off kiwis, have our economy pay for their education, and then to use the visa to move on to residence or work.

o It will be mandatory for any international student who wants to work in New Zealand while they study to apply under strict conditions to work. First and foremost, those job opportunities cannot be taken from New Zealanders looking for work.

· Immigration Ministerial Portfolio - It is imperative that a New Zealand First Minister holds the Immigration portfolio when part of the next coalition government to ensure the oversight, control and reform of the immigration system.

New Zealanders have the right to determine the future size and character of their country.

This set of polices is about taking back control of the future of our country and valuing our unique citizenship the way it should be - just like the vast majority of other countries do around the world.

Notes:

1: Citizens Only Voting (ANNOUNCED 5/07/2026)

Only New Zealand Citizens should vote in our elections.

Current law allows permanent residents to vote in New Zealand’s elections[1].This makes New Zealand one of the few countries that extends this right to non-citizens.

Voting is the most fundamental expression of democratic participation. Extending that right to non-citizens dilutes what it means to be a New Zealander and removes a meaningful incentive to apply for citizenship.

New Zealand is an outlier among comparable nations. The vast majority of countries prohibit non-citizens from voting in national elections. That's the global default (Australia included).

Very few countries extend equal voting rights to non-citizens in national elections. Under current settings, permanent residents become eligible to vote after just one year of residence, lower than any comparable democratic peer.

This setting change would simply align our rules with the overwhelming international norm.

2: Superannuation for Citizens only (ANNOUNCED 6/9/2026)

We will ensure that only citizens will be eligible to receive Superannuation.

New Zealand Superannuation is a commitment to Kiwis that have spent their lives working and contributing to this country. Our population is ageing and the cost of the scheme is increasing. However, we believe it should remain universal for all New Zealander citizens.

However, current settings allow for non-citizens to receive Superannuation:

“You may qualify for NZ Super if you: are 65 or older, and either: are a New Zealand (NZ) citizen, are a NZ permanent resident, or hold an NZ residence class visa, are ordinarily resident in New Zealand, the Cook Islands, Niue or Tokelau when you apply, and have lived in New Zealand for a certain amount of time.”[2]

A non-citizen who arrives later in life and obtains permanent residence has the same entitlements as a citizen who has spent decades paying their share of taxes. We don’t think this is a fair or reasonable arrangement.

The cost of this decision is growing. In 2025, the government spend on superannuation for new migrants hit a billion dollars a year - roughly $20 million NZD per week[3].

Tightening eligibility to citizens only will reduce long-term pressure on the scheme, protect it for future generations for citizens, and provide a further incentive for permanent residents to pursue citizenship. Those who wish to retire here and access superannuation within the rules are welcome to do so - as citizens.

All non-citizens that currently receive New Zealand superannuation will not be impacted by this change – a 24-month grace period will apply, so that those seeking citizenship have ample time to do so prior to this change taking effect.

This adjustment will not infringe upon the rights of citizens of countries with which we have social security agreements already in place (also known as SSAs).

3: Birthright Citizenship

We will update birthright citizenship requirements, so that only the children of citizens will be automatically entitled to New Zealand citizenship at birth.

Under our current rules, newborns qualify as a citizen if, at the time of birth, at least one parent met one of these conditions (from govt.nz)[4]:

You are only a citizen if, when you were born, at least 1 of your parents was:

a New Zealand citizen, or

had a visa that allowed them to reside in New Zealand indefinitely.

For context, the Citizenship Amendment Act 2005 had previously removed unrestricted birthright citizenship, by requiring that the at least one parent be a New Zealand citizen or permanent resident[5].

We believe that only the children of citizens should automatically receive citizenship at birth.

This further change is necessary because of the relative ease by which current permanent residence is attained. After just two years, migrants in some roles are able to apply for permanent residence. Children born after this point currently qualify for New Zealand citizenship automatically, even when their parents are not citizens.

The impact of this has been profound. The proportion of children born to permanent residents has rapidly grown within recent years. In combination with our previous policies, this would provide another incentive to apply for full citizenship. This would align our settings with other countries like Austria, Denmark, Japan and China, among (many) others.

4: Asylum Seeker Changes

New Zealand First will reform the asylum seeker system, updating current settings so that they are fair, consistent, and fit for purpose. Protected person status will become temporary by design, subject to ongoing assessment.

Over the past several decades, globalisation and mass migration have reshaped the immigration landscape in ways that our current approach was never designed to accommodate.

Asylum claims have continued to rise in recent times, reaching a record 2,345 in 2023. Meanwhile, the system's ability to process them has deteriorated. In 2019, 68% of claims reached a decision within the year. By 2023, that figure had fallen to 30%. Claims remained elevated at 2,270 in 2024/25. Of those who withdraw their claims, a third do so at the same time as lodging an application for a different New Zealand visa[6]. Current settings provide insufficient tools to distinguish genuine protection needs from those using the process as an alternative immigration pathway.

Currently, anyone can make an asylum claim. Each undergoes assessment, and where it succeeds, the claimant is granted refugee or protected person status. Refugees and protected persons can apply for permanent residence following recognition by a Refugee and Protection Officer or the New Zealand citizenship after 5 years of residence. They are meanwhile eligible to access employment, income support, and public health services, the same as New Zealand residents or citizens[7].

New Zealand First believes protected persons and refugees should instead be granted a renewable 24-month residence permit, temporary by design, with extensions considered individually according to each claimant's ongoing circumstances.

Where the genuine need for safe harbour has lapsed, because conditions in the country of origin have improved, that refugee status will end, and the individual will be returned home when conditions allow.

This model draws on the approach adopted by Denmark. Refugee status in Denmark is temporary and subject to review every 30 months, with settlement only available after an extended qualifying period.[8] Denmark reduced its asylum claims by more than 90% over a decade using this framework.

Any person who seeks asylum while in New Zealand on a working-class visa will be ineligible to do so. We do not consider these claims legitimate and will not accept them for asylum consideration.

New Zealand First will also legislate to prevent asylum claimants from using the temporary work rights granted during the assessment period as a stepping stone to other visa classes. Under current settings, individuals who lodge a claim are granted temporary work rights for the duration of the assessment process, a period that sometimes can last up to two and a half years[9]. This is exploitative and not in the spirit of the asylum seeker system.

Asylum claims are often used as a mechanism in exploitation; the visa holder is provided a statement to sign to lodge a claim and then become obligated to the facilitator of the claim process. It effectively creates a servitude relationship because the claimant is party to a fraudulent claim. As noted, it gives them up to 2.5 years of working rights while the claim is processed.

5: Permanent Resident and Citizenship Qualifying Period Adjustments

New Zealand First will double the required period to qualify for citizenship from five years to a minimum of ten years.

The qualifying periods for permanent residence will be extended from two to five years. We will also extend the qualifying period for New Zealand Citizenship from five to ten years.

Current rules allow for applications for Permanent Residence after holding a Resident Visa for just two years (with a minimum of 184 days in-country per year)[10]. The current pathway to full citizenship requires five years on any resident class visa.

In practice, the entire journey from arriving in New Zealand to holding a New Zealand passport can take just five years. Our current pathways are comparatively short when compared to similar nations.

How we compare:

United Kingdom: The standard path to Indefinite Leave to Remain (UK's equivalent of permanent residence) takes five years, followed by a further year before citizenship eligibility - six years total[11]

Japan: Recently updated the standard citizenship qualifying period to 10 years (previously 5)[12]. This brings it into line with the pathway to permanent residence, which is also 10 years.

Denmark: Permanent residence is available after eight years of continuous lawful residence on a temporary permit, with a four-year accelerated pathway available pending additional requirements[13]. Normally, for acquiring citizenship, it is a condition that you have been a resident continuously for 9 years[14].

Austria: Citizenship requires ten years of legal and uninterrupted residence, including five years as a resident[15]

Australia: Citizenship requires four years of residence (including one year as a permanent resident)[16]

Canada: Citizenship requires three years of physical presence within a five-year window, after obtaining permanent residence[17]

Italy, Spain: Require five years of continuous residence for permanent residence[18]. Also require ten years for naturalisation[19][20]

Portugal: Recently raised its residency requirements for citizenship from 5 to 10 years for non EU citizens[21].

Switzerland: Residents become eligible for permanent residence after 10 years of lawful residence in Switzerland[22].

Extending the qualifying period for permanent residence from two to five years, and period thereafter for citizenship from five to ten years ensures that newcomers are awarded on the basis of sustained, demonstrated commitment to our country.

6: Citizenship and Permanent Residence Application Fees

The cost of applying for New Zealand citizenship and permanent residence will be increased to reflect its value and to better fund the wider immigration system.

New Zealand citizenship by grant currently costs NZD $641 for adults[23]. This remains low by international standards, and recent increases were primarily driven by the need to cover processing costs.

Citizenship is the permanent commitment to New Zealand, entitling the holder the right to vote, to hold a New Zealand passport with all its privileges, and to pass that citizenship onto children. Under our policy, meeting specific time demands, would also entitle you to superannuation. The cost structure should reflect that.

Revenue generated through higher application fees can be directed toward processing backlogs, enforcement, and other crucial parts of the immigration system, reducing the burden on general taxpayers. This will include properly resourcing the asylum claims system, ensuring that claims and appeals are dealt with more quickly.

For these reasons, we propose increasing the cost of citizenship by grant to $950 to adequately reflect the value offered by being a New Zealand citizen. We propose a similar increase to Permanent Residence Class Visas (currently at $315[27], will be raised to $630).

A twenty-four-month grace period will apply prior to the new citizen application fee structure taking effect, providing ample opportunity for those already here and planning on applying for citizenship by grant to do so.

7: 10-year Welfare Stand Down Period for Visa Holders:

New Zealand First will implement a 10-year welfare stand-down period for all visa holders, meaning non-citizens will be ineligible for jobseeker, accommodation supplement, or any other benefits during their first 10 years in New Zealand. We believe that new residents should actually contribute to our country before being eligible for taxpayer support.

This is about attracting migrants who we need, not who need us.

New Zealand taxpayers should be protected from government decisions that have effectively imported poverty through settings that result in dependents and spouses being reliant on the main visa holder where salary settings are not sufficient to support dependents.

New Zealand would not be alone in applying this principle. Australia imposes a four-year waiting period on most newly arrived permanent residents before they can access certain welfare payments[28]. The United Kingdom also applies a habitual residence test before migrants can access income-related benefits.

8: Broadened Ability to Deport

We will widen the criteria for deportation liability to cover any offence for which imprisonment is available as a sentence - with automatic deportation for those found guilty of violent or sexual crimes, or found guilty of fraud, participating in an organised criminal group, money laundering, aiding and abetting under the Immigration Act or migrant exploitation.

Under current settings, deportation is only activated at specific sentencing thresholds. Section 161 of the Immigration Act 2009 establishes a tiered framework for determining when holders of resident and permanent resident visas who have committed criminal offences become liable for deportation. Each tier weighs two factors: how long the person has held their residence class visa, and how serious the offence is[29] [30].

This means a non-citizen can be convicted of a criminal offence and potentially face no immigration consequences whatsoever, simply because their sentence falls below an arbitrary line. This is not an outcome that serves New Zealanders economically or socially.

If a court has determined that an offence is serious enough to warrant the possibility of imprisonment (including rape and sexual offending, serious violent crime, migrant exploitation, domestic violence, and offences involving weapons or organised criminal association) that should be sufficient to instantly trigger deportation liability for any non-citizen post-prison term, regardless of the period that they have held a visa.

We will also amend the law so that any non-citizen specifically found guilty of committing a sexual or violent crime or found guilty of fraud or migrant exploitation will have no avenue to appeal their deportation to the Immigration and Protection Tribunal on humanitarian grounds. This is currently an avenue available to migrants to appeal deportation decisions[31].

(N.B: This differentiates NZF policy from the ongoing Immigration (Enhanced Risk Management) Amendment Bill, which has passed its first reading and is at select committee[32]. The Amendment Bill extends the period of residence during which a person can be subject to liability for deportation from ten to twenty years[33]. We will remove any cap on deportation liability for serious crimes, and also extend the inability to appeal to Immigration and Protection Tribunal on humanitarian grounds to permanent residence holders, rather than just temporary visas[34]

9: Resident Visa Cap Referendum

New Zealand First will hold a referendum on the introduction of a legislated annual cap on the number of resident visas issued each year.

This policy is about one thing: the gap between how fast New Zealand is growing and how fast it is able to build and maintain the infrastructure, housing, public services, and jobs that growth demands. That gap is not sustainable, and it is not acceptable to continue widening it in the absence of any deliberate plan to manage that growth sustainably.

One of the largest recent drivers was a deliberate policy decision, not an organic trend. The previous Labour Government's 2021 Resident Visa scheme resulted in 212,649 new residence permits being granted.[35] At its peak, more than 10,000 residence visas per month were being approved[36]. Our public services were not designed to absorb that pace of growth. There should be checks in place to prevent something like this from occurring again under future governments.

The consequences are visible to every New Zealander: hospitals overwhelmed, housing unaffordable, doctors waiting lists growing, and infrastructure investment perpetually chasing a population that keeps outpacing it. Compounding this, Immigration New Zealand recorded 72,700 New Zealand citizen departures in the year ending September 2025, (and, more recently, 63,900 in the year ended June 2026). New Zealand is simultaneously importing people at an unprecedented rate and losing our own citizens at a record pace. That is not a sustainable or coherent population policy.

Australia has operated a similar model for decades - an annual cap, currently 185,000 places, split 70/30 between skilled migrants and family and humanitarian entrants[37]. Australia adjusts this figure annually against labour market conditions, housing capacity, and settlement infrastructure. It is a deliberate, managed system. We should do the same.

The United States maintains a cap on permanent residence, presently set at 675,000 visas per year, with employment and family categories set by Congress. Canada publishes an annual Immigration Levels Plan, calibrated against housing supply, healthcare capacity, and labour market need - and has recently reduced its targets in direct response to housing pressure.

What these countries share is a recognition that population growth driven by migration is a policy lever that must be carefully managed.

A resident visa cap of 20,000 per year represents a sustainable and manageable level, allowing New Zealand to continue attracting skilled migrants, meet its humanitarian obligations, while also giving public services and labour market the breathing room needed to recover.

The management of New Zealand's population should be a subject of deliberate public choice. The social consequences of unmanaged mass migration are visible in other developed nations. We have the opportunity and an obligation to future generations to do better.

Included in the Resident Visa Cap Referendum will be:

Linking immigration settings directly to infrastructure capacity.

Requiring major population growth to occur only where infrastructure already exists or is fully funded and scheduled for delivery.

Stopping councils being forced to adapt to central government planning where local infrastructure cannot support it.

Development of a 30-year Population Plan to coordinate immigration, housing and infrastructure planning.

Population growth must not outpace our ability to build the country.

For decades, governments have encouraged mass immigration without ensuring that the roads, water infrastructure, schools, hospitals and housing needed to support population growth were in place.

10: Social Housing for Citizens First

We believe that social housing should be made available to citizens first and foremost.

Demand for Social housing is under extreme pressure. As of June 2026, there were 19,002 applicants on the Housing Register[38]. Under current settings, social housing eligibility extends equally to citizens and permanent residents[39]. New Zealand First believes this requires adjustment. Citizenship represents the fullest expression of commitment to this country, and those who have made that commitment should be prioritised when it comes to state resources that are in short supply.

Those who have made the fullest commitment to this country and have gone through the naturalisation process and taken on the rights and responsibilities of citizenship, should be prioritised when state resources are being allocated.

For this reason, we will direct MSD to implement a prioritisation framework that places citizens at the front of the queue ahead of permanent residents when allocating social housing placements.

11: Chronic Overstayers

Immigration New Zealand estimates that 20,980 people are currently in New Zealand having unlawfully overstayed their visas[40]. This places additional pressure on public services and undermines the integrity of an immigration system that the vast majority navigate lawfully and in good faith.

A large portion of illegal overstayers originate from neighbouring Pacific island nations. It goes without saying that we share a unique and close bond with these countries. In recognition of our commitment to our enduring Pacific relations, we will allow for a 24-month clemency period to facilitate any Pacific overstayer to apply to remain in the country legally on a valid visa. This will apply only to overstayers of Pacific origin.

Any overstayer found past the clemency period has lapsed will be deported. These deported overstayers will be permanently ineligible to apply for any visa in the future, due to their heightened risk of reoffending.

We will also invest a further $50 million into frontline immigration compliance resourcing. This will ensure that Immigration NZ has the tools and the staff needed to cut into the growing number of chronic overstayers.

New Zealand First recognises that overstayers live in a vulnerable state with a higher likelihood of being victims of exploitation, trafficking, and forced labour. Investment in investigative resource and enforcement will provide opportunity for intervention and protections.

12: Student Visa Holders Must Reapply for Other Visas from Offshore

Student visa holders intending to transition to another visa will be required to leave before reapplying for another visa from abroad.

From the over 90,000 student visa holders enrolled in 2025, approximately 32,000 will move to a resident or work visa.

The student visa exists for a specific purpose: to allow foreigners to study. It does not (and should not) function as a backdoor to long-term residence. Requiring applicants to reapply from offshore closes this potential loophole whereby student visas are used as a staging ground for extended stays that was never the original intent of the education visa grant. For those who are genuinely committed to a long-term future here, departing to reapply presents no real issue.

The requirement applies only to those seeking to change their visa status, not to students completing their studies and departing as intended. This change protects the integrity of the student visa category.

13. Removal of Automatic Work Rights for Student Visa Holders

Export education was designed to allow foreign students to come here, pay for their education, and go back. It was not designed to allow them to take jobs off kiwis, and then to use the visa to move on to residence or work.

New Zealand’s current Youth Unemployment rate is approximately 22% and Youth Under-utilisation rate is approximately 37%.

That means approximately 70,000 15–24-year-old New Zealanders who are unemployed and actively looking for work.
