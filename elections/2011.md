## At a glance

- **Winner:** National (John Key) – 47.31% of the party vote and 59 of 121 seats, the best National result under MMP up to that point,
  but two seats short of a majority on its own.
- **Government:** a second-term **National minority government** with confidence-and-supply agreements with ACT (1 seat),
  United Future (1) and the Māori Party (3) – 64 of 121 seats. New ministry appointed 14 December 2011.
- **Opposition:** Labour (Phil Goff) fell to 27.48% and 34 seats; Goff resigned and David Shearer became leader in December 2011.
- **Big movers:** the Greens reached 11.06% (14 seats), their best result to that date; NZ First returned to Parliament with 6.59%
  (8 seats) after missing out in 2008; ACT collapsed to 1.07% and survived only through John Banks winning Epsom.
- **Referendum:** held alongside the election, 57.8% voted to **keep MMP**.
- **Turnout:** 74.21% of enrolled electors, which Wikipedia describes as the lowest turnout since 1887.

## Context

National had governed since November 2008 as a minority government (58 of 122 seats) supported by ACT, United Future and the Māori
Party. The 49th Parliament covered the aftermath of the global financial crisis and the Canterbury earthquakes of 2010–11, and the
election date was fixed – unusually early, in February 2011 – for 26 November, after New Zealand hosted the Rugby World Cup
(9 September – 23 October 2011), which ruled out dates in that window.

Key's decision to announce the date early broke with the tradition of keeping it secret as long as possible. The writ return was
later delayed from 15 to 17 December because the judicial recount in Waitakere was not finished in time.

## Campaign and issues

- **Partial asset sales ("mixed ownership").** National campaigned on selling minority stakes in state-owned energy companies and
  reducing the Crown's share of Air New Zealand. After the election Key rejected claims the government lacked a mandate for the
  policy. Opponents later forced the 2013 citizens-initiated referendum on the sales (held by postal ballot, Nov–Dec 2013), which the
  government said it would ignore because the 2011 election had given it a mandate.
- **Capital gains tax.** Labour went into 2011 (and again 2014) with a capital gains tax policy – a point noted in the 2017 election
  article when Labour finally dropped the idea for its first term. Phil Goff's Wikipedia biography records an earlier leaked capital
  gains tax proposal that critics saw as unpopular.
- **The Epsom "tea tape".** On 11 November Key met ACT's Epsom candidate John Banks for a cup of tea in a Newmarket café to signal to
  Epsom voters that they should give their electorate vote to ACT and their party vote to National – a deal that lets a small party
  bypass the 5% threshold by winning one electorate. A recording of the conversation, made by a device left on the table, was passed
  to the *Herald on Sunday* and dominated the last fortnight of the campaign. Banks won Epsom by 2,261 votes over National's Paul
  Goldsmith.
- **Coalition signals.** National ruled out working with Winston Peters; ACT committed to National; Goff ruled out a coalition with
  Hone Harawira's new Mana Party but left the door open to NZ First; the Greens preferred Labour but did not rule out National;
  United Future ruled out Labour; the Māori Party would not work in government with ACT.
- **Media coverage.** A Massey University study (released Nov 2012) found the four big newspapers printed 72% more photos of Key than
  of Goff in the last month and gave him twice as many column inches.

Main party pitches in one line each (a summary; detailed 2011 manifesto texts were not re-checked against primary sources for this archive): **National** – economic management,
returning to surplus and the mixed-ownership programme; **Labour** – opposing asset sales and a capital gains tax excluding the
family home; **Greens** – jobs, child poverty and clean rivers; **NZ First** – opposing asset sales and protecting superannuation;
**ACT** (under Don Brash) – smaller government; **Mana** – a new kaupapa Māori/left party formed by Hone Harawira after he left the
Māori Party.

### What the numbers meant

- **National's high-water mark.** National's 47.31% was close to a majority of votes cast for parties that won seats, but the
  Sainte-Laguë allocation still left it at 59 seats – two short of 61 in a 121-seat House – so it again depended on small partners.
- **The support parties shrank.** ACT went from 5 seats to 1, the Māori Party from 5 to 3 (losing Te Tai Tonga to Labour's Rino
  Tirikatene), and United Future held only Peter Dunne's Ōhāriu seat. Together they still gave the government 64 votes.
- **Overhang.** The Māori Party's three electorates exceeded its party-vote entitlement, creating one overhang seat (121 MPs).
- **Wasted vote.** 3.37% of valid party votes went to parties that won no seats – the lowest of the six elections in this archive,
  mainly because the Conservatives (2.65%) were the only sizeable party left out.
- **Labour's low.** 27.48% was Labour's worst result in decades at the time (it fell further in 2014). Goff and deputy Annette King
  resigned on 29 November; David Shearer beat David Cunliffe for the leadership.
- **ACT leadership.** Don Brash resigned as ACT leader on election night, taking responsibility for the result.

## Electorates

70 electorates (63 general, 7 Māori). Notable contests:

- **Waitakere** – National's Paula Bennett held on by just 9 votes over Labour's Carmel Sepuloni after a judicial recount (the delay
  pushed back the return of the writ).
- **Christchurch Central** – National's Nicky Wagner took the seat from Labour's Brendon Burns by 47 votes – the first time National
  had won the seat; part of a Canterbury swing in a year dominated by the earthquake recovery.
- **Epsom** – ACT's John Banks won with National's tacit support (see above).
- **Te Tai Tokerau** – Hone Harawira kept the seat for his new Mana Party (majority 1,165 over Labour's Kelvin Davis), having won it
  at a June 2011 by-election after leaving the Māori Party.
- **West Coast-Tasman** – Labour's Damien O'Connor regained the seat from National.
- **Wigram** – Labour's Megan Woods succeeded the retiring Jim Anderton (Progressive), ending the Progressive Party's presence in
  Parliament.

## Government formation

National renewed its confidence-and-supply arrangements with ACT, United Future and the Māori Party in December 2011. Governor-General Sir Jerry Mateparae appointed the new ministry on 14 December 2011, with 24 National ministers and four
ministers from support parties. The arrangement – a large party governing in minority with one-seat partners – was the high point of
"electorate-deal" politics in New Zealand: ACT and United Future were in Parliament only because they won Epsom and Ōhāriu.

## The 50th Parliament (2011–2014)

Notable changes: NZ First expelled list MP Brendan Horan from its caucus in December 2012 (he sat as an independent and later
founded the NZ Independent Coalition); United Future was briefly deregistered in mid-2013, leaving Peter Dunne technically an
independent; by-elections in Ikaroa-Rāwhiti (Meka Whaitiri, Labour, June 2013) and Christchurch East (Poto Williams, Labour,
November 2013); and ACT's John Banks resigned in June 2014 after being convicted of filing a false electoral return (the conviction was
later overturned), leaving ACT without an MP until the election.

## What changed

1. MMP was confirmed by referendum, triggering the Electoral Commission's 2012 MMP review (which recommended a 4% threshold and
   abolishing the one-electorate "coat-tail" rule – recommendations Parliament did not adopt; see [2014](2014.md)).
2. The Greens established themselves as the clear third party (11%), and NZ First showed it could come back from outside Parliament.
3. The election entrenched the pattern of National relying on small electorate-based partners, and of Labour struggling with
   leadership changes (Goff → Shearer → Cunliffe → Little before 2017).
