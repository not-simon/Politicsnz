# 2017 New Zealand general election (23 September 2017)

## At a glance

- **Largest party:** National (Bill English) – 44.45%, 56 of 120 seats, but no majority even with ACT (57).
- **Government:** **Labour–NZ First coalition** with **Green confidence and supply** (46 + 9 + 8 = 63 of 120). NZ First announced its
  choice on 19 October 2017; the government was sworn in on 26 October with **Jacinda Ardern** as Prime Minister and Winston Peters as
  Deputy PM. It was the first time under MMP that a party led a government without winning the most party votes.
- **Labour's surge:** 36.89% (+11.76 pp) and 46 seats after Ardern replaced Andrew Little as leader just seven weeks before polling day.
- **Fewest parties under MMP:** only five parties entered Parliament (National, Labour, NZ First, Greens, ACT). The Māori Party
  (1.18%) lost all its seats, and United Future did not return after Peter Dunne retired.
- **TOP** (Gareth Morgan's Opportunities Party, founded 2016) won 2.44% but no seats.
- **Turnout:** 79.75%.

## Context

National was seeking a fourth term – no government had won four terms since 1972 and none had under MMP. John Key resigned
unexpectedly in December 2016 and Bill English, his finance minister, became Prime Minister. Labour began 2017 polling in the
mid-20s under Andrew Little; on **1 August 2017** Little resigned and deputy leader Jacinda Ardern was elected unopposed, with Kelvin
Davis as deputy. Labour reported $250,000 in donations and 1,000 new volunteers within 24 hours.

The Greens had a turbulent campaign: co-leader **Metiria Turei** disclosed in July that she had not told Work and Income about
flatmates' rent while on a benefit in the 1990s, and later that she had enrolled at a false address in 1993. Two Green MPs (David
Clendon, Kennedy Graham) quit the caucus and Turei resigned as co-leader on 9 August, leaving James Shaw as sole leader.

## Campaign and issues

A Roy Morgan poll in January 2017 found 26% of respondents named housing supply and affordability the most important issue and 17%
poverty and inequality.

- **Housing.** House prices nationally rose 34.2% between June 2014 and June 2017 (45.5% in Auckland). Labour's flagship was
  **KiwiBuild** (100,000 homes in 10 years), plus extending the bright-line test from two to five years, ending negative gearing and
  tenancy changes (90-day notice, no termination without cause, rent rises at most yearly). The Greens wanted a **capital gains tax**
  (excluding the family home) in the first term; ACT wanted to scrap Auckland's urban growth boundary.
- **Tax.** Labour would reverse the 2017 Budget tax cuts and put the money into Working for Families and a new payment for families
  with children under three (the Best Start policy), plus a winter energy payment. Nine days before the election, finance spokesman
  Grant Robertson promised **"no new taxes or levies" until after the 2020 election** – any capital gains tax from a tax working group
  would not take effect until 2021.
- **Immigration.** Net migration was 72,300 in the year to June 2017. Labour promised to cut it by 20–30,000 a year (mainly by
  tightening "low-value" student visas); NZ First wanted it cut to about 10,000; National tightened skilled-migrant pay thresholds.
- **Education.** Labour promised three years' **fees-free tertiary education** (first year from 2018) and a $50/week rise in student
  allowances; National pledged a $379 million education package; ACT offered bulk-funding with large teacher pay rises.
- **Poverty.** At the 4 September debate English committed to lifting 100,000 children out of poverty; Ardern committed to legislating
  child-poverty measurement in every Budget.
- **Infrastructure/transport.** National promised ten new "Roads of National Significance" ($10.5 billion) and $267 million for
  Auckland and Wellington commuter rail; Labour promised Auckland light rail (CBD–airport within a decade) and Auckland–Hamilton–Tauranga
  rail; the Greens proposed Wellington light rail.
- **NZ First** campaigned on regional development, cutting immigration, keeping super at 65, moving the Ports of Auckland to Northport,
  and referendums on abolishing the Māori seats and reducing Parliament to 100 MPs.
- **Water and environment.** The Greens proposed a 10c/litre levy on bottled water and a $2/kg nitrate (fertiliser) charge on farmers.

### Reading the result

- **Preliminary vs final.** Election night gave National 58 seats, Labour 45, NZ First 9, Greens 7, ACT 1. Special votes moved one
  seat each from National to Labour and the Greens, making NZ First the decisive "kingmaker": National+NZ First = 65, or
  Labour+NZ First+Greens = 63.
- **No overhang** (120 seats) – the first 120-seat House since 2002, because neither United Future nor the Māori Party won an electorate.
- **Minor-party squeeze.** Combined National+Labour vote was 81.34%, the highest of the six elections in this archive. The Greens
  (6.27%) halved; TOP's 2.44% and the Māori Party's 1.18% were "wasted" – 4.68% of valid party votes in total.
- **Māori electorates.** Labour won all seven – Tāmati Coffey beat Māori Party co-leader Te Ururoa Flavell in Waiariki – the first
  clean sweep since 2002, leaving the Māori Party out of Parliament.

## Electorates

71 electorates (64 general, 7 Māori). Notable:

- **Christchurch Central** – Labour's Duncan Webb took the seat back from National's Nicky Wagner.
- **Hutt South** – National list MP Chris Bishop beat Labour's Trevor Mallard, the first time the seat had changed hands since 1996
  (helped by boundary changes moving Naenae out).
- **Ōhāriu** – after Peter Dunne retired, Labour's Greg O'Connor won (majority 1,051).
- **Northland** – National's Matt King won back the seat from Winston Peters, who had won it at the 2015 by-election.
- **Epsom** – David Seymour (ACT) held on with National's tacit support; ACT's 0.50% party vote gave it no extra seats.
- **Hamilton West** was described as the country's bellwether.

## Government formation

Both National and Labour negotiated with NZ First, whose board and caucus met on 16 October. On **19 October** Winston Peters announced
a coalition with Labour; the Greens agreed confidence and supply the same day. NZ First received four Cabinet posts (Peters as Deputy
PM and Foreign Minister, plus Ron Mark, Tracey Martin, Shane Jones) and one under-secretary (Fletcher Tabuteau); the Greens three
ministers outside Cabinet (James Shaw, Julie Anne Genter, Eugenie Sage) and one under-secretary (Jan Logie). English had ruled out a
formal arrangement with ACT. The Sixth Labour Government was sworn in on **26 October 2017**.

## The 52nd Parliament (2017–2020)

Bill English and Steven Joyce resigned in 2018 (replaced by Maureen Pugh and Nicola Willis); the **2018 Northcote by-election** was
held by National's Dan Bidois; **Jami-Lee Ross** left National in October 2018 after a public dispute with leader Simon Bridges and sat
as an independent (later co-leading Advance NZ); National's Andrew Falloon resigned in July 2020, too close to the election for a
by-election. Notable legislation included the Zero Carbon Act, the End of Life Choice Act (put to a binding referendum in 2020), the
cannabis referendum commitment (from the Green agreement) and the post-Christchurch-mosque-attacks firearms ban (2019). The election
originally set for 19 September 2020 was delayed to 17 October because of COVID-19.

## What changed

1. The "largest party forms government" convention was broken – under MMP the bloc that can command 61 votes governs.
2. Parliament consolidated to five parties; electorate-deal partners (United Future, the Māori Party) disappeared.
3. NZ First's kingmaker role (as in 1996 and 2005) peaked; in 2020 it lost all its seats.
